\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:derive_lens_eq}: Derivation of the Lens Equation}

\textbf{(a)} From the lensing geometry (Figure~\ref{fig:lensing_geometry}),
consider a light ray that passes through the lens plane at physical position
$\vecxi$ and is deflected by the angle $\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}$.  In the
small-angle approximation, the physical position in the source plane is
determined by two contributions: (i) the extrapolation of the undeflected
ray from the lens plane to the source plane, which gives a position
$(\Ds/\Dd)\,\vecxi$, and (ii) the deflection, which displaces the ray by
$-\Dds\,\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}(\vecxi)$ in the source plane.  Therefore:
\begin{equation*}
    \veceta = \frac{\Ds}{\Dd}\,\vecxi
    - \Dds\,\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}(\vecxi) \,.
\end{equation*}
\begin{derivation}[Part (a) without the small-angle shortcut]
In the plane containing the observer O, the lens L and the ray, the exact
flat-space relation between the transverse positions is
$\Ds\tan\beta = \Ds\tan\theta - \Dds\tan\alphahat$ (Congdon \& Keeton eq.~2.15).
Here $\Ds\tan\theta$ is where the straight line from O at angle $\theta$
meets the source plane, $\Dds\tan\alphahat$ is how far the bent ray has
moved away from that straight line after travelling $\Dds$ beyond the
kink, and $\Ds\tan\beta$ is where the source actually is.
For lensing angles $\lesssim 10^{-4}$~rad, $\tan x = x(1 + x^2/3 + \dots)$ differs
from $x$ by less than $10^{-8}$. With $\tan \to$ angle, and $\Ds\theta =
(\Ds/\Dd)\xi$, this gives the displayed result. In two dimensions the same
argument applies to each component, and the vector $\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}$
(pointing away from the mass) is subtracted because the ray is bent
\emph{toward} the mass.
\end{derivation}

\textbf{(b)} Substituting $\vecxi = \Dd\,\vectheta$ and
$\veceta = \Ds\,\vecbeta$:
\begin{equation*}
    \Ds\,\vecbeta = \frac{\Ds}{\Dd}\,\Dd\,\vectheta
    - \Dds\,\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}(\Dd\,\vectheta)
    = \Ds\,\vectheta - \Dds\,\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}(\Dd\,\vectheta) \,.
\end{equation*}
Dividing by $\Ds$:
\begin{equation*}
    \vecbeta = \vectheta
    - \frac{\Dds}{\Ds}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}(\Dd\,\vectheta) \,.
\end{equation*}

\textbf{(c)} Defining the reduced deflection angle
$\vecalpha(\vectheta) \equiv (\Dds/\Ds)\,
\boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}(\Dd\,\vectheta)$, the lens equation becomes:
\begin{equation*}
    \vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta) \,. \qquad \square
\end{equation*}
\begin{annotation}[What went into the lens equation]
Four assumptions are hidden in the derivation, and each is relaxed somewhere
later in the book. They are: (1) a single thin screen (relaxed by multi-plane lensing,
Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}); (2) small angles; (3) distances that
satisfy ``separation $=$ angle $\times$ distance'', i.e.\ angular-diameter
distances, with $\Dds \neq \Ds - \Dd$; and (4) weak fields, so that
deflections superpose linearly. A quick self-check of any rearrangement is
dimensions: every term of $\veceta = \dots$ is a length, and every term of
$\vecbeta = \dots$ is an angle.
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:point_mass_solve}: Point Mass Lens Equation}

\textbf{(a)} For a point mass $M$ at the origin, the physical deflection
angle is $\alphahat = 4\Gnewton M / (\speedoflight^2 \xi)$ where
$\xi = \Dd\,\theta$.  The reduced deflection angle is:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha(\theta) = \frac{\Dds}{\Ds}\,
    \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2\,\Dd\,\theta}
    = \frac{1}{\theta}\,
    \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2}\,\frac{\Dds}{\Dd\,\Ds}
    = \frac{\thetaE^2}{\theta} \,.
\end{equation*}
The lens equation is:
$\beta = \theta - \alpha(\theta) = \theta - \thetaE^2/\theta$.

\textbf{(b)} Multiplying by $\theta$:
$\theta^2 - \beta\,\theta - \thetaE^2 = 0$.
By the quadratic formula:
\begin{equation*}
    \theta_\pm = \frac{\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\,\thetaE^2}}{2} \,.
\end{equation*}

\textbf{(c)} The discriminant is
$\Delta = \beta^2 + 4\,\thetaE^2 > 0$ for all $\beta$ (since $\thetaE > 0$).
Therefore two real solutions always exist.

\textbf{(d)} Vieta's formulas for $\theta^2 - \beta\,\theta - \thetaE^2 = 0$
give the sum of the roots, $\theta_+ + \theta_- = \beta$, and their
product, $\theta_+\,\theta_- = -\thetaE^2$. \checkmark

\textbf{(e)} For $\beta > 0$: since $\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2} > \beta$,
we have $\theta_+ > 0$.  Moreover,
$\theta_+ = (\beta + \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2})/2 > \sqrt{\thetaE^2} = \thetaE$
because $\beta + \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2} > 2\thetaE$ (square both
sides to verify).

For $\theta_-$: $\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2} > \beta$ implies
$\theta_- = (\beta - \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2})/2 < 0$.
Also $|\theta_-| = (\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2} - \beta)/2 < \thetaE$,
which can be verified by noting $\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2} - \beta < 2\thetaE$.
\hfill $\square$
\begin{derivation}[The two ``square both sides'' steps of part (e)]
\emph{Claim 1: $\theta_+ > \thetaE$}, i.e.\
$\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2} > 2\thetaE - \beta$.
If $2\thetaE - \beta \le 0$ the claim is immediate, since a positive square root
exceeds a non-positive number. If $2\thetaE - \beta > 0$, both sides are
positive and squaring preserves the inequality:
$\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2 > 4\thetaE^2 - 4\beta\thetaE + \beta^2
\iff 0 > -4\beta\thetaE$, which is true for $\beta > 0$.

\emph{Claim 2: $|\theta_-| < \thetaE$}, i.e.\
$\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2} < 2\thetaE + \beta$. Both sides are positive, so
square: $\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2 < 4\thetaE^2 + 4\beta\thetaE + \beta^2
\iff 0 < 4\beta\thetaE$, again true for $\beta > 0$.

\emph{Alternative route (Vieta).} $\theta_+\theta_- = -\thetaE^2 < 0$ forces
opposite signs. $\theta_+ + \theta_- = \beta > 0$ makes the positive root the
larger in magnitude. Two unequal magnitudes whose product is $\thetaE^2$
must lie on either side of $\thetaE$. Both routes are checked in
\texttt{problems\_04.wl}.
\end{derivation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:einstein_radius_compute}: Einstein Radius for Various Systems}

Using $\thetaE = \sqrt{(4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2)\,(\Dds/\Dd\Ds)}$
with $\Gnewton = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$~m$^3$\,kg$^{-1}$\,s$^{-2}$,
$\speedoflight = 2.998 \times 10^{8}$~m\,s$^{-1}$:

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(a)} Stellar microlensing:
$M = 1.989 \times 10^{30}$~kg, $\Dd = \Dds = 5$~kpc $= 1.543 \times 10^{20}$~m,
$\Ds = 10$~kpc $= 3.086 \times 10^{20}$~m.
\begin{align*}
    \thetaE &= \sqrt{\frac{4 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times
    1.989 \times 10^{30}}{(2.998 \times 10^8)^2} \times
    \frac{1.543 \times 10^{20}}{1.543 \times 10^{20} \times
    3.086 \times 10^{20}}} \\
    &= \sqrt{5.906 \times 10^{3} \times
    \frac{1}{3.086 \times 10^{20}}} \\
    &= \sqrt{1.914 \times 10^{-17}} \\
    &\approx 4.38 \times 10^{-9}~\text{rad}
    \approx 0.90~\text{mas} \,.
\end{align*}
Physical Einstein radius: $\RE = 5 \times 3.086 \times 10^{19} \times
4.38 \times 10^{-9} \approx 6.8 \times 10^{11}$~m
$\approx 2.2 \times 10^{-11}$~Mpc $\approx 2.2 \times 10^{-5}$~pc $\approx 4.5$~AU.
(Earlier printings wrote $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2$ as $5.906\times10^{-10}$~m
here; the correct value is $5.906\times10^{3}$~m, twice the Sun's Schwarzschild
radius, and the result now agrees with eq.~\eqref{eq:thetaE_microlensing}.)

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(b)} Galaxy lensing:
$M = 10^{12} \times 1.989 \times 10^{30}$~kg, $\Dd = 919$~Mpc,
$\Ds = 1652$~Mpc, $\Dds = 1055$~Mpc.
Converting to meters ($1~\text{Mpc} = 3.086 \times 10^{22}$~m):
\begin{align*}
    \thetaE &= \sqrt{\frac{4 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times
    1.989 \times 10^{42}}{(2.998 \times 10^8)^2} \times
    \frac{1055}{919 \times 1652}~\text{Mpc}^{-1}} \\
    &\approx 1.15 \times 10^{-5}~\text{rad} \approx 2.4'' \,.
\end{align*}
Physical Einstein radius: $\RE = 919 \times 1.15 \times 10^{-5}~\text{Mpc}
\approx 1.06 \times 10^{-2}$~Mpc $\approx 10.6$~kpc.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{(c)} Cluster lensing:
$M_{\mathrm{E}} \sim 2 \times 10^{14} \times 1.989 \times 10^{30}$~kg
(mass within the Einstein radius for a typical massive cluster),
$\Dd = 919$~Mpc, $\Ds = 1727$~Mpc, $\Dds = 1328$~Mpc.
\begin{equation*}
    \thetaE \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-4}~\text{rad} \approx 37'' \,.
\end{equation*}
Physical Einstein radius: $\RE \approx 165$~kpc.
\begin{derivation}[Carrying every power of ten]
\emph{(a)} $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2 = 4\times6.674\times10^{-11}\times1.989\times10^{30}
/(2.998\times10^{8})^2 = 5.91\times10^{3}$~m. Since $\Dd = \Dds$,
$\Dds/(\Dd\Ds) = 1/\Ds = 1/(3.086\times10^{20}~\mathrm{m})$. Hence
$\thetaE^2 = 5.91\times10^{3}/3.086\times10^{20} = 1.91\times10^{-17}$,
$\thetaE = 4.38\times10^{-9}$~rad, and
$\thetaE = 4.38\times10^{-9}\times206265'' = 9.0\times10^{-4}{}'' = 0.90$~mas,
as in eq.~\eqref{eq:thetaE_microlensing}. Physical radius:
$\RE = 1.543\times10^{20}~\mathrm{m}\times4.38\times10^{-9} = 6.8\times10^{11}$~m
$= 4.5$~AU $= 2.2\times10^{-5}$~pc.

\emph{(b)} $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2 = 5.91\times10^{15}$~m.
$\Dds/(\Dd\Ds) = 1055/(919\times1652)~\mathrm{Mpc^{-1}} = 6.95\times10^{-4}~\mathrm{Mpc^{-1}}
= 2.25\times10^{-26}~\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Hence $\thetaE^2 = 1.33\times10^{-10}$,
$\thetaE = 1.15\times10^{-5}$~rad $= 2.4''$, and
$\RE = 919~\mathrm{Mpc}\times1.15\times10^{-5} = 10.6$~kpc.

\emph{(c)} With the mass inside the Einstein radius,
$M_{\mathrm E} = 2\times10^{14}\,\Msun$:
$\thetaE = 1.79\times10^{-4}$~rad $= 37''$ and $\RE = 165$~kpc.
The exercise statement gives this mass inside $\thetaE$; the \emph{total}
mass of such a cluster is $M_{200}\sim10^{15}\,\Msun$.
Inserted literally into the point-mass formula, that total would give
$\thetaE = \sqrt5\times37'' \approx 83''$ and $\RE \approx 0.37$~Mpc. That is
too large, because most of a cluster's mass lies outside $\thetaE$ and does not
contribute to the deflection there (2D shell theorem, Section~\ref{sec:surface_mass_density}).
\end{derivation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:sigma_cr_compute}: Critical Surface Mass Density}

\textbf{(a)} Dimensional analysis of $\Sigmacr = \speedoflight^2 /
(4\pi\Gnewton) \times \Ds / (\Dd\,\Dds)$:
\begin{equation*}
    [\Sigmacr] = \frac{(\text{m/s})^2}{(\text{m}^3\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-2})}
    \times \frac{\text{m}}{\text{m} \times \text{m}}
    = \frac{\text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2} \times \text{kg}\,\text{s}^{2}}
    {\text{m}^3} \times \frac{1}{\text{m}}
    = \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^2} \,.
\end{equation*}
So $\Sigmacr$ has units of mass per area, as required. \checkmark

\textbf{(b)} For the galaxy lens ($\Dd = 919$~Mpc, $\Ds = 1652$~Mpc,
$\Dds = 1055$~Mpc):
\begin{equation*}
    \Sigmacr = \frac{(2.998 \times 10^{8})^2}{4\pi \times
    6.674 \times 10^{-11}} \times
    \frac{1652 \times 3.086 \times 10^{22}}{919 \times 3.086 \times 10^{22}
    \times 1055 \times 3.086 \times 10^{22}}
    \approx 5.9~\text{kg\,m}^{-2} \,.
\end{equation*}
Converting: $5.9~\text{kg\,m}^{-2} = 0.59~\text{g\,cm}^{-2}
\approx 2830~\Msun\,\text{pc}^{-2}$ (2840 if $\speedoflight$ is rounded to
$3\times10^{8}$~m\,s$^{-1}$, as in eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma_cr_numerical}).

\textbf{(c)} For the cluster lens ($\Dd = 919$~Mpc, $\Ds = 1727$~Mpc,
$\Dds = 1328$~Mpc):
\begin{equation*}
    \Sigmacr \approx 4.9~\text{kg\,m}^{-2} = 0.49~\text{g\,cm}^{-2}
    \approx 2350~\Msun\,\text{pc}^{-2} \,.
\end{equation*}
\begin{derivation}[Unit conversions in (b) and (c)]
\emph{(b)} $\speedoflight^2/(4\pi\Gnewton) = (2.998\times10^{8})^2/(4\pi\times6.674\times10^{-11})
= 1.07\times10^{26}$~kg\,m$^{-1}$, and
$\Ds/(\Dd\Dds) = 1652/(919\times1055)~\mathrm{Mpc^{-1}} = 5.52\times10^{-26}~\mathrm{m^{-1}}$.
Their product is $5.92$~kg\,m$^{-2}$. Dividing by
$1~\Msun\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}} = 1.989\times10^{30}/(3.086\times10^{16})^2 = 2.09\times10^{-3}$~kg\,m$^{-2}$
gives $2.83\times10^{3}~\Msun\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$ (or $2.84\times10^{3}$ with
$\speedoflight$ rounded to $3\times10^{8}$, as in eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma_cr_numerical}).

\emph{(c)} Only $\Ds$ and $\Dds$ change, so the shortcut is a ratio:
$\Sigmacr(z_s{=}2)/\Sigmacr(z_s{=}1) = (1727/1328)/(1652/1055) = 0.83$, giving
$0.83\times2.83\times10^{3} \approx 2.35\times10^{3}~\Msun\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$ and
$4.9$~kg\,m$^{-2}$.
\end{derivation}

\textbf{(d)} A typical elliptical galaxy with
$\Sigma_0 \approx 5000~\Msun\,\text{pc}^{-2}$ has
$\kappab = \Sigma_0/\Sigmacr \approx 5000/2830 \approx 1.8 > 1$.
It is a strong lens: the central surface mass density exceeds
the critical value, so multiple images can form.
\begin{annotation}[Two caveats on part (d)]
(1) The verdict depends on the source. For a $z_s = 2$ source the same galaxy
has $\kappab_0 = 5000/2350 \approx 2.1$. More distant sources are
\emph{more} strongly lensed. (2) ``$\kappab > 1$ somewhere'' is sufficient for
multiple imaging, and for an axisymmetric lens also necessary (Congdon \& Keeton Sec.~2.5).
Whether multiple images are actually \emph{observed} depends on where the source sits
relative to the caustics (Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}).
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:image_positions_plot}: Image Positions for a Point Mass Lens}

\textbf{(a)} For $\thetaE = 1''$ and $\theta_\pm = (\beta \pm
\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4})/2$, with $|\mu_\pm| = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[\frac{u^2+2}{u\sqrt{u^2+4}}\pm 1\right]$
and $u = \beta/\thetaE$ (so that $|\mu_+|-|\mu_-|=1$ is exact for all
$u>0$):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rccccc}
\toprule
$\beta$ (arcsec) & $\theta_+$ (arcsec) & $\theta_-$ (arcsec)
& $\Delta\theta$ (arcsec) & $|\mu_+|$ & $|\mu_-|$ \\
\midrule
0.1  & $+1.051$ & $-0.951$ & 2.002 & 5.519 & 4.519 \\
0.5  & $+1.281$ & $-0.781$ & 2.062 & 1.591 & 0.591 \\
1.0  & $+1.618$ & $-0.618$ & 2.236 & 1.171 & 0.171 \\
2.0  & $+2.414$ & $-0.414$ & 2.828 & 1.030 & 0.030 \\
5.0  & $+5.193$ & $-0.193$ & 5.385 & 1.001 & 0.001 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{derivation}[One row in full: $\beta = 0.5''$]
$\sqrt{\beta^2 + 4} = \sqrt{4.25} = 2.0616$, so
$\theta_\pm = (0.5 \pm 2.0616)/2 = +1.2808''$ and $-0.7808''$, and
$\Delta\theta = 2.0616''$. With $u = 0.5$:
$(u^2+2)/(2u\sqrt{u^2+4}) = 2.25/(1\times2.0616) = 1.0914$, so
$|\mu_+| = 1.591$ and $|\mu_-| = 0.591$.
\end{derivation}
\begin{annotation}[Three checks to run on every row]
Each row must satisfy three exact identities: $\theta_+ + \theta_- = \beta$,
$\theta_+\theta_- = -\thetaE^2 = -1$, and $|\mu_+| - |\mu_-| = 1$. Together they
catch most transcription and rounding errors, because each tests a different
column.
\end{annotation}

\textbf{(b)--(d)} Plots are generated in
\mathlink{04_Lens_Equation/lens_equation.wl}{lens\_equation.wl}
and
\solnlink{04_Lens_Equation/problems\_04.wl}{problems\_04.wl}.
See Figures~\ref{fig:image_positions} and~\ref{fig:einstein_ring}.

The minimum image separation is $\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{min}} = 2\thetaE = 2''$,
occurring at $\beta = 0$ (Einstein ring).

The total magnification $|\mu_+| + |\mu_-|$ diverges as $\beta \to 0$
and approaches 1 (no magnification) as $\beta \to \infty$.  For
$\beta = \thetaE$, the total magnification is
$(1 + 2)/(1 \cdot \sqrt{5}) \approx 1.34$.
\begin{annotation}[Cross-check with the literature]
$3/\sqrt5 = 1.342$, split as $1.171 + 0.171$, is the value quoted by Narayan
\& Bartelmann (eq.~29). It is the standard ``source crosses the Einstein
radius'' threshold of microlensing surveys.
\end{annotation}
