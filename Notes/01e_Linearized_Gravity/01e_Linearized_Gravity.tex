\chapter{Linearized Gravity and the Weak-Field Metric}
\label{ch:linearized_gravity}

\begin{quote}
\textit{The weak-field limit described in this chapter is less restrictive, assuming
that the field is still weak but it can vary with time, and without any
restrictions on the motion of test particles.  This will allow us to discuss
phenomena that are absent or ambiguous in the Newtonian theory, such as
gravitational radiation (where the field varies with time) and the deflection
of light (which involves fast-moving particles).}
\hfill --- Sean Carroll, \textit{Spacetime and Geometry}, Sect.~7.1
\end{quote}

\noindent
In Chapters~\ref{ch:special_relativity}--\ref{ch:geodesics_orbits} we developed the full machinery of general relativity
and applied it to the exact Schwarzschild solution.  That framework is
necessary for black holes and strong-field orbits, but
most gravitational lensing (by galaxies, galaxy clusters,
and large-scale structure) operates in a regime where gravity is
weak: the Newtonian potential satisfies $|\Phi|/c^2 \ll 1$ and peculiar
velocities are small compared to $c$.  In this regime the metric can be
treated as a small perturbation of flat Minkowski spacetime, and the
resulting \emph{linearized gravity} formalism is both simpler and
more directly connected to the lens equation that we will develop in
Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}.

This chapter derives the weak-field metric from first principles, recovers
Newtonian gravity as a limiting case, computes the deflection of light
(reproducing the factor of two over the Newtonian prediction),
and introduces the effective refractive index interpretation on which
Fermat's principle for lensing rests.
The linearity of the perturbation equations also guarantees that
deflection angles from multiple masses superpose, which is the
foundation for treating extended mass distributions in later chapters.

\section{Introduction: Why Linearize?}
\label{sec:why_linearize}

Consider a photon passing the Sun at the solar limb.  The relevant
dimensionless quantity characterizing the strength of gravity is
(Carroll eq.~4.21):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{|\Phi|}{c^2} = \frac{GM}{rc^2} \bigg|_{r = R_\odot}
    \approx \frac{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.989 \times 10^{30}}
    {6.96 \times 10^{8} \times (3 \times 10^{8})^2}
    \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-6} \,.
    \label{eq:sun_potential}
\end{equation}
For a typical galaxy lens ($M \sim 10^{12}\,\Msun$, $r \sim 10$~kpc),
the ratio is $|\Phi|/c^2 \approx 5 \times 10^{-6}$.  Even for
galaxy clusters ($M \sim 10^{15}\,\Msun$, $r \sim 1$~Mpc), one finds
$|\Phi|/c^2 \sim 5 \times 10^{-5}$; values of $\sim 10^{-4}$ are reached
only in the inner few hundred kpc of a massive cluster.

In all of these astrophysical settings gravity is very
weak.  This justifies expanding the metric perturbatively around flat
spacetime and keeping only first-order terms, which is the linearized
gravity approximation.

\begin{annotation}[Units, and what $|\Phi|/c^2$ predicts]
The ratio is dimensionless, $[GM/(rc^2)] =
(\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}})(\mathrm{kg})/
(\mathrm{m}\cdot\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}) = 1$.  With
$c = 2.998\times10^8$~m\,s$^{-1}$ the three point-mass estimates are
$2.12\times10^{-6}$ (Sun at the limb), $4.8\times10^{-6}$
($10^{12}\,\Msun$ at 10~kpc) and $4.8\times10^{-5}$
($10^{15}\,\Msun$ at 1~Mpc).
It also sets the deflection directly: for a point mass,
$\alphahat = 4GM/(c^2b) = 4\,|\Phi(b)|/c^2$, four times the
potential at closest approach.  For the Sun, $4\times2.12\times10^{-6} =
8.49\times10^{-6}$~rad $= 1.75''$; the galaxy estimate gives
$\approx 4''$.  Arcsecond image splittings are therefore the
\emph{natural} scale of galaxy lensing.
\end{annotation}


\begin{remark}
The only astrophysical setting in which linearized gravity is
insufficient for lensing is near black holes and neutron stars,
where $|\Phi|/c^2$ approaches unity.  Strong lensing by black holes
(e.g., the photon ring observed by the Event Horizon Telescope) requires
the full Schwarzschild or Kerr solution developed in Chapters~\ref{ch:schwarzschild} and~\ref{ch:geodesics_orbits}.
\end{remark}

\section{The Weak-Field Expansion}
\label{sec:weak_field_expansion}

We write the full spacetime metric as a perturbation of the Minkowski
metric (Carroll eq.~7.1; Congdon \& Keeton Sec.~3.3.2):
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu} \,,
    \qquad |h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1
    }
    \label{eq:metric_perturbation}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, +1, +1, +1)$ is the flat
Minkowski metric and $h_{\mu\nu}$ is the \emph{metric perturbation}.
The condition $|h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1$ means that spacetime is ``almost
flat'': deviations from Minkowski geometry are small.

The inverse metric to first order in $h$ is (Carroll eq.~7.2):
\begin{equation}
    g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu} + \order{h^2} \,,
    \label{eq:inverse_metric_linearized}
\end{equation}
where indices on $h_{\mu\nu}$ are raised and lowered with $\eta$.

\begin{derivation}[Why the inverse has a minus sign]
We try $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu}$ with
$h^{\mu\nu} \equiv \eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}h_{\alpha\beta}$:
\begin{align*}
g_{\mu\lambda}\,g^{\lambda\nu}
&= (\eta_{\mu\lambda} + h_{\mu\lambda})(\eta^{\lambda\nu} - h^{\lambda\nu}) \\
&= \delta_\mu^{\ \nu} - \eta_{\mu\lambda}h^{\lambda\nu}
   + h_{\mu\lambda}\eta^{\lambda\nu} - h_{\mu\lambda}h^{\lambda\nu}
  && \text{(expand)} \\
&= \delta_\mu^{\ \nu} - h_\mu^{\ \nu} + h_\mu^{\ \nu} + \order{h^2}
  && \text{(lower/raise with } \eta\text{)} \\
&= \delta_\mu^{\ \nu} + \order{h^2} .
\end{align*}
A sign trap: $h^{00} = \eta^{00}\eta^{00}h_{00} = +h_{00}$, so
$g^{00} = -1 - h_{00}$.  A direct check for a diagonal metric gives
$g^{00} = 1/g_{00} = -1/(1 - h_{00}) = -1 - h_{00} + \order{h_{00}^2}$.
\end{derivation}


The Christoffel symbols to first order are (Carroll eq.~7.4):
\begin{equation}
    \christoffel{\alpha}{\mu}{\nu}
    = \half \eta^{\alpha\beta}\bigl(
    \partial_\mu h_{\nu\beta} + \partial_\nu h_{\mu\beta}
    - \partial_\beta h_{\mu\nu}\bigr) + \order{h^2} \,.
    \label{eq:christoffel_linearized}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[Linearising the Christoffel symbols]
We start from the exact
$\christoffel{\alpha}{\mu}{\nu} = \tfrac12 g^{\alpha\beta}
(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\beta} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\beta} - \partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu})$.
Because $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ is constant, every derivative of $g$ is a
derivative of $h$, i.e.\ already first order.  Then
\[
\christoffel{\alpha}{\mu}{\nu}
= \tfrac12\bigl(\eta^{\alpha\beta} - h^{\alpha\beta}\bigr)
  (\partial_\mu h_{\nu\beta} + \partial_\nu h_{\mu\beta} - \partial_\beta h_{\mu\nu}) ,
\]
and the $h^{\alpha\beta}\,\partial h$ piece is $\order{h^2}$.  Dropping it
leaves eq.~\eqref{eq:christoffel_linearized}.  Consequently, in flat
coordinates all Christoffel symbols vanish at zeroth order, so the
connection is ``made of'' gradients of the potential.
\end{derivation}


\begin{remark}
In linearized gravity we systematically discard all terms of order
$h^2$ and higher.  The Christoffel symbols are then first order
in $h$, the Riemann tensor is first order, and the Einstein equations
become \emph{linear} in $h_{\mu\nu}$.  Linearity is the
simplification that allows superposition of gravitational effects.
\end{remark}

\section{The Newtonian Limit}
\label{sec:newtonian_limit}

We now show that the geodesic equation in the weak-field, slow-motion
limit reduces to Newton's second law, thereby identifying the metric
perturbation $h_{00}$ with the Newtonian potential $\Phi$.

\subsection{Assumptions}

The \emph{Newtonian limit} requires three conditions
(Carroll Sec.~4.1):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a weak field, $|h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1$;
    \item slow motion, $v \ll c$, so that
          $dx^i/d\tau \ll dx^0/d\tau = c\,dt/d\tau$;
    \item a static field, $\partial_0 h_{\mu\nu} = 0$
          (the gravitational field is not changing with time).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Derivation}

The geodesic equation for a massive particle is (Carroll eq.~3.44)
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2 x^\mu}{d\tau^2}
    + \christoffel{\mu}{\alpha}{\beta}\,
    \frac{dx^\alpha}{d\tau}\, \frac{dx^\beta}{d\tau} = 0 \,.
    \label{eq:geodesic_full}
\end{equation}
In the slow-motion limit, $dx^i/d\tau$ is negligible compared to
$dx^0/d\tau = c\,dt/d\tau$, so the double sum over $\alpha, \beta$
is dominated by the $\alpha = \beta = 0$ term:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2 x^\mu}{d\tau^2}
    + \christoffel{\mu}{0}{0}\left(\frac{dx^0}{d\tau}\right)^{\!2}
    \approx 0 \,.
    \label{eq:geodesic_slow}
\end{equation}

From eq.~\eqref{eq:christoffel_linearized}, using the static-field
condition ($\partial_0 h_{\mu\nu} = 0$):
\begin{equation}
    \christoffel{\mu}{0}{0}
    = \half \eta^{\mu\alpha}\bigl(
    \partial_0 h_{0\alpha} + \partial_0 h_{0\alpha}
    - \partial_\alpha h_{00}\bigr)
    = -\half \eta^{\mu\alpha}\, \partial_\alpha h_{00} \,.
    \label{eq:gamma_00}
\end{equation}
For the spatial components ($\mu = i$), this gives
$\christoffel{i}{0}{0} = -\half \partial_i h_{00}$.

\begin{derivation}[All first-order Christoffel symbols of the static weak field]
For the static, diagonal perturbation of eq.~\eqref{eq:h_components}
($h_{00} = h_{xx} = h_{yy} = h_{zz} = -2\Phi/c^2$, $h_{0i} = 0$, $x^0 = ct$),
eq.~\eqref{eq:christoffel_linearized} gives:
\begin{align*}
\christoffel{0}{0}{0} &= 0 , &
\christoffel{0}{0}{i} &= \christoffel{i}{0}{0} = \frac{\partial_i\Phi}{c^2}
  = -\tfrac12\,\partial_i h_{00} , \\
\christoffel{i}{0}{j} &= 0 , &
\christoffel{i}{j}{k} &= \frac{1}{c^2}\bigl(\delta_{jk}\,\partial_i\Phi
  - \delta_{ij}\,\partial_k\Phi - \delta_{ik}\,\partial_j\Phi\bigr) .
\end{align*}
The sizes of the terms in the geodesic equation for a slow particle ($dx^0/d\tau \approx c$,
$dx^j/d\tau \approx v^j$) are as follows: the $\christoffel{i}{0}{0}$ term is
$\sim|\nabla\Phi|$; the $\christoffel{i}{0}{j}$ term is zero; the
$\christoffel{i}{j}{k}$ term is $\sim|\nabla\Phi|\,v^2/c^2$.  Dropping the
latter is the slow-motion approximation.  For light ($v = c$) it is
\emph{not} negligible, which is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:null_geodesics_weak}.
\end{derivation}


The spatial part of the geodesic equation then becomes
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2 x^i}{d\tau^2}
    = \half \partial_i h_{00}\left(\frac{dx^0}{d\tau}\right)^{\!2}
    = \half c^2 \partial_i h_{00}\left(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\right)^{\!2} .
    \label{eq:geodesic_spatial}
\end{equation}
Using $d\tau \approx dt$ (valid for slow motion, since $\gamma \approx 1$),
we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2 x^i}{dt^2} = \half c^2\, \partial_i h_{00} \,.
    \label{eq:newton_from_geodesic}
\end{equation}

\begin{annotation}[$d\tau \approx dt$ is not needed]
The $0$-component of the geodesic equation is
$d^2x^0/d\tau^2 = -2\christoffel{0}{0}{i}\,(dx^0/d\tau)(dx^i/d\tau)$, which is
suppressed by $v/c$ relative to the spatial equation, so $dt/d\tau$ is
constant to the order kept.  Then
$d^2x^i/d\tau^2 = (dt/d\tau)^2\,d^2x^i/dt^2 + (dx^i/dt)\,d^2t/d\tau^2$, the
last term is higher order, and the factor $(dt/d\tau)^2$ cancels
\emph{exactly} between the two sides of eq.~\eqref{eq:geodesic_spatial}.
\end{annotation}


\subsection{Identification with Newtonian Gravity}

Newton's second law in a gravitational field is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2 x^i}{dt^2} = -\partial_i \Phi \,.
    \label{eq:newton_law}
\end{equation}
Comparing with eq.~\eqref{eq:newton_from_geodesic}, we identify
(Carroll eq.~4.19):
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    h_{00} = -\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}
    }
    \label{eq:h00_identification}
\end{equation}
The time-time component of the metric perturbation is determined by the
Newtonian potential.  For a point mass $M$, $\Phi = -GM/r$, so
\begin{equation}
    h_{00} = \frac{2GM}{c^2 r} = \frac{R_S}{r} \,,
    \label{eq:h00_point_mass}
\end{equation}
where $R_S = 2GM/c^2$ is the Schwarzschild radius.

\begin{annotation}[Sign conventions to keep straight]
The signature is $(-,+,+,+)$ and $x^0 = ct$, so $h_{\mu\nu}$ and
$\Phi/c^2$ are dimensionless; $\eta_{00} = -1$, hence
$h_{00} = g_{00} + 1$.
Since $\Phi < 0$ near a mass, $h_{00} = -2\Phi/c^2 = R_S/r > 0$ and
$g_{00} = -(1 - R_S/r)$, which is Schwarzschild's $g_{tt}/c^2$.
With the opposite signature $(+,-,-,-)$ one has
$g_{00} = 1 + 2\Phi/c^2$ and $h_{00} = +2\Phi/c^2$, and copying
formulas between books without converting is the usual
sign error.
The identification fixes $h_{00}$ only up to a constant,
$\partial_i(h_{00} + 2\Phi/c^2) = 0$; the constant is zero because
both $h_{00}$ and $\Phi$ vanish at infinity (asymptotic
flatness).
\end{annotation}

\label{ex:newtonian_limit}

slow-moving particles in a static, weak gravitational field:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item The only relevant Christoffel symbol is
          $\christoffel{i}{0}{0} = -\half \partial_i h_{00}$.
    \item The geodesic equation reduces to
          $d^2 x^i / dt^2 = \half c^2\, \partial_i h_{00}$.
    \item Comparing with Newton's law identifies
          $h_{00} = -2\Phi/c^2$.
\end{enumerate}

\end{exercise}

\section{The Weak-Field Metric}
\label{sec:weak_field_metric}

The Newtonian limit only determined $h_{00}$.  To obtain the full
weak-field metric, including the spatial components needed for
light bending, we must go beyond the slow-motion assumption and
use the linearized Einstein equations.

For a static, spherically symmetric source, the solution to the
linearized Einstein equations in isotropic coordinates gives
(Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.71; Carroll eq.~7.59):
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    ds^2 = -\left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) c^2\, dt^2
    + \left(1 - \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right)
    \bigl(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\bigr)
    }
    \label{eq:weak_field_metric}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ is the Newtonian gravitational potential
(with $\Phi < 0$ for attractive gravity).  Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
    h_{00} = -\frac{2\Phi}{c^2} \,, \qquad
    h_{ij} = -\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\,\delta_{ij} \,.
    \label{eq:h_components}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[Where $h_{ij}$ comes from: the linearised field equation]
We define the trace-reversed perturbation
$\bar h_{\mu\nu} \equiv h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h$ with
$h = \eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}$ (so $\bar h = -h$).  In Lorenz gauge,
$\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the linearised Einstein equation reads
$\Box\,\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -(16\pi G/c^4)\,T_{\mu\nu}$.
For a slow, pressureless, static source ($T_{00} = \rho c^2$,
$T_{0i} \approx T_{ij} \approx 0$, $\Box \to \nabla^2$), with all
perturbations vanishing at infinity and $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$:
\begin{align*}
\nabla^2\bar h_{00} &= -\frac{16\pi G\rho}{c^2}
  \;\Rightarrow\; \bar h_{00} = -\frac{4\Phi}{c^2},
  \qquad \bar h_{0i} = \bar h_{ij} = 0 , \\
\bar h &= \eta^{00}\bar h_{00} = +\frac{4\Phi}{c^2}
  && \text{(trace)} \\
h_{\mu\nu} &= \bar h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h
  && \text{(undo the reversal)} \\
h_{00} &= -\frac{4\Phi}{c^2} + \frac12\cdot\frac{4\Phi}{c^2} = -\frac{2\Phi}{c^2},
\qquad
h_{ij} = -\frac12\,\delta_{ij}\,\frac{4\Phi}{c^2} = -\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\,\delta_{ij} .
\end{align*}
A gauge-free check (done in \texttt{problems\_01e.wl}) is to insert the metric
eq.~\eqref{eq:weak_field_metric} into the exact Einstein tensor and keep
first order.  One finds $G_{00} = 2\nabla^2\Phi/c^2$, $G_{0i} = 0$ and
$G_{ij} = 0$.  With $G_{00} = 8\pi G T_{00}/c^4 = 8\pi G\rho/c^2$ this is
Poisson's equation, and $G_{ij} = 0$ matches the stress-free source.  Had
we tried $g_{ij} = (1 - 2\gamma\Phi/c^2)\delta_{ij}$, the off-diagonal
components would be $G_{ij} = (\gamma - 1)\,\partial_i\partial_j\Phi/c^2$,
nonzero unless $\gamma = 1$.  The equal perturbations are forced by
the absence of stresses in the source.
\end{derivation}


\begin{remark}
Both the time and space components of
the metric are perturbed, and by equal amounts.  The Newtonian limit
(which assumes slow motion) only probes $h_{00}$, because the spatial
components of the four-velocity are negligible.  Photons, however, travel at
$c$, so all components of the metric contribute equally to the
propagation of light.  This is why light bending in GR is
\emph{twice} the Newtonian prediction.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:schwarzschild_weak_field}
The Schwarzschild metric in standard coordinates is:
\[
    ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{R_S}{r}\right) c^2 dt^2
    + \left(1 - \frac{R_S}{r}\right)^{-1} dr^2
    + r^2\, d\Omega^2 \,.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Expand $(1 - R_S/r)^{-1} \approx 1 + R_S/r + \order{(R_S/r)^2}$
          and show that the metric becomes:
          \[
              ds^2 \approx -\left(1 - \frac{R_S}{r}\right) c^2 dt^2
              + \left(1 + \frac{R_S}{r}\right) dr^2
              + r^2\, d\Omega^2 \,.
          \]
    \item Transform to Cartesian-like coordinates and show that this
          matches the weak-field metric~\eqref{eq:weak_field_metric}
          with $\Phi = -GM/r$ to first order in $R_S/r$.
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\mathlink{01e_Linearized_Gravity/weak_field_metric.wl}{weak\_field\_metric.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{annotation}[A trap in part (b)]
$(1 + R_S/r)\,dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$ and $(1 + R_S/r)(dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2)$
differ by $R_S\,r\,d\Omega^2$, which is \emph{first} order in $R_S/r$
relative to $r^2d\Omega^2$.  Relabelling $r$ as the Cartesian radius
therefore does not work.  The radial coordinate must change:
$r = \rho(1 + R_S/4\rho)^2$ (eq.~\eqref{eq:isotropic_r}), so
$r \simeq \rho + R_S/2$.  The worked version is in the derivation box of
the solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:weak_field_schwarzschild}.
\end{annotation}

\section{Null Geodesics in the Weak-Field Metric}
\label{sec:null_geodesics_weak}

We now derive the deflection of light in the weak-field metric.  This
is the central result of this chapter and the starting point for the
entire gravitational lensing formalism.

\subsection{Setup}

Consider a photon propagating through the weak-field
metric~\eqref{eq:weak_field_metric} generated by a point mass $M$ at
the origin, with $\Phi = -GM/r$.  The photon travels along a null
geodesic: $ds^2 = 0$.

For a photon moving primarily in the $x$-direction, passing the mass
at impact parameter $b$ (the closest approach distance in the absence
of deflection), we can use the \emph{Born approximation}: we compute
the deflection along the unperturbed (straight-line) path.  This is
valid when the deflection angle is small, which is guaranteed by the
weak-field condition.

\subsection{The Null Condition}

Setting $ds^2 = 0$ in eq.~\eqref{eq:weak_field_metric} gives
\begin{equation}
    \left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) c^2 dt^2
    = \left(1 - \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right)
    \bigl(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\bigr) \,.
    \label{eq:null_condition}
\end{equation}
The coordinate speed of light is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \frac{|\mathbf{dx}|}{dt}
    = c\,\sqrt{\frac{1 + 2\Phi/c^2}{1 - 2\Phi/c^2}}
    \approx c\left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right)
    \label{eq:coordinate_speed}
\end{equation}
to first order in $\Phi/c^2$.  Since $\Phi < 0$, the coordinate speed
is less than $c$ near a mass: gravity slows light.

\begin{derivation}[Expanding the coordinate speed]
With $p \equiv \Phi/c^2$, $|p| \ll 1$:
$\sqrt{1 + 2p} = 1 + p + \order{p^2}$ and
$1/\sqrt{1 - 2p} = 1 + p + \order{p^2}$, so
\[
\sqrt{\frac{1 + 2p}{1 - 2p}} = (1 + p)(1 + p) + \order{p^2}
= 1 + 2p + \order{p^2} .
\]
One $p$ comes from $g_{00}$ and one from $g_{ij}$, the same ``half and
half'' that doubles the deflection.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Coordinate speed, not measured speed]
A static observer at the photon's location measures exactly $c$: her
proper time is $d\tau = \sqrt{1 + 2\Phi/c^2}\,dt$ and her proper length is
$d\ell = \sqrt{1 - 2\Phi/c^2}\,|d\mathbf{x}|$, so
$d\ell/d\tau = c$.  The slowdown in eq.~\eqref{eq:coordinate_speed} is
relative to the clocks and rulers of a distant observer.  It is
nevertheless observable, as the extra travel time (Shapiro delay)
accumulated along the whole path.
\end{annotation}


\subsection{Deflection Angle}

The deflection due to the temporal perturbation $h_{00}$ alone is
computed by integrating the transverse gradient of $|\Phi|$ along the
unperturbed (straight-line) path (Congdon \& Keeton Sec.~3.4.1):
\begin{equation}
    \alphahat_{\mathrm{time}} = \frac{1}{c^2} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
    \nabla_\perp |\Phi|\, dl \,,
    \label{eq:deflection_integral_half}
\end{equation}
where $\nabla_\perp$ denotes the gradient of $|\Phi|$ perpendicular to
the photon's path and $dl$ is the path length along the unperturbed
trajectory.  This gives the ``Newtonian'' contribution to the
deflection.

The spatial perturbation $h_{ij}$ contributes an \emph{equal} amount,
doubling the result.  In the PPN (parametrized post-Newtonian)
formalism, the full deflection angle is written as
(Will 1993)
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \alphahat = \frac{1 + \gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}}}{c^2}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \nabla_\perp |\Phi|\, dl
    }
    \label{eq:deflection_integral}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} = 1$ in GR\@.  The Parametrized
Post-Newtonian (PPN) formalism describes deviations from Newtonian
gravity using a set of dimensionless parameters; $\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}}$
measures how much spatial curvature is produced per unit rest
mass.  In GR, $\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} = 1$ (equal time and space
perturbations to the metric, as derived in
Section~\ref{sec:weak_field_metric}); in Brans--Dicke theory,
$\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} = (1+\omega)/(2+\omega)$ where $\omega$ is the
coupling constant.  Solar system tests, most precisely the Cassini
spacecraft's measurement of the Shapiro delay \citep{bertotti_2003}, give
$\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} - 1 = (2.1 \pm 2.3) \times 10^{-5}$, in
agreement with GR \citep{will_2014}.\footnote{Earlier versions of this chapter quoted
$|\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} - 1| < 2.3\times10^{-5}$, the number listed as the
$\gamma - 1$ limit in Table~4 of \citet{will_2014}.  The measurement itself is a
central value of $2.1\times10^{-5}$ with a $1\sigma$ uncertainty of
$2.3\times10^{-5}$, as quoted in the text of \citet{will_2014} and of
\citet{bertotti_2003}.}
The Newtonian prediction corresponds to $\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} = 0$
(no spatial curvature contribution), giving half the GR value.

\begin{derivation}[The two equal halves, straight from the geodesic equation]
We take $x^0 = ct$, a photon moving along $+x$, and the Born approximation:
along the unperturbed ray $k^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda = (1, 1, 0, 0)$, with
$\lambda = x$ at zeroth order.  The transverse ($y$) component of the
geodesic equation is
\[
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -\christoffel{y}{\alpha}{\beta}\,k^\alpha k^\beta
= -\bigl(\christoffel{y}{0}{0} + 2\,\christoffel{y}{0}{x} + \christoffel{y}{x}{x}\bigr) .
\]
\begin{align*}
\christoffel{y}{0}{0} &= -\tfrac12\,\partial_y h_{00} = \frac{\partial_y\Phi}{c^2}
  && \text{(from } g_{00}\text{: the ``time'' half)} \\
\christoffel{y}{0}{x} &= 0
  && (\text{static},\ h_{0i} = 0) \\
\christoffel{y}{x}{x} &= \tfrac12\bigl(2\partial_x h_{xy} - \partial_y h_{xx}\bigr)
  = \frac{\partial_y\Phi}{c^2}
  && (h_{xy} = 0;\ \text{from } g_{xx}\text{: the ``space'' half}) \\
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} &= -\frac{2}{c^2}\,\partial_y\Phi .
\end{align*}
Integrating along the straight line, the change of direction is
$\Delta(dy/dx) = -(2/c^2)\int\partial_y\Phi\,dx$.  For $\Phi = -GM/r$ and a ray
at $y = b > 0$, $\partial_y\Phi = GMb/(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2} > 0$: the ray turns
toward the mass by $(2/c^2)(2GM/b) = 4GM/(c^2b)$.  With
$g_{ij} = (1 - 2\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}}\Phi/c^2)\,\delta_{ij}$ the space half
becomes $\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}}\,\partial_y\Phi/c^2$, which is the
$1 + \gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}}$ of eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_integral}.  A slow
particle feels only $\christoffel{y}{0}{0}$; a photon has $|k^x| = |k^0|$
and feels both terms equally.
\end{derivation}


\subsection{Point Mass Deflection}

For a point mass with $|\Phi| = GM/r$, the photon travels along the
$x$-axis at perpendicular distance $b$ (so
$r = \sqrt{x^2 + b^2}$ and
$\nabla_\perp |\Phi| = (GM\, b)/(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2}$).
The line-of-sight integral evaluates to
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \nabla_\perp |\Phi|\, dl
    = GM \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
    \frac{b\, dx}{(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2}}
    = GM \cdot \frac{2}{b}
    = \frac{2GM}{b} \,,
    \label{eq:deflection_integral_eval}
\end{equation}
using the standard integral
$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} b\, dx/(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2} = 2/b$.
With $\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} = 1$ (GR), the deflection angle is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \alphahat = \frac{4GM}{c^2 b}
    }
    \label{eq:deflection_point_mass}
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{Einstein deflection angle} for a point mass,
the central equation of gravitational lensing.  We derived
it here via the Born approximation in the weak-field metric; in
Chapter~\ref{ch:light_deflection} we will re-derive it from the exact null
geodesic orbit equation in the full Schwarzschild metric, and the two
approaches agree.

\begin{derivation}[The ``standard integral'', and where the bending happens]
We substitute $x = b\tan\vartheta$, $dx = b\sec^2\vartheta\,d\vartheta$,
$x^2 + b^2 = b^2\sec^2\vartheta$:
\[
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{b\,dx}{(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2}}
= \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{b\cdot b\sec^2\vartheta}{b^3\sec^3\vartheta}\,d\vartheta
= \frac1b\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\vartheta\,d\vartheta = \frac2b .
\]
The same substitution on $|x| < X$ gives the fraction of the total
deflection accrued there, $X/\sqrt{X^2 + b^2}$: 71\% within $|x| < b$ and
99.5\% within $|x| < 10b$.  The bend is concentrated within a few impact
parameters of closest approach, a tiny path length compared with
cosmological distances.  This is the physical justification of the thin-lens
(single-plane) approximation.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Gradient of $|\Phi|$ versus gradient of $\Phi$]
The text uses $\nabla_\perp|\Phi|$ for the \emph{magnitude}
$GMb/(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2}$.  As vectors, $\nabla|\Phi|$ points toward the mass
($|\Phi|$ grows inward), the direction in which the ray bends, while
$\nabla\Phi$ points away from it.  The vector form used from
eq.~\eqref{eq:superposition_deflection} onward is
$\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha} = (2/c^2)\int\nabla_\perp\Phi\,dl$, which points
\emph{away} from the mass (along $\vecxi - \vecxi_i$); see the annotation in
Section~\ref{sec:superposition}.
\end{annotation}

\subsection{The Factor of Two}

The factor of 4 in eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_point_mass} (rather than 2)
is one of the best-known results of GR, and its origin is as follows.
The temporal perturbation $h_{00} = -2\Phi/c^2$ contributes a
deflection of $2GM/(c^2 b)$.  This is the ``Newtonian'' part,
which can also be derived by treating the photon as a
Newtonian particle moving at speed $c$.
The spatial perturbation $h_{ij} = -2\Phi/c^2\, \delta_{ij}$
contributes an \emph{equal} additional deflection of
$2GM/(c^2 b)$, because photons, unlike massive
particles, are equally sensitive to the spatial curvature.
The total is $4GM/(c^2 b) = 2 \times (2GM/(c^2 b))$, i.e., twice
the Newtonian prediction.  This factor of 2 was confirmed by
Eddington's 1919 solar eclipse expedition and is one of the classical
tests of general relativity.

\begin{annotation}[Where each factor of 2 in ``4'' comes from]
$4 = 2\times2$: one 2 is the line integral, $\int b\,dx/(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2} =
2/b$ (both halves of the path, incoming and outgoing); the other is
$1 + \gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} = 2$ (time plus space).  Two common errors:
quoting $2GM/(c^2b)$ (the Newtonian/1911 value), and doubling twice by
multiplying the full GR result by $1 + \gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}}$ again.  Unit
check: $GM/(c^2b)$ is dimensionless, so $\alphahat$ comes out in radians.
\end{annotation}


\begin{remark}
Historically, Einstein himself initially published the ``Newtonian''
value $2GM/(c^2 b)$ in 1911, before he had completed general
relativity.  The correct value $4GM/(c^2 b)$ appeared in 1915, after
Einstein accounted for the spatial curvature.  The Eddington expedition
confirmed the GR value, not the 1911 prediction.
\end{remark}

\section{The Effective Refractive Index}
\label{sec:refractive_index}

The weak-field metric leads to an analogy between gravitational
lensing and optics.  From eq.~\eqref{eq:coordinate_speed}, the
coordinate speed of light near a mass is
\begin{equation}
    v_{\mathrm{coord}} = c\left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) \,.
    \label{eq:coord_speed_light}
\end{equation}
We can define an \emph{effective refractive index} by analogy with
optics, where $v = c/n$:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    n(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{c}{v_{\mathrm{coord}}}
    \approx 1 - \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}
    = 1 + \frac{2|\Phi|}{c^2}
    }
    \label{eq:refractive_index}
\end{equation}
Since $\Phi < 0$ near a mass, $n > 1$: gravity acts as a medium with
refractive index greater than unity, slowing light (in
coordinate time) and bending it toward the mass.

\begin{derivation}[Ray optics in the medium $n$ reproduces the full $4GM/(c^2b)$]
Here $n = c/v_{\mathrm{coord}} = \sqrt{(1 - 2\Phi/c^2)/(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)}
= 1 - 2\Phi/c^2 + \order{\Phi^2/c^4}$, so that
$n - 1 = \tfrac12(h_{00} + h_{xx})$: the refractive index already contains
both halves.  For a point mass, $n - 1 = 2GM/(c^2r) = R_S/r$.  The ray
equation of geometrical optics,
$\frac{d}{dl}\bigl(n\,\hat{\mathbf t}\bigr) = \nabla n$ ($\hat{\mathbf t}$ the
unit tangent), gives to first order
$d\hat{\mathbf t}_\perp/dl = \nabla_\perp n$, hence
\[
|\Delta\hat{\mathbf t}_\perp| = \int|\nabla_\perp n|\,dl
= \frac{2}{c^2}\int\nabla_\perp|\Phi|\,dl = \frac{4GM}{c^2b} ,
\]
with the bend toward larger $n$, i.e.\ toward the mass.  No separate
``space part'' has to be added: the factor 2 is built into $n$.
\end{derivation}


\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{../Figures/01e_Linearized_Gravity/refractive_index.pdf}
    \caption{Effective refractive index $n = 1 + 2|\Phi|/c^2$ as a
    function of distance from a point mass, plotted for a solar-mass
    object.  The refractive index is largest (light is slowest) at the
    surface of the mass and approaches unity far away.}
    \label{fig:refractive_index}
\end{figure}

This refractive index interpretation has two consequences.

\paragraph{Fermat's Principle.}
In optics, light follows the path that extremizes the optical path
length $\int n\, dl$.  The same principle applies in gravitational
lensing: photons follow paths that extremize the
\emph{time delay functional} (Carroll eq.~7.85;
Congdon \& Keeton eq.~4.25):
\begin{equation}
    t[\mathbf{x}(l)] = \frac{1}{c} \int n\, dl
    = \frac{1}{c} \int \left(1 + \frac{2|\Phi|}{c^2}\right) dl \,.
    \label{eq:fermat_principle}
\end{equation}
This will be the foundation for the time delay formalism in Chapter~\ref{ch:fermat_time_delays}.

\paragraph{Shapiro Time Delay.}
Light passing near a mass arrives later than it would in flat spacetime,
by an amount proportional to $\int 2|\Phi|/c^2\, dl$ along the path.
This \emph{Shapiro delay} has been measured to high precision using
radar signals bouncing off Mercury and Venus.

\begin{derivation}[Shapiro delay along a straight path]
The excess over the flat-space travel time is
$\Delta t = c^{-1}\int(n - 1)\,dl = (2/c^3)\int|\Phi|\,dl$.  For a point mass
and a straight path from $x = -x_1$ to $x = x_2$ at impact parameter $b$:
\begin{align*}
\Delta t &= \frac{2GM}{c^3}\int_{-x_1}^{x_2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + b^2}}
= \frac{2GM}{c^3}\,
  \ln\frac{\bigl(x_2 + \sqrt{x_2^2 + b^2}\bigr)\bigl(x_1 + \sqrt{x_1^2 + b^2}\bigr)}{b^2} \\
&\simeq \frac{2GM}{c^3}\ln\frac{4x_1x_2}{b^2}
  \qquad (x_1, x_2 \gg b) .
\end{align*}
The dependence on $b$ is logarithmic, and the $b$-dependent part,
$-(4GM/c^3)\ln b$, is the piece that differs between images.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[From this functional to the Fermat potential]
In Chapter~\ref{ch:fermat_time_delays} the arrival time of an image is
$\propto\tau = \tfrac12|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2 - \psiL(\vectheta)$
(eq.~\eqref{eq:fermat_potential}).  The first term is the extra
\emph{geometric} path of the kinked ray; the second is the Shapiro term
$\int 2|\Phi|/c^2\,dl$ above, projected onto the lens plane and
rescaled by distances.  For a point mass $\psiL \propto \ln|\vectheta|$,
the same logarithm as here.
\end{annotation}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images}: the arrival-time surface of a compound lens]
Images sit at stationary points of
$\tau = \tfrac12|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2 - \psiL$, and the Shapiro term
$\psiL$ is a projection of the $2|\Phi|/c^2$ of this section.  For a point
mass, $\psiL \propto \ln|\vectheta|$, so $-\psiL \to +\infty$ at the mass:
a central black hole punctures the arrival-time surface exactly where a
smooth galaxy would have its central maximum (the odd image).  Whether that
image survives is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images}.
\end{bridge}

\section{Superposition in Linearized Gravity}
\label{sec:superposition}

Because the metric perturbation $h_{\mu\nu}$ satisfies \emph{linear}
equations in the weak-field limit, we can superpose solutions.  If we
have two masses $M_1$ and $M_2$ at positions $\mathbf{x}_1$ and
$\mathbf{x}_2$, the total Newtonian potential is
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{GM_1}{|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_1|}
    - \frac{GM_2}{|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_2|} \,,
    \label{eq:superposition_potential}
\end{equation}
and the total deflection angle is the vector sum of
the individual deflections:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}} = \sum_i \frac{4GM_i}{c^2}\,
    \frac{\vecxi - \vecxi_i}{|\vecxi - \vecxi_i|^2}
    }
    \label{eq:superposition_deflection}
\end{equation}
where $\vecxi$ is the position in the lens plane and $\vecxi_i$
is the projected position of mass $M_i$.

For a continuous mass distribution with surface mass density
$\Sigma(\vecxi)$, this becomes (Congdon \& Keeton eq.~4.9):
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}(\vecxi) = \frac{4G}{c^2}
    \int \frac{(\vecxi - \vecxi')\, \Sigma(\vecxi')}
    {|\vecxi - \vecxi'|^2}\, d^2\xi' \,.
    \label{eq:continuous_deflection}
\end{equation}
This integral is the starting point for all lens models in Chapters~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}--\ref{ch:cluster_lensing}.

\begin{derivation}[From the sum to the integral, and a second route via the projected potential]
The step sum $\to$ integral is made by chopping the lens plane into cells of area
$d^2\xi'$ carrying mass $\Sigma(\vecxi')\,d^2\xi'$ and applying
eq.~\eqref{eq:superposition_deflection} with $M_i \to \Sigma(\vecxi')\,d^2\xi'$,
$\vecxi_i \to \vecxi'$, $\sum_i \to \int d^2\xi'$.

The second route goes through the projected potential.  Since
$\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha} = (2/c^2)\int\nabla_\perp\Phi\,dz =
\nabla_\perp\hat\psi$ with $\hat\psi \equiv (2/c^2)\int\Phi\,dz$, and
$\int\partial_z^2\Phi\,dz = 0$ for an isolated lens,
\[
\nabla_\perp^2\hat\psi = \frac{2}{c^2}\int\nabla^2\Phi\,dz
= \frac{2}{c^2}\,4\pi G\Sigma = \frac{8\pi G}{c^2}\,\Sigma .
\]
The 2D Green's function is $\ln|\vecxi|/(2\pi)$
($\nabla^2\ln|\vecxi| = 0$ away from the origin, and the flux of
$\nabla\ln|\vecxi| = \vecxi/|\vecxi|^2$ through any circle is $2\pi$), so
\[
\hat\psi = \frac{4G}{c^2}\int\Sigma(\vecxi')\ln|\vecxi - \vecxi'|\,d^2\xi' ,
\qquad
\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha} = \nabla_\perp\hat\psi
= \frac{4G}{c^2}\int\Sigma(\vecxi')\,\frac{\vecxi - \vecxi'}{|\vecxi - \vecxi'|^2}\,d^2\xi' .
\]
For $\Sigma = M\,\delta^{(2)}(\vecxi')$ this returns
$|\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}| = 4GM/(c^2|\vecxi|)$.  Even though
$\int\Phi\,dz$ diverges logarithmically for a point mass, its transverse
derivative is finite: $\partial_b\int_{-Z}^{Z}(-GM/\sqrt{z^2 + b^2})\,dz \to 2GM/b$
as $Z \to \infty$.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Direction convention of the vector $\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$]
In eq.~\eqref{eq:superposition_deflection} each term points along
$\vecxi - \vecxi_i$, i.e.\ \emph{away} from the mass, although light is bent
\emph{toward} it.  The vector is defined as (incoming direction) minus
(outgoing direction), so that the lens equation reads
$\vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha$ with a minus sign
(Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}, eq.~\eqref{eq:lens_equation}): the source
lies closer to the lens centre than its image.  Flipping this convention
reflects all images through the lens centre, a common bug in lens-modelling
code.
\end{annotation}


\begin{remark}
Superposition of deflection angles does \emph{not} hold in the
exact (nonlinear) theory.  It is a consequence of linearization and is
valid only when all masses satisfy $|\Phi|/c^2 \ll 1$.  This
condition is satisfied for nearly all astrophysical lensing
systems.
\end{remark}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}: linear in each plane, nonlinear across planes]
Within one lens plane, deflections add because the field equations are
linear: this is eq.~\eqref{eq:superposition_deflection}.  In a compound
(multi-plane) lens the deflection of plane $j$ is evaluated at the position
$\vecxi_j$ where the ray crosses that plane, and $\vecxi_j$ depends on the
bends already suffered in planes $i < j$.  The multi-plane lens equation is
therefore a recursion: each step is linear in the mass of one plane, but the
composition is not.  A double-source-plane lens is the simplest case, with
the first source acting as a lens for the second.
\end{bridge}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass}: the point-mass building block]
The compound model of Part~V is a power-law galaxy plus a central point
mass in one plane.  By the linearity above, its deflection is \emph{exactly}
the sum of the two:
$\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha} = \hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}_{\mathrm{PL}}
+ (4GM_\bullet/c^2)\,\vecxi/|\vecxi|^2$, the second term being
eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_point_mass} in vector form.  Nonlinear
corrections (including any coupling between the black hole and the galaxy)
are suppressed by a further factor of order $|\Phi|/c^2 \sim 10^{-5}$
relative to the deflection itself, far below observational errors.
\end{bridge}

\section{Preview: From Deflection to the Lens Equation}
\label{sec:preview_lens_equation}

The deflection angle~\eqref{eq:deflection_point_mass} is a
\emph{physical} angle, measured in the frame of the lens.  To connect
it to observed image positions on the sky, we need the
\emph{lens equation}, which relates the true source position
$\vecbeta$ to the observed image position $\vectheta$ through the
deflection angle (Congdon \& Keeton eq.~4.26):
\begin{equation}
    \vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta) \,,
    \label{eq:lens_equation_preview}
\end{equation}
where $\vecalpha = (\Dds / \Ds)\, \boldsymbol{\hat{\alpha}}$ is the
\emph{reduced deflection angle} and $\Dd$, $\Ds$, $\Dds$ are the
angular diameter distances from observer to lens, observer to source,
and lens to source.\footnote{Earlier versions of this preview wrote
$\vecbeta = \vectheta - (\Dds/\Ds)\,\vecalpha$ with
$\vecalpha = (\Dd/\Ds)\,\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$, which combine to the
wrong factor $\Dd\Dds/\Ds^2$ in front of $\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$.  The
small-angle geometry gives $\Ds\vecbeta = \Ds\vectheta -
\Dds\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$, i.e.\ the form above, which is Congdon \&
Keeton eq.~4.26 and eqs.~\eqref{eq:reduced_deflection}
and~\eqref{eq:lens_equation} of Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}.}

The full derivation of this equation, along with the thin-lens
approximation and the definition of angular diameter distances, will be
developed in Chapters~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances} and~\ref{ch:lens_equation}.  For now, the
weak-field deflection angle computed in this chapter is the
input to the lens equation.

\begin{annotation}[Reading eq.~\eqref{eq:lens_equation_preview}: physical versus reduced deflection]
The preview is already the form used from Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}
onward, $\vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta)$ with
$\vecalpha \equiv (\Dds/\Ds)\,\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$
(eqs.~\eqref{eq:reduced_deflection} and~\eqref{eq:lens_equation}).  The
factor is $\Dds/\Ds$ because the deflection acts over the lens--source leg:
$\Ds\vecbeta = \Ds\vectheta - \Dds\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$.  The physical $\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$ (this
chapter) depends only on the lens; the reduced $\vecalpha$ also depends on
the source distance through $\Dds/\Ds$.  Also, $\Dds \neq \Ds - \Dd$ for
angular-diameter distances in an expanding universe
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}).
\end{annotation}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}: one physical deflection, two reduced deflections]
In a double-source-plane lens the same foreground galaxy, with one physical
deflection field $\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$, lenses two sources at different
redshifts.  The two reduced deflections differ only by the distance factors
$\Dds/\Ds$ of each source, so their ratio is a ratio of angular-diameter
distances that depends on cosmology and not on the lens mass.  This is why
the multi-plane chapter keeps $\hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$ (source-independent)
and applies the distance ratios plane by plane.
\end{bridge}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:linearized_summary}

In the weak-field limit, the metric is
$g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$ with
$|h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1$.
The Newtonian limit of the geodesic equation identifies
$h_{00} = -2\Phi/c^2$, where $\Phi$ is the Newtonian
gravitational potential, and the full weak-field metric has equal perturbations in
time and space,
$ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)\,c^2 dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi/c^2)\,
d\mathbf{x}^2$.
Null geodesics in this metric yield the Einstein deflection
angle $\alphahat = 4GM/(c^2 b)$, twice the
Newtonian value; the factor of 2 arises from the spatial
curvature contribution.
The weak-field metric defines an effective refractive index
$n = 1 + 2|\Phi|/c^2 > 1$, which connects gravitational lensing
to Fermat's principle and the Shapiro time delay.
Linearized gravity allows superposition: deflection
angles from multiple masses add vectorially, which is the
foundation for treating galaxies, clusters, and continuous
mass distributions as gravitational lenses.
The deflection angle is the input to the lens equation
(Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}), which maps source positions to image positions.

\section{Problems}
\label{sec:linearized_problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:newtonian_limit_prob}
\textbf{(Newtonian limit from the geodesic equation.)}
Carry out the full Newtonian limit derivation described in
Section~\ref{sec:newtonian_limit}.  Starting from the geodesic
equation with $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Compute the Christoffel symbol $\christoffel{i}{0}{0}$ to
          first order in $h$.
    \item Apply the slow-motion and static-field approximations.
    \item Show that $d^2 x^i/dt^2 = -\partial_i \Phi$ implies
          $h_{00} = -2\Phi/c^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:weak_field_schwarzschild}
\textbf{(Weak-field Schwarzschild.)}
Expand the Schwarzschild metric to first order in $R_S/r$ and verify
that it matches the weak-field metric~\eqref{eq:weak_field_metric}
with $\Phi = -GM/r$.  Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Expand $(1 - R_S/r)^{-1} \approx 1 + R_S/r$.
    \item Write the result in isotropic Cartesian coordinates.
    \item Identify $h_{00} = R_S/r$ and $h_{ij} = (R_S/r)\,\delta_{ij}$
          and confirm that the time and spatial diagonal perturbations
          are equal (i.e., $h_{00} = h_{xx} = h_{yy} = h_{zz}$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:refractive_index_prob}
\textbf{(Effective refractive index.)}
Compute the effective refractive index $n = 1 + 2|\Phi|/c^2$ for a
point mass $M = \Msun$ at distances:
(a) $r = R_\odot$,
(b) $r = 1$~AU,
(c) $r = 1$~kpc.
Comment on whether the weak-field approximation is valid in each case.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:deflection_angle_derivation}
\textbf{(Deflection angle from null geodesics.)}
Derive $\alphahat = 4GM/(c^2 b)$ by evaluating the
integral~\eqref{eq:deflection_integral} with $\gamma_{\mathrm{PPN}} = 1$
for a point mass along the unperturbed path.  Compute the deflection angle for:
(a) the Sun at the solar limb,
(b) a $10^{12}\,\Msun$ galaxy at $b = 10$~kpc.
Express results in arcseconds.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:superposition_prob}
\textbf{(Superposition of deflection angles.)}
Two equal point masses $M$ are separated by distance $d$ in the lens
plane.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Derive the total deflection angle for a photon passing at
          perpendicular distance $b$ from the midpoint (along the
          symmetry axis perpendicular to the line joining the masses).
    \item Show that for $b \gg d$, the result approaches
          $\alphahat \to 4G(2M)/(c^2 b)$: at large distances the two
          masses act as a single mass $2M$.
    \item Plot $\hat{\alpha}(b)$ for $d = 1$~kpc and
          $M = 10^{11}\,\Msun$ using Mathematica.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
