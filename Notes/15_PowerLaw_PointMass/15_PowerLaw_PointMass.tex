\chapter{Power-Law Lenses with a Central Point Mass}
\label{ch:power_law_point_mass}

\noindent
Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models} built the circular-lens formalism
and used it on the point mass, the singular and nonsingular isothermal
spheres, and the NFW halo.  Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing} then
introduced the spherical power law $\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$ as the
workhorse of galaxy-scale lens modeling.  This chapter puts those two
threads together and adds one more ingredient: a \textbf{central point
mass}, such as the supermassive black hole that sits at the centre of
essentially every massive galaxy.

The questions are concrete.  Where are the critical curves of a power
law, and when does it have a \emph{radial} critical curve at all?  What
does a point mass at the centre do to those curves?  The answer to the
second question is the main result of the chapter.  A point mass has no
radial critical curve of its own, and adding one to a profile that
\emph{does} have one pushes the radial eigenvalue upward precisely at
the small radii where radial images form, until, above a critical mass
fraction, the radial critical curve is destroyed.  We derive that
critical mass fraction in closed form.  We then solve the
isothermal-plus-point-mass lens exactly, and finally introduce the
\textbf{broken power law}, whose inner slope can mimic a central point
mass so well that, outside the break radius, the two are
\emph{identical}.

By the end of the chapter you should be able to compute the critical
curves and caustics of any axisymmetric profile plus a central point
mass, and to say which observations could and could not tell a central
point mass from a steeper inner density profile.

\section{The Circular Toolbox, Revisited}
\label{sec:pl_toolbox}

For an axisymmetric convergence $\kappab(\theta)$, with
$\theta=|\vectheta|$, the deflection points radially and depends only
on the mass enclosed within $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\theta) = \frac{2}{\theta}\int_0^{\theta}\kappab(\theta')\,\theta'\,\dd\theta'
                   = \bar{\kappa}(\theta)\,\theta ,
    \qquad
    \bar{\kappa}(\theta)\equiv\frac{2}{\theta^2}\int_0^{\theta}\kappab(\theta')\,\theta'\,\dd\theta' ,
    \label{eq:pl_alpha_kbar}
\end{equation}
where $\bar\kappa$ is the mean convergence inside $\theta$.  Multiplying
by $\theta$ and differentiating inverts this relation:
\begin{equation}
    \kappab(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\theta}\deriv{}{\theta}\big[\theta\,\alpha(\theta)\big] .
    \label{eq:pl_kappa_inversion}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{Einstein radius} is where the mean interior convergence is
unity, $\bar\kappa(\thetaE)=1$, equivalently $\alpha(\thetaE)=\thetaE$.

\paragraph{The signed one-dimensional lens equation.}
Source, lens centre, and images all lie on one line through the centre,
so we let $\theta$ run over the whole real line, negative values
denoting the side opposite the source.  The deflection is then an odd
function, $\alpha(-\theta)=-\alpha(\theta)$, and the lens equation is
\begin{equation}
    \ysrc = \theta - \alpha(\theta) ,
    \label{eq:pl_lens_eq_signed}
\end{equation}
with $\ysrc\ge0$ the source position.  This is
eq.~\eqref{eq:lens_eq_1d} of Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}
written for both sides of the lens at once; we use the letter $\ysrc$
for the signed source coordinate, as in the rest of Part~V.  Every image
of a source at $\ysrc_0$ is a root of $\ysrc(\theta)=\ysrc_0$.

\paragraph{Eigenvalues.}
The Jacobian of a circular lens is diagonal in polar coordinates, with
a tangential and a radial eigenvalue
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_t(\theta) = 1-\frac{\alpha(\theta)}{\theta} = 1-\bar\kappa(\theta) ,
    \qquad
    \lambda_r(\theta) = 1-\deriv{\alpha}{\theta} = 1+\bar\kappa(\theta)-2\kappab(\theta) ,
    \qquad
    \mu = \frac{1}{\lambda_t\,\lambda_r} .
    \label{eq:pl_eigenvalues}
\end{equation}
The tangential critical curve is $\lambda_t=0$ (arcs, Einstein rings);
the radial critical curve is $\lambda_r=0$ (radially stretched images).
The second form of $\lambda_r$ uses the identity
\begin{equation}
    \deriv{\alpha}{\theta} = 2\kappab(\theta)-\bar\kappa(\theta) ,
    \label{eq:pl_dalpha}
\end{equation}
which holds for every axisymmetric profile.

\begin{derivation}[The identity $\dd\alpha/\dd\theta = 2\kappa-\bar\kappa$]
Write $\alpha=\bar\kappa\,\theta$ and differentiate with the product rule:
\[
  \deriv{\alpha}{\theta} = \bar\kappa + \theta\,\deriv{\bar\kappa}{\theta} .
\]
Differentiating the definition
$\bar\kappa=2\theta^{-2}\int_0^\theta\kappab\,\theta'\,\dd\theta'$
gives two terms, one from the prefactor and one from the upper limit:
\[
  \deriv{\bar\kappa}{\theta}
  = -\frac{4}{\theta^3}\int_0^\theta\kappab\,\theta'\,\dd\theta' + \frac{2}{\theta^2}\,\kappab(\theta)\,\theta
  = \frac{2}{\theta}\big[\kappab(\theta)-\bar\kappa(\theta)\big] .
\]
Substituting, $\dd\alpha/\dd\theta = \bar\kappa + 2(\kappab-\bar\kappa) = 2\kappab-\bar\kappa$.
Then $\lambda_r = 1-2\kappab+\bar\kappa$.  Since the shear of a circular
lens is $|\gammaL|=\bar\kappa-\kappab$, this is also
$\lambda_r=1-\kappab+|\gammaL|$ and $\lambda_t=1-\kappab-|\gammaL|$.
[Checks 0a--0c of \texttt{power\_law\_point\_mass.wl}.]
\end{derivation}

\begin{bridge}[Chapters~\ref{ch:magnification} and~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}]
Equation~\eqref{eq:pl_eigenvalues} is
eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalues_circular} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models} (\S\ref{sec:circular_formalism}).
In the general language of Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}
(eq.~\ref{eq:mu_eigenvalues}) the two eigenvalues are
$\lambda_\mp = 1-\kappab\mp|\gammaL|$: for a circular lens the
\emph{tangential} eigenvalue is $\lambda_t=\lambda_-$ and the
\emph{radial} one is $\lambda_r=\lambda_+$.  Nothing new is being
introduced; we are only specializing a result you already have.
\end{bridge}

\paragraph{Superposition.}
Deflections add, so for a composite lens
$\alpha=\alpha_1+\alpha_2$ the \emph{departures from unity} of the
eigenvalues add too:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_t - 1 = (\lambda_{t,1}-1) + (\lambda_{t,2}-1) ,
    \qquad
    \lambda_r - 1 = (\lambda_{r,1}-1) + (\lambda_{r,2}-1) .
    \label{eq:pl_superposition}
\end{equation}
This one line is what makes the rest of the chapter work.

\begin{keybox}[Recipe: critical curves of any axisymmetric profile plus a point mass]
For a profile with mean convergence $\bar\kappa(\theta)$ and local
convergence $\kappab(\theta)$, plus a central point mass of Einstein
radius $\thetaBH$:
\begin{align}
    \lambda_t(\theta) &= 1-\bar\kappa(\theta)-\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta^2} ,
    \label{eq:pl_recipe_lt}\\
    \lambda_r(\theta) &= 1+\bar\kappa(\theta)-2\kappab(\theta)+\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta^2} .
    \label{eq:pl_recipe_lr}
\end{align}
The tangential critical curve solves $\lambda_t=0$ and the radial
critical curve(s) solve $\lambda_r=0$.  Each critical radius
$\theta_c$ maps to a caustic at $\ysrc_c=\theta_c-\alpha(\theta_c)$.
The point mass \emph{lowers} $\lambda_t$ and \emph{raises}
$\lambda_r$, by the same amount $\thetaBH^2/\theta^2$.
\end{keybox}

\begin{annotation}[Reading $\lambda_r$ as the slope of the map]
Because $\lambda_r=1-\dd\alpha/\dd\theta=\dd\ysrc/\dd\theta$, the
radial eigenvalue is just the slope of the curve $\ysrc(\theta)$.  A
radial critical curve is a place where that curve turns over (a local
maximum or minimum), and the corresponding radial caustic is the value
of $\ysrc$ there.  Sources whose $\ysrc_0$ lies beyond a turning value
lose two roots of $\ysrc(\theta)=\ysrc_0$; sources inside gain them.
So you can count images by eye from a plot of $\ysrc(\theta)$, the
``image diagram'' of \citet{meneghetti_introduction_2021} (\S5.2.1).
The tangential eigenvalue has a similar reading:
$\lambda_t=\ysrc/\theta$, so it vanishes exactly where the map passes
through $\ysrc=0$ away from the centre, i.e.\ on the Einstein ring.
\end{annotation}

\section{The Power-Law Lens}
\label{sec:pl_powerlaw}

\subsection{Convergence and deflection}

Take the spherical power law of \S\ref{sec:power_law_profile},
$\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$.  Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing} projected it
along the line of sight with a Beta-function integral and found
$\Sigma\propto R^{1-\gp}$ (finite for $\gp>1$), hence a convergence
with the same exponent, normalized as in
eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_power_law}.  We take that result as our starting
point and derive the deflection from it.  Writing the convergence as
\begin{equation}
    \kappab(\theta)=\frac{3-\gp}{2}\left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{\gp-1} ,
    \qquad 1<\gp<3 ,
    \label{eq:pl_kappa}
\end{equation}
the enclosed-mass integral~\eqref{eq:pl_alpha_kbar} gives
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta)=\thetaE^{\,\gp-1}\,\theta^{\,2-\gp}\;}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \bar\kappa(\theta)=\left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{\gp-1} .
    \label{eq:pl_alpha}
\end{equation}
The Einstein condition $\alpha(\thetaE)=\thetaE$ holds for every
$\gp$; the prefactor $(3-\gp)/2$ in~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa} is exactly what
makes it so.  In the isothermal case $\gp=2$ the deflection is
constant, $\alpha=\thetaE$, and $\kappab=\thetaE/(2\theta)$: the SIS of
\S\ref{sec:sis}.

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}: where the convergence comes from]
The projection $\rho\propto r^{-\gp}\to\Sigma\propto R^{1-\gp}$ was
done in \S\ref{sec:power_law_profile} with the substitution $u=z/\xi$
and the integral
$\int_0^\infty(1+u^2)^{-\gp/2}\dd u=\tfrac12 B\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\gp-1}{2}\big)$,
which is finite only for $\gp>1$.  That integral fixes the
\emph{physical} normalization (in terms of $\rho_0$ and $r_0$).  For
lensing we only need the shape $\kappab\propto\theta^{1-\gp}$; the
normalization is then fixed most cleanly by demanding
$\bar\kappa(\thetaE)=1$, as in the derivation below.  The upper limit
$\gp<3$ is a separate requirement: it keeps the projected mass inside
any finite radius finite.
\end{bridge}

\begin{derivation}[Deflection and normalization from the enclosed mass]
Start from the shape $\kappab = A\,\theta^{1-\gp}$ with an unknown
constant $A$.  The integrand of~\eqref{eq:pl_alpha_kbar} is
$\kappab\,\theta' = A\,\theta'^{\,2-\gp}$, so
\[
  \int_0^\theta A\,\theta'^{\,2-\gp}\,\dd\theta' = \frac{A\,\theta^{\,3-\gp}}{3-\gp}
  \qquad(\text{convergent at }0\text{ only if }\gp<3),
\]
and
\[
  \alpha(\theta) = \frac{2}{\theta}\cdot\frac{A\,\theta^{\,3-\gp}}{3-\gp}
                 = \frac{2A}{3-\gp}\,\theta^{\,2-\gp} .
\]
Impose $\alpha(\thetaE)=\thetaE$:
$2A\,\thetaE^{\,2-\gp}/(3-\gp)=\thetaE$, so
$A=\tfrac{3-\gp}{2}\,\thetaE^{\,\gp-1}$.  Substituting back gives
eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa} and $\alpha=\thetaE^{\,\gp-1}\theta^{\,2-\gp}$.
Going the other way, eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa_inversion} gives
$\theta\alpha=\thetaE^{\gp-1}\theta^{3-\gp}$,
$\dd(\theta\alpha)/\dd\theta=(3-\gp)\thetaE^{\gp-1}\theta^{2-\gp}$, and
dividing by $2\theta$ recovers~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa}.
[Checks 1a--1e of \texttt{power\_law\_point\_mass.wl}.]
\end{derivation}

\paragraph{Cross-check against the literature.}
\citet{meneghetti_introduction_2021} (\S5.2, eqs.~5.38--5.40) writes the
same lens in units of $\thetaE$, $x=\theta/\thetaE$, through the
dimensionless enclosed mass $m(x)=x^{3-n}$, with $n$ the 3-D slope:
$\kappab=m'(x)/(2x)=\tfrac{3-n}{2}x^{1-n}$ and $\alpha=m/x=x^{2-n}$.
With $n\equiv\gp$ these are eqs.~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa}
and~\eqref{eq:pl_alpha} divided by $\thetaE$.
\citet{keeton_2001_catalog} (eqs.~26 and~29, ``softened power law'')
uses $\kappab=\tfrac12 b^{2-\alpha_{\rm K}}(s^2+\xi^2)^{\alpha_{\rm K}/2-1}$
with $M_{\rm cyl}\propto r^{\alpha_{\rm K}}$ asymptotically.  Setting the
core $s=0$, $\alpha_{\rm K}=3-\gp$, and
$b^{\gp-1}=(3-\gp)\,\thetaE^{\gp-1}$ reproduces both of our
expressions; Keeton's scale $b$ equals $\thetaE$ only for the
isothermal case.  [Checks 1i--1n.]

\begin{annotation}[The slope-convention trap: $\gp$ versus $s=\gp-1$]
Three different numbers are all called ``the slope'' of the same
power-law lens, and confusing them is the most common error in
power-law work:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  quantity & exponent & value for $\rho\propto r^{-1.5}$ \\ \hline
  3-D density $\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$ & $\gp$ (Meneghetti's $n$) & $1.5$ \\
  convergence $\kappab\propto\theta^{-s}$ & $s=\gp-1$ & $0.5$ \\
  enclosed mass $M_{\rm cyl}\propto\theta^{\alpha_{\rm K}}$ & $\alpha_{\rm K}=3-\gp$ (Keeton) & $1.5$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The isothermal case is $\gp=2$, $s=1$, $\alpha_{\rm K}=1$.  Before
using any formula, lens-modeling code, or prior range, check
\emph{which} exponent it means.  A prior box written for $\gp$ but
applied to $s$ (or vice versa) can silently exclude the true model: a
fit can then fail to converge for a reason that looks, from the
outside, exactly like a physical degeneracy.  The broken power law of
\S\ref{sec:bpl_broken} is written in convergence slopes $t=\gp-1$, and we flag
the trap again there.  [AN2, AN8 of \texttt{problems\_15.wl}.]
\end{annotation}

\begin{annotation}[A one-line sanity check: $\kappab(\thetaE)=(3-\gp)/2$]
Evaluate~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa} at $\theta=\thetaE$:
$\kappab(\thetaE)=(3-\gp)/2$.  For the SIS this is $1/2$; for
$\gp=1.5$ it is $0.75$; it is below unity for every $\gp>1$, even
though the \emph{mean} convergence inside $\thetaE$ is exactly $1$.
Two useful consequences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The local convergence is a fixed fraction of the mean one at
        every radius, $\kappab=\tfrac{3-\gp}{2}\bar\kappa$, so the shear
        is $|\gammaL|=\bar\kappa-\kappab=\tfrac{\gp-1}{2}\bar\kappa$.
  \item The radial eigenvalue at the Einstein radius is
        $\lambda_r(\thetaE)=\gp-1=2[1-\kappab(\thetaE)]$ (it is $0.5$
        for $\gp=1.5$).  Since the radial magnification of arcs near
        the Einstein ring is $1/\lambda_r$, the local convergence at
        $\thetaE$, equivalently the slope, controls how thick the arcs
        look.
\end{itemize}
If a code or a derivation gives you a power law with
$\kappab(\thetaE)\ne(3-\gp)/2$, its $\thetaE$ is not the Einstein
radius as defined here.  [Checks 1f--1h, 2d; numbers in AN1 of
\texttt{problems\_15.wl}.]
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Critical curves of the power law}

Inserting~\eqref{eq:pl_alpha} into~\eqref{eq:pl_eigenvalues},
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_t = 1-\left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{\gp-1} ,
    \qquad
    \lambda_r = 1-(2-\gp)\left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{\gp-1} .
    \label{eq:pl_lambdas}
\end{equation}

\begin{keybox}[Critical radii of a power law]
\begin{align}
    \text{tangential:}\quad & \theta_t=\thetaE ,
    \label{eq:pl_theta_t}\\
    \text{radial:}\quad & \theta_r=\thetaE\,(2-\gp)^{1/(\gp-1)}
    \qquad(\text{exists only for }1<\gp<2) .
    \label{eq:pl_theta_r}
\end{align}
As $\gp\to2$ (isothermal), $\theta_r\to0$: the singular isothermal
sphere has no radial critical curve.  Shallower profiles push
$\theta_r$ outward: $\theta_r/\thetaE=0.0427$ at $\gp=1.95$, $0.217$ at
$\gp=1.6$, $0.25$ at $\gp=1.5$, approaching $1/e$ as $\gp\to1$.
\end{keybox}

\begin{derivation}[Solving $\lambda_r=0$]
Set the radial eigenvalue in~\eqref{eq:pl_lambdas} to zero:
\[
  (2-\gp)\left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{\gp-1}=1
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \left(\frac{\theta}{\thetaE}\right)^{\gp-1}=2-\gp
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \theta_r=\thetaE\,(2-\gp)^{1/(\gp-1)} .
\]
The second step raises both sides to the power $1/(\gp-1)$, which is
legitimate only if the base is positive and real, i.e.\ $\gp<2$.  For
$\gp\ge2$ write $\lambda_r=1+(\gp-2)(\thetaE/\theta)^{\gp-1}\ge1$: it
never vanishes.  For $1<\gp<2$ the base $2-\gp$ lies in $(0,1)$ and the
exponent is positive, so $0<\theta_r<\thetaE$.  As $\gp\to2^-$ the base
goes to $0$ with exponent $\to1$, hence $\theta_r\to0$.  As
$\gp\to1^+$, substitute $n'=1/(\gp-1)$: $\theta_r/\thetaE=(1-1/n')^{n'}\to
e^{-1}$, which is eq.~5.44 of \citet{meneghetti_introduction_2021}.  The
tangential root is simpler: $\lambda_t=0$ when $(\thetaE/\theta)^{\gp-1}=1$,
i.e.\ $\theta=\thetaE$, for every slope.  [Checks 3a--3i; AN9.]
\end{derivation}

The radial critical curve maps to a radial caustic at
\begin{equation}
    \ysrc_r = \theta_r-\alpha(\theta_r)
            = -\frac{\gp-1}{2-\gp}\,\theta_r
            = -\thetaE\,(\gp-1)(2-\gp)^{(2-\gp)/(\gp-1)} ,
    \label{eq:pl_radial_caustic}
\end{equation}
which is $-\thetaE/4$ at $\gp=1.5$.  The negative sign means the
critical point lies on the opposite side of the lens from the sources
it serves.  As $\gp\to2^-$, $|\ysrc_r|\to\thetaE$: the radial caustic
grows even as the radial critical curve shrinks to a point (compare the
statement after eq.~5.44 of \citet{meneghetti_introduction_2021}).
[Checks 3j--3k.]

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}: two routes to a radial critical curve]
In \S\ref{sec:nis} a radial critical curve appeared when the SIS was
given a \emph{core}: the NIS has one for $\theta_c<\thetaE/2$
(Figure~\ref{fig:nis_critical_curves}).  Here the profile has no core
at all ($\kappab\to\infty$ at the centre) and still has a radial
critical curve, as long as the cusp is \emph{shallower} than
isothermal.  The common thread is eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_recipe_lr} without
the point mass: $\lambda_r=1+\bar\kappa-2\kappab$ must change sign.
Near the centre of a power law $2\kappab-\bar\kappa=(2-\gp)\bar\kappa$
grows without bound when $\gp<2$, which forces $\lambda_r<0$ at small
$\theta$; for the SIS the coefficient $2-\gp$ vanishes and the
divergence is cancelled exactly.  ``Cuspy'' therefore does not mean
``no radial image''; ``at least as steep as isothermal'' does.
\end{bridge}

\subsection{Reading the source-plane map}
\label{sec:pl_reading_map}

For $1<\gp<2$ the curve $\ysrc(\theta)=\theta-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta)$
on the $\theta>0$ side starts at $\ysrc(0)=0$ (because
$\alpha_{\rm PL}\propto\theta^{2-\gp}\to0$), dips negative to a minimum
$\ysrc_r$ at $\theta_r$, returns to zero at $\theta=\thetaE$, and then
rises.  The $\theta<0$ side is the mirror image, $\ysrc(-\theta)=-\ysrc(\theta)$.
A source at $0<\ysrc_0<|\ysrc_r|$ therefore has three images:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item an outer image at $\theta>\thetaE$ (positive parity);
  \item a counter-image on the far side, between $-\thetaE$ and
        $-\theta_r$;
  \item a \textbf{radial} image on the far side, between $-\theta_r$
        and the centre.
\end{itemize}
Outside the radial caustic, $\ysrc_0>|\ysrc_r|$, only the outer image
survives.  For $\gp=1.5$ ($|\ysrc_r|=\thetaE/4$) a source at
$\ysrc_0=0.05\,\thetaE$ has three images and one at $0.3\,\thetaE$ has
one [check 5q].  This is the familiar odd-number count for a
nonsingular lens; Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images} returns to it.

\section{The Point Mass}
\label{sec:pl_point_mass}

A point mass $M_\bullet$ at the origin puts all of its convergence into
a delta function; away from the centre $\kappab=0$ and
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_\bullet(\theta)=\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta} ,
    \qquad
    \thetaBH^2=\frac{4\Gnewton M_\bullet}{\speedoflight^2}\frac{\Dds}{\Dd\Ds} ,
    \qquad
    \lambda_t=1-\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta^2} ,
    \qquad
    \lambda_r=1+\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta^2} .
    \label{eq:pl_point_mass}
\end{equation}
(This is the potential $\psi=\thetaBH^2\ln\theta$ of
\citet{keeton_2001_catalog}, eqs.~23--24.)  Two facts matter for the
rest of the chapter.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\lambda_t=0$ at $\theta=\thetaBH$ (the point mass's own
        Einstein ring), but $\lambda_r>0$ \emph{everywhere}:
        \textbf{a point mass has no radial critical curve of its own.}
  \item $\alpha_\bullet\to\infty$ as $\theta\to0$, so the point mass
        dominates the innermost region of any lens it is added to.
\end{enumerate}
We measure its strength by
\begin{equation}
    \mBH \equiv \frac{\thetaBH^2}{\thetaE^2} = \frac{M_\bullet}{M_{\rm PL}(<\thetaE)} ,
    \label{eq:pl_mass_fraction}
\end{equation}
the point mass as a fraction of the mass of the power law inside
$\thetaE$.  The second equality uses
$\thetaBH^2=M_\bullet/(\pi\Sigmacr\Dd^2)$, which follows from
eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_enclosed_mass}, together with
$M_{\rm PL}(<\thetaE)=\pi\Sigmacr\Dd^2\thetaE^2\,\bar\kappa(\thetaE)$
and $\bar\kappa(\thetaE)=1$ [check 4f].  Throughout this chapter
$\thetaE$ denotes the Einstein radius of the power law \emph{alone};
the tangential critical curve of the composite lens sits slightly
outside it (Problem~\ref{ex:pl_tangential_shift}).  For a black hole
in a galaxy, $\mBH\ll1$.

\begin{bridge}[Chapters~\ref{ch:magnification} and~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}: the point mass you already know]
Equation~\eqref{eq:pl_point_mass} is the point-mass lens of
\S\ref{sec:point_mass_recap} (and \S\ref{sec:point_mass_example} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}) with its Einstein radius renamed
$\thetaBH$, so that $\thetaE$ stays free for the galaxy.  The remark in
\S\ref{sec:point_mass_recap} already noted that
$\lambda_r=1+\bar\kappa>0$; here $\bar\kappa=\thetaBH^2/\theta^2$.  What
is new in this chapter is what that positive $\lambda_r$ does when it
is \emph{added} to a profile whose own $\lambda_r$ goes negative.
\end{bridge}

\section{Power Law plus Point Mass}
\label{sec:pl_plpm}

\subsection{Where the point mass acts}

Apply the recipe~\eqref{eq:pl_recipe_lt}--\eqref{eq:pl_recipe_lr} to
the power law~\eqref{eq:pl_alpha} plus the point
mass~\eqref{eq:pl_point_mass}.  In units $u=\theta/\thetaE$,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_r(u) = 1-(2-\gp)\,u^{1-\gp}+\mBH\,u^{-2} ,
    \qquad
    \lambda_t(u) = 1-u^{1-\gp}-\mBH\,u^{-2} .
    \label{eq:pl_plpm_lambdas}
\end{equation}
The point mass enters $\lambda_r$ with a plus sign: it can only push
the radial eigenvalue up, toward and past zero.  How hard it pushes,
relative to the power law, depends on radius.  The ratio of the two
terms that compete in $\lambda_r-1$ is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mBH\,u^{-2}}{(2-\gp)\,u^{1-\gp}} = \frac{\mBH}{2-\gp}\,u^{-(3-\gp)} .
    \label{eq:pl_weight_ratio}
\end{equation}
Since $3-\gp>0$, the point mass gains on the power law as $u$
decreases.  Compared with its weight at the Einstein radius, its weight
at the radial critical curve is larger by $(\thetaE/\theta_r)^{3-\gp}$:
a factor of $4^{3/2}=8$ for $\gp=1.5$.  And at the very centre the
point-mass term always wins, $\lambda_r\to+\infty$, whereas the pure
power law has $\lambda_r\to-\infty$ there [checks 5a--5e].

\begin{keybox}[Why a central point mass acts precisely where radial images form]
A point mass changes $\lambda_t$ and $\lambda_r$ by $\mp\thetaBH^2/\theta^2$.
At the Einstein ring this is a small correction of size $\mBH$.  The
radial critical curve, however, lives deep inside, at
$\theta_r<\thetaE$, where the same term is larger by
$(\thetaE/\theta_r)^2$, and larger \emph{relative to the profile's own
contribution} by $(\thetaE/\theta_r)^{3-\gp}$.  Radial images form
exactly in the region a central mass controls.  A black hole that
barely moves the Einstein ring can therefore decide whether a radial
image exists at all.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The stationary point and the critical mass fraction}

With $\mBH>0$, $\lambda_r$ is $+\infty$ at the centre and tends to $1$
from below at large radius, so it has a minimum in between.  A radial
critical curve exists if and only if that minimum is $\le0$.

\begin{derivation}[The minimum of $\lambda_r$ and the annihilation threshold]
\textbf{Stationary point.}  Differentiate~\eqref{eq:pl_plpm_lambdas}
and multiply by $u^3>0$:
\[
  u^3\deriv{\lambda_r}{u} = (2-\gp)(\gp-1)\,u^{3-\gp}-2\mBH .
\]
The right-hand side increases monotonically in $u$ (its derivative is
$(2-\gp)(\gp-1)(3-\gp)u^{2-\gp}>0$), runs from $-2\mBH$ at $u=0$ to
$+\infty$, and so has exactly one zero:
\[
  u_\star=\left[\frac{2\mBH}{(2-\gp)(\gp-1)}\right]^{1/(3-\gp)} .
\]
This single stationary point is the global minimum.

\textbf{Value at the minimum.}  At $u_\star$ the stationarity condition
gives $\mBH u_\star^{-2}=\tfrac12(2-\gp)(\gp-1)u_\star^{1-\gp}$.
Substituting into $\lambda_r$,
\[
  \lambda_r(u_\star) = 1-(2-\gp)u_\star^{1-\gp}\Big[1-\frac{\gp-1}{2}\Big]
                     = 1-\frac{(2-\gp)(3-\gp)}{2}\,u_\star^{1-\gp} .
\]

\textbf{Threshold.}  The radial critical curve disappears when this
minimum touches zero, $\lambda_r(u_\star)=0$, which fixes the location
\[
  u_c=\left[\frac{(2-\gp)(3-\gp)}{2}\right]^{1/(\gp-1)} ,
\]
and then the stationarity condition fixes the mass:
$\mBH_{\rm crit}=\tfrac12(2-\gp)(\gp-1)\,u_c^{3-\gp}$, which is
eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_mcrit}.  [Checks 5f--5j.]
\end{derivation}

\begin{keybox}[Annihilation of the radial critical curve]
A power law with $1<\gp<2$ plus a central point mass of fractional mass
$\mBH$ has a radial critical curve if and only if $\mBH\le\mBH_{\rm crit}(\gp)$, with
\begin{equation}
    \mBH_{\rm crit}(\gp)=\frac{(2-\gp)(\gp-1)}{2}
      \left[\frac{(2-\gp)(3-\gp)}{2}\right]^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)} .
    \label{eq:pl_mcrit}
\end{equation}
At $\gp=3/2$ this is exactly $\mBH_{\rm crit}=27/4096\approx0.0066$,
with the merger at $u_c=9/64$.  Over the whole range $1<\gp<2$ the
threshold never exceeds $0.0066$ (maximum at $\gp\approx1.48$), and it
vanishes at both ends.
\end{keybox}

The numbers are worth absorbing.  A point mass carrying less than one
per cent of the projected mass inside the Einstein radius is enough to
remove the radial critical curve of \emph{any} power law [checks
5k--5m].  Figure~\ref{fig:pl_lambda_r_annihilation} shows how it
happens.  Without the point mass, $\lambda_r$ rises monotonically from
$-\infty$ and crosses zero once, at $\theta_r$.  A small point mass
bends the curve back up at the centre, so that $\lambda_r$ crosses zero
\emph{twice}: there are now \textbf{two} radial critical curves, an
inner one created by the point mass and an outer one inherited from
the power law.  As $\mBH$ grows the two approach each other, merge at
$u_c$ when $\mBH=\mBH_{\rm crit}$, and disappear together.  This is the
annihilation of the radial critical curve [check 5n].

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../Figures/15_PowerLaw_PointMass/lambda_r_annihilation.pdf}
    \caption{Annihilation of the radial critical curve by a central
    point mass.
    \textbf{(a)}~Radial eigenvalue $\lambda_r(\theta)$,
    eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_plpm_lambdas}, for a $\gp=1.5$ power law with
    point masses $\mBH=0$, $\mBH_{\rm crit}/2$, $\mBH_{\rm crit}$, and
    $2\mBH_{\rm crit}$ ($\mBH_{\rm crit}=27/4096$).  The dashed line marks
    the radial critical radius $\theta_r=\thetaE/4$ of the bare power
    law.  For $0<\mBH<\mBH_{\rm crit}$ the curve crosses zero twice (two
    radial critical curves); at $\mBH_{\rm crit}$ it touches zero; above
    it $\lambda_r>0$ everywhere.
    \textbf{(b)}~The threshold $\mBH_{\rm crit}(\gp)$ of
    eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_mcrit}.  Below the curve the lens has radial
    critical curves; above it, none.  Generated by
    \texttt{power\_law\_point\_mass.wl}.}
    \label{fig:pl_lambda_r_annihilation}
\end{figure}

\begin{annotation}[Why the threshold vanishes at both ends]
At $\gp\to2$ the power law's own radial curve has already shrunk to
the centre ($\theta_r\to0$), so an arbitrarily small point mass, whose
influence grows without bound inward, overwhelms it.  At $\gp\to1$ the
power law approaches a uniform sheet with $\kappab\to1$ and
$\lambda_r\to0$ everywhere, so its $\lambda_r$ inside $\theta_r$ is only
marginally negative, and again an arbitrarily small push removes it.  The radial
critical curve is most robust at intermediate slopes, but even there it
yields to less than one per cent of the Einstein mass.  [Checks 5l,
5m; the $0.0066$ maximum is AN5 of \texttt{problems\_15.wl}.]
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Caustics and image counts}

Each radial critical curve has its own caustic.  Below threshold the
\emph{inner} radial curve maps to a caustic of radius $|\ysrc_{\rm in}|$
and the outer one to $|\ysrc_{\rm out}|$, with
$|\ysrc_{\rm in}|<|\ysrc_{\rm out}|$; for $\gp=1.5$ and $\mBH=0.003$
these are $0.234\,\thetaE$ and $0.263\,\thetaE$ [check 5r].  Reading
the image diagram (the annotation of \S\ref{sec:pl_toolbox}) then gives
the image count as a function of source position:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\ysrc_0<|\ysrc_{\rm in}|$: \textbf{two} images, the outer
        image and the counter-image.  The radial image is gone.
  \item $|\ysrc_{\rm in}|<\ysrc_0<|\ysrc_{\rm out}|$: \textbf{four}
        images: the outer image, the counter-image, the radial image,
        and an extra faint image very close to the point mass.
  \item $\ysrc_0>|\ysrc_{\rm out}|$: \textbf{two} images, the outer
        image and a faint image very close to the point mass.
\end{itemize}
Above threshold, $\mBH>\mBH_{\rm crit}$, $\ysrc(\theta)$ is monotonic on
each side and there are exactly two images for every source position
[checks 5s, 5w].  Because the point mass makes the lens singular, these
counts are even, not odd; Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images} explains why.  At
$\mBH=\mBH_{\rm crit}$ the two caustics coincide (at
$|\ysrc|=9\thetaE/32$ for $\gp=1.5$) and the four-image annulus closes
[check 5t].

The inner caustic is a \textbf{zone of influence}: sources inside it
lose their radial image.  It is large.  To leading order in small
$\mBH$ the inner radial curve sits where the point-mass and power-law
terms of $\lambda_r$ balance, $u_{\rm in}\simeq[\mBH/(2-\gp)]^{1/(3-\gp)}$,
and its caustic radius is
\begin{equation}
    |\ysrc_{\rm in}| \simeq (3-\gp)\left(\frac{\mBH}{2-\gp}\right)^{(2-\gp)/(3-\gp)}\thetaE .
    \label{eq:pl_zone}
\end{equation}
For $\gp=1.5$ this scales as $\mBH^{1/3}$: a point mass of
$\mBH=10^{-3}$ already suppresses the radial image for every source
within $0.17\,\thetaE$ of the axis (the leading-order formula gives
$0.19$) [checks 5u--5v; AN4].  \citet{mao_witt_koopmans_2001} found the
same behaviour for a cored isothermal ellipsoid with a central black
hole: above a critical black-hole mass the central images are
suppressed for all source positions, while below it they are
suppressed only inside a zone of influence, and the black hole can
create an additional image.  Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias} takes up
what happens when such a point mass is present but left out of the
model.

\begin{derivation}[The zone-of-influence radius~\eqref{eq:pl_zone}]
For $\mBH\to0$ the inner radial curve moves to $u\to0$, where the
``$1$'' in $\lambda_r$ is negligible against the two power-law terms.
Setting $(2-\gp)u^{1-\gp}=\mBH u^{-2}$ gives
$u_{\rm in}^{3-\gp}=\mBH/(2-\gp)$.  The caustic is
$\ysrc_{\rm in}=u_{\rm in}-u_{\rm in}^{2-\gp}-\mBH/u_{\rm in}$ (in units of
$\thetaE$).  The first term is subleading; the third equals
$(2-\gp)u_{\rm in}^{2-\gp}$ by the balance condition.  So
$\ysrc_{\rm in}\simeq-(3-\gp)u_{\rm in}^{2-\gp}$, which
is~\eqref{eq:pl_zone}.  The neglected terms are smaller by a factor of
order $u_{\rm in}^{\gp-1}$, so convergence is slow for $\gp$ near $1$;
Mathematica checks that the exact-to-approximate ratio tends to $1$ as
$\mBH\to0$ for $\gp=1.3$, $1.5$, $1.7$.
\end{derivation}

\subsection{The tangential critical curve}

The point mass also moves the tangential critical curve, but only
slightly.  From~\eqref{eq:pl_plpm_lambdas}, $\lambda_t=0$ now requires
$u^{1-\gp}+\mBH u^{-2}=1$, whose root lies just outside $u=1$:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_t \simeq \thetaE\left(1+\frac{\mBH}{\gp-1}\right) .
    \label{eq:pl_theta_t_shift}
\end{equation}
For $\mBH\sim10^{-3}$ this is a per-mille effect, compared with the
order-unity effect on the radial curve.  The derivation is
Problem~\ref{ex:pl_tangential_shift} [checks 5o--5p].

\section{The Singular Isothermal Sphere plus a Point Mass}
\label{sec:pl_sis_pm}

The case $\gp=2$ with a central point mass is the one composite lens
that solves in elementary closed form.  The signed lens
equation~\eqref{eq:pl_lens_eq_signed} is
\begin{equation}
    \ysrc = \theta-\thetaE\,\mathrm{sgn}(\theta)-\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta} .
    \label{eq:pl_sis_pm_lens_eq}
\end{equation}
On the $\theta>0$ branch, multiplying by $\theta$ gives a quadratic
whose positive root is the outer image:
\begin{equation}
    \theta^2-(\ysrc+\thetaE)\,\theta-\thetaBH^2=0
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \theta_+=\tfrac12\Big[(\ysrc+\thetaE)+\sqrt{(\ysrc+\thetaE)^2+4\thetaBH^2}\Big] .
    \label{eq:pl_sis_pm_plus}
\end{equation}
On the $\theta<0$ branch, writing $\theta=-s$ with $s>0$,
\begin{equation}
    s^2+(\ysrc-\thetaE)\,s-\thetaBH^2=0
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \theta_-=-\tfrac12\Big[(\thetaE-\ysrc)+\sqrt{(\thetaE-\ysrc)^2+4\thetaBH^2}\Big] .
    \label{eq:pl_sis_pm_minus}
\end{equation}
As $\thetaBH\to0$ these reduce to the SIS images $\ysrc\pm\thetaE$ of
eq.~\eqref{eq:sis_two_images} (for $\ysrc<\thetaE$).  Both closed forms
agree with a brute-force numerical root solve of~\eqref{eq:pl_sis_pm_lens_eq}
to better than $10^{-9}$ for $\thetaE=1$, $\thetaBH=0.08$ and
$\ysrc=0.3$ or $1.4$ [checks 6a--6h].

\begin{keybox}[SIS plus point mass: always exactly two images]
Each quadratic has root product $-\thetaBH^2<0$, so each has exactly
one positive root: \textbf{one image per side, two in total, for every
source position}.  For $\ysrc<\thetaE$ these are the SIS pair, each
pushed slightly away from the centre.  For $\ysrc>\thetaE$, where the
bare SIS has a single image, the point mass keeps a second, faint
image on the far side at $|\theta_-|\simeq\thetaBH^2/(\ysrc-\thetaE)$.
There is no radial critical curve, since
$\lambda_r=1+\thetaBH^2/\theta^2>0$, so the SIS plus point mass has no
radial image for any $\mBH$.
\end{keybox}

\begin{annotation}[A tempting misreading]
The $\theta>0$ quadratic~\eqref{eq:pl_sis_pm_plus} has a second,
negative root, $\tfrac12[(\ysrc+\thetaE)-\sqrt{\cdots}]$, and since the
product of the roots is $-\thetaBH^2$ it is tempting to call it a
``central image seeded by the point mass''.  It is not an image: it is
a root of the $\theta>0$ equation lying at $\theta<0$, where that
equation does not apply.  The only valid root on the far side is the
one from~\eqref{eq:pl_sis_pm_minus}.  The general lesson for piecewise
lens equations: solve each branch separately, then keep only the roots
that lie on their own branch.  [Check 6e; the $\ysrc>\thetaE$ example
is AN7 of \texttt{problems\_15.wl}: $\theta_-\simeq-0.016\,\thetaE$ for
$\ysrc=1.4\,\thetaE$, $\thetaBH=0.08\,\thetaE$.]
\end{annotation}

To first order in $\mBH$, both images move \emph{away} from the centre
by $\delta\theta_\pm=\thetaBH^2/\theta_\pm^{(0)}$, where
$\theta_\pm^{(0)}=\ysrc\pm\thetaE$ are the SIS positions
(Problem~\ref{ex:pl_sis_pm_shift}).  This small, linear astrometric
shift is the starting point of the bias calculations in
Chapters~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias} and~\ref{ch:perturbative_estimators}.

\section{Broken Power Laws}
\label{sec:bpl_broken}

A single power law forces one slope on the whole galaxy.  The simplest
generalization lets the inner slope differ from the outer one.  We now
show that this extra freedom is closely related to a central point
mass: the point mass is a limiting case of the broken power law, and
outside the break the two are indistinguishable.

\subsection{Definition and slope convention}
\label{sec:bpl_convention}

Define the broken power law directly in the convergence,
\begin{equation}
    \kappab(\theta)=
    \begin{cases}
      \kappa_B\,(\theta_B/\theta)^{t_1}, & \theta\le\theta_B ,\\[2pt]
      \kappa_B\,(\theta_B/\theta)^{t_2}, & \theta>\theta_B ,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:bpl_kappa}
\end{equation}
continuous at the \textbf{break radius} $\theta_B$, with inner slope
$t_1$ and outer slope $t_2$.  These are \emph{convergence} slopes: for
a 3-D density $\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$ the convergence falls as
$\theta^{-(\gp-1)}$, so
\begin{equation}
    t=\gp-1 .
    \label{eq:bpl_slope_convention}
\end{equation}
The isothermal case is $t=1$; a $\gp=1.5$ profile has $t=0.5$.  A
finite projected mass at the centre needs $t<2$ (i.e.\ $\gp<3$), and
the radial images of \S\ref{sec:pl_powerlaw} need $t<1$.

\begin{annotation}[The slope trap, again]
Equation~\eqref{eq:bpl_slope_convention} is the trap of
\S\ref{sec:pl_powerlaw} in a new form.  A broken-power-law code that
takes convergence slopes $t$, fed a prior range meant for density
slopes (say $1.3<t_2<2.2$), cannot represent a $\gp=1.5$ galaxy
($t_2=0.5$) at all.  A cheap test before trusting any fit: with
$t_1=t_2=t$ the broken power law must reproduce the single power law
of slope $\gp=t+1$ exactly (check 2c--2d of
\texttt{broken\_power\_law.wl}).  If a configuration known to fit the
data is outside the prior box, no amount of sampling will find it.
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Deflection in closed form}
\label{sec:bpl_deflection}

Integrating~\eqref{eq:bpl_kappa} piecewise
in~\eqref{eq:pl_alpha_kbar},
\begin{align}
    \theta\le\theta_B:\qquad
    \alpha(\theta) &= \frac{2\kappa_B\,\theta_B^{2}}{(2-t_1)\,\theta}
                     \left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_B}\right)^{2-t_1} ,
    \label{eq:bpl_alpha_in}\\[4pt]
    \theta>\theta_B:\qquad
    \alpha(\theta) &= \frac{2\kappa_B\,\theta_B^{2}}{\theta}
    \left[\frac{1}{2-t_1}+\frac{(\theta/\theta_B)^{2-t_2}-1}{2-t_2}\right] .
    \label{eq:bpl_alpha_out}
\end{align}

\begin{derivation}[Integrating the broken profile]
The deflection is $\alpha=m_{2\rm D}(\theta)/(\pi\theta)$ with
$m_{2\rm D}(\theta)=2\pi\int_0^\theta\kappab\,\theta'\,\dd\theta'$ the
(reduced) enclosed mass.  Inside the break,
\[
  m_{2\rm D}(\theta)=2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^{t_1}\int_0^\theta\theta'^{\,1-t_1}\dd\theta'
  =\frac{2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^{2}}{2-t_1}\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_B}\right)^{2-t_1} ,
\]
convergent at the origin because $t_1<2$; dividing by $\pi\theta$
gives~\eqref{eq:bpl_alpha_in}.  Outside, split the integral at
$\theta_B$.  The inner disc contributes the constant
$2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^2/(2-t_1)$; the annulus contributes
\[
  2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^{t_2}\int_{\theta_B}^\theta\theta'^{\,1-t_2}\dd\theta'
  =\frac{2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^{2}}{2-t_2}\left[\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_B}\right)^{2-t_2}-1\right] .
\]
Adding and dividing by $\pi\theta$ gives~\eqref{eq:bpl_alpha_out}.
\textbf{Checks.}  At $\theta=\theta_B$ both branches equal
$2\kappa_B\theta_B/(2-t_1)$, so $\alpha$ is continuous; since $\kappab$
is continuous, so is $\dd\alpha/\dd\theta=2\kappab-\bar\kappa$, and
$\lambda_r$ has no jump at the break.  With $t_1=t_2=t$ the bracket
collapses and $\alpha=2\kappa_B\theta_B^t\theta^{1-t}/(2-t)$, the single
power law of \S\ref{sec:pl_powerlaw} with
$\thetaE^{t}=2\kappa_B\theta_B^t/(2-t)$.
[Checks 1a--2d of \texttt{broken\_power\_law.wl}.]
\end{derivation}

\paragraph{Normalization.}
Define $\thetaE$ by $\alpha(\thetaE)=\thetaE$ and write
$\nu\equiv\theta_B/\thetaE<1$.  Then~\eqref{eq:bpl_alpha_out} fixes the
break convergence:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_B = \frac{2-t_1}{2\nu^{2}\left[1+\Delta t\,\big(\nu^{\,t_2-2}-1\big)\right]} ,
    \qquad
    \Delta t\equiv\frac{2-t_1}{2-t_2} .
    \label{eq:bpl_norm}
\end{equation}
For $t_1=t_2=t$ this reduces to $\kappa_B=\tfrac{2-t}{2}\nu^{-t}$,
i.e.\ $\kappab(\thetaE)=\kappa_B\nu^t=(2-t)/2=(3-\gp)/2$: the
annotation check of \S\ref{sec:pl_powerlaw} again [checks 3a--3c].

\subsection{The point mass as the corner $t_1\to2$}
\label{sec:bpl_pm_limit}

The inner slope cannot reach $t_1=2$, where the enclosed mass diverges
logarithmically.  But it can approach it while the inner mass is held
fixed.  Fix the projected mass inside the break,
$m_{2\rm D}(\theta_B)=2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^2/(2-t_1)\equiv\pi\thetaBH^2$,
i.e.\ $\kappa_B=(2-t_1)\thetaBH^2/(2\theta_B^2)$.  Then the inner
branch~\eqref{eq:bpl_alpha_in} is
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\theta)=\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta}\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_B}\right)^{2-t_1}
    \;\xrightarrow{\;t_1\to2\;}\;\frac{\thetaBH^2}{\theta}
    \qquad(\theta\le\theta_B) :
    \label{eq:bpl_pm_corner}
\end{equation}
all of the inner mass collapses onto the centre and the inner branch
becomes \emph{exactly} the point-mass deflection.  The same statement in
Meneghetti's language (Remark~5.3 of
\citet{meneghetti_introduction_2021}): the power law with 3-D slope
$n=3$ (here $t=2$) has constant enclosed mass and \emph{is} the point
mass.

There is a subtlety.  If $\theta_B$ is held fixed, $\kappa_B\to0$
takes the outer power law down with it, and the \emph{whole} lens
collapses to a point mass [check 4c].  To get a galaxy \emph{plus} a
point mass one must also hold the outer power law fixed,
$K\equiv\kappa_B\theta_B^{t_2}$ constant.  Together with the fixed
inner mass this forces
\begin{equation}
    \theta_B=\left[\frac{(2-t_1)\,\thetaBH^2}{2K}\right]^{1/(2-t_2)}
    \;\xrightarrow{\;t_1\to2\;}\;0 ,
    \label{eq:bpl_joint_limit}
\end{equation}
and in that joint limit the deflection at every fixed $\theta>0$ tends
to $2K\theta^{1-t_2}/(2-t_2)+\thetaBH^2/\theta$: an outer power law
plus a point mass [checks 4d--4e].  So the power-law-plus-point-mass
model of \S\ref{sec:pl_plpm} is a boundary point of the
broken-power-law family, reached by sending the inner slope to $2$ and
the break radius to $0$ together.

\subsection{Outside the break: a power law plus an effective point mass}
\label{sec:bpl_identity}

Compare the broken power law with the single power law of the same
outer slope $t_2$ and the same prefactor $\kappa_B\theta_B^{t_2}$, i.e.\
the outer branch continued all the way to the centre.  For
$\theta>\theta_B$ the two enclosed masses differ by a constant,
\[
  \Delta m_{2\rm D}=2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^2\left[\frac{1}{2-t_1}-\frac{1}{2-t_2}\right]
                   =2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^2\,\frac{t_1-t_2}{(2-t_1)(2-t_2)} ,
\]
and a constant enclosed mass is exactly what a point mass is.

\begin{keybox}[Outside the break, broken power law $=$ power law $+$ point mass]
For every $\theta>\theta_B$, exactly,
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{\rm bPL}(\theta)=\frac{2\kappa_B\theta_B^{t_2}}{2-t_2}\,\theta^{1-t_2}
      +\frac{\theta_{\rm eff}^2}{\theta} ,
    \qquad
    \theta_{\rm eff}^{2}=2\kappa_B\theta_B^{2}\,\frac{t_1-t_2}{(2-t_1)(2-t_2)} .
    \label{eq:bpl_identity}
\end{equation}
Consequently, by the superposition rule~\eqref{eq:pl_superposition},
outside the break $\lambda_r$ and $\lambda_t$ are those of a power law
plus a point mass $\theta_{\rm eff}$, and everything in
\S\ref{sec:pl_plpm} (the weight ratio, the stationary point, the
annihilation threshold with $\mBH\to\theta_{\rm eff}^2/\thetaE^2$)
applies unchanged to any critical curve that lies outside $\theta_B$.
\end{keybox}

This is an identity, not an approximation [checks 5a--5b, 5f--5g].
Two properties of $\theta_{\rm eff}$ are worth stating separately.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Its sign is the sign of $t_1-t_2$.}  An inner cusp
        steeper than the outer slope ($t_1>t_2$) acts as a positive
        point mass.  A shallower inner profile, a core ($t_1<t_2$),
        acts as a \emph{negative} one: a mass deficit relative to the
        continued power law [check 5c].
  \item \textbf{It is not the total inner mass.}  With
        $\pi\thetaBH^2\equiv2\pi\kappa_B\theta_B^2/(2-t_1)$ the mass
        inside the break,
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{\theta_{\rm eff}^2}{\thetaBH^2}=\frac{t_1-t_2}{2-t_2} ,
            \label{eq:bpl_eff_ratio}
        \end{equation}
        which reaches $1$ only as $t_1\to2$.  The effective point mass
        is what the inner cusp adds \emph{over and above} what the
        continued outer power law already puts there [checks 5d--5e].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Inside the break: a resolved break is necessary, not sufficient}
\label{sec:bpl_inside}

Equation~\eqref{eq:bpl_identity} settles the outside.  Inside the
break, compare the broken power law with a point mass carrying the same
total inner mass $\pi\thetaBH^2$.  From~\eqref{eq:bpl_pm_corner},
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\alpha_{\rm bPL}(\theta)}{\alpha_\bullet(\theta)}
      =\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_B}\right)^{2-t_1} ,
    \qquad\theta\le\theta_B .
    \label{eq:bpl_inner_ratio}
\end{equation}
The two agree at $\theta_B$ by construction and separate inward.  For
$t_1=2$ the ratio is $1$ at every radius; for any $t_1<2$ the
fractional difference is
\begin{equation}
    1-\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_B}\right)^{2-t_1}
      =(2-t_1)\ln\frac{\theta_B}{\theta}+\order{(2-t_1)^2} ,
    \label{eq:bpl_log_dev}
\end{equation}
linear in the slope deficit $2-t_1$ and only logarithmic in radius
[checks 6a--6d].

This has a practical meaning.  Data that never reach inside $\theta_B$
cannot tell a broken power law from a power law plus a point mass, by
eq.~\eqref{eq:bpl_identity}; resolving the break is \emph{necessary}.
But it is not \emph{sufficient}: inside the break the models part
company only logarithmically.  A fractional difference $\delta$ is
reached at
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\theta}{\theta_B}=(1-\delta)^{1/(2-t_1)} ,
    \label{eq:bpl_delta_radius}
\end{equation}
so a $10$ per cent difference appears at $0.9\,\theta_B$ for a mild
cusp ($2-t_1=1$) but only at $0.35\,\theta_B$ for a steep one
($2-t_1=0.1$) [checks 6e--6f].  The observational requirement is to
resolve the radius where the deflections differ by more than the
astrometric precision, which can be much smaller than $\theta_B$
itself.

\begin{annotation}[Two different reasons to be indistinguishable]
A broken power law and a power law plus point mass can look the same
for two unrelated reasons.  (i) \emph{Resolution:} no datum lies
inside $\theta_B$, so~\eqref{eq:bpl_identity} makes the two models
identical where they are tested; sharper imaging fixes this.
(ii) \emph{Degeneracy:} $t_1\to2$, so~\eqref{eq:bpl_inner_ratio} is $1$
at every radius; no telescope fixes this, because the models are then
the same lens.  Eq.~\eqref{eq:bpl_log_dev} sits between the two: even
when the break is resolved, a steep cusp ($2-t_1$ small) needs a large
logarithmic lever arm, $\ln(\theta_B/\theta)\gtrsim\delta/(2-t_1)$,
before the difference becomes visible.  [Numbers: AN6 of
\texttt{problems\_15.wl}.]
\end{annotation}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:pl_summary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item For any axisymmetric lens, $\lambda_t=1-\bar\kappa$ and
          $\lambda_r=1+\bar\kappa-2\kappab$; the departures from unity
          add under superposition.  A central point mass adds
          $\mp\thetaBH^2/\theta^2$ to $\lambda_{t,r}$
          (eqs.~\ref{eq:pl_recipe_lt}--\ref{eq:pl_recipe_lr}).

    \item The power law $\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$ has
          $\alpha=\thetaE^{\gp-1}\theta^{2-\gp}$,
          $\kappab=\tfrac{3-\gp}{2}(\thetaE/\theta)^{\gp-1}$, and
          $\kappab(\thetaE)=(3-\gp)/2$.  Its convergence slope is
          $t=\gp-1$; keep the conventions apart.

    \item Its tangential critical curve is the Einstein ring; its
          radial critical curve,
          $\theta_r=\thetaE(2-\gp)^{1/(\gp-1)}$, exists only for
          $1<\gp<2$ and shrinks to the centre as $\gp\to2$.

    \item A point mass has $\lambda_r=1+\thetaBH^2/\theta^2>0$ and no
          radial critical curve.  Added to a power law, its influence
          relative to the profile grows inward as $\theta^{-(3-\gp)}$,
          so it acts precisely where radial images form.

    \item Below $\mBH_{\rm crit}(\gp)$ (eq.~\ref{eq:pl_mcrit}) the
          composite has two radial critical curves; at threshold they
          merge and annihilate.  $\mBH_{\rm crit}\le0.0066$ for every
          $\gp$; at $\gp=1.5$ it is $27/4096$.  Below threshold the
          inner caustic is a zone of influence of radius
          $\propto\mBH^{(2-\gp)/(3-\gp)}$ inside which the radial image
          is suppressed.

    \item The SIS plus point mass solves in closed form and has exactly
          two images for every source position, and no radial image.

    \item The broken power law has closed-form deflections; the point
          mass is its $t_1\to2$ corner at fixed inner mass; and outside
          the break it is \emph{exactly} a power law plus an effective
          point mass, $\theta_{\rm eff}^2\propto(t_1-t_2)$.  Inside the
          break the two separate only logarithmically, so resolving the
          break is necessary but not sufficient to tell a cusp from a
          point mass.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problems}
\label{sec:pl_problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pl_kappa_inversion}
\textbf{(Power-law convergence by inversion.)}
Starting from $\alpha_{\rm PL}=\thetaE^{\gp-1}\theta^{2-\gp}$, use
eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa_inversion} to derive
eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_kappa}.  Then rebuild $\alpha$ from $\kappab$ with
eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_alpha_kbar}, state where the integral converges, and
verify $\bar\kappa(\thetaE)=1$ for every $\gp$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pl_point_mass_mag}
\textbf{(Point-mass magnification.)}
Show that a point mass has magnification
$\mu=\theta^4/(\theta^4-\thetaBH^4)$ at image position $\theta$.
Show that its two images, at $\theta_\pm$, have total magnification
$|\mu_+|+|\mu_-|=(u^2+2)/(u\sqrt{u^2+4})$ with $u=\ysrc/\thetaBH$, that
this always exceeds $1$, and that the signed magnifications sum to
exactly $1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pl_radial_radius}
\textbf{(Radial critical radius.)}
Derive $\theta_r=\thetaE(2-\gp)^{1/(\gp-1)}$ from $\lambda_r=0$.
Show that $\theta_r\to0$ as $\gp\to2$ and that there is no radial
critical curve for $\gp>2$.  Evaluate $\theta_r/\thetaE$ at
$\gp=1.6$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pl_sis_pm_shift}
\textbf{(SIS plus point mass: first-order shifts.)}
Expand the closed-form images~\eqref{eq:pl_sis_pm_plus}
and~\eqref{eq:pl_sis_pm_minus} to first order in
$\mBH=\thetaBH^2/\thetaE^2$ and show that both images move away from
the centre by $\delta\theta_\pm=\thetaBH^2/\theta_\pm^{(0)}$, with
$\theta_\pm^{(0)}=\ysrc\pm\thetaE$.  Evaluate exact and linearized
positions for $\thetaE=1$, $\thetaBH=0.08$, $\ysrc=0.3$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pl_tangential_shift}
\textbf{(Tangential critical curve with a point mass.)}
For a power law plus a point mass, show that the tangential critical
curve lies at $\theta_t\simeq\thetaE[1+\mBH/(\gp-1)]$ to first order in
$\mBH$, eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_theta_t_shift}.  Find the exact result for the
SIS and compare.  Evaluate $\theta_t$ numerically for $\gp=1.5$,
$\mBH=0.01$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pl_annihilation_15}
\textbf{(Annihilation at $\gp=3/2$ by hand.)}
For $\gp=3/2$ substitute $u=s^2$ in eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_plpm_lambdas} and
show that $u^2\lambda_r=s^4-\tfrac12 s^3+\mBH$.  A double root marks
the merger of the two radial critical curves; use this to derive
$u_c=9/64$ and $\mBH_{\rm crit}=27/4096$ without the general
formula~\eqref{eq:pl_mcrit}.  Find the radius of the merged radial
caustic.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:bpl_outside}
\textbf{(Broken power law outside the break.)}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Compute the projected mass inside $\theta_B$ and in the annulus
        $\theta_B<\theta'<\theta$ for the profile~\eqref{eq:bpl_kappa},
        and hence derive eq.~\eqref{eq:bpl_alpha_out}.
  \item Show that it is continuous with eq.~\eqref{eq:bpl_alpha_in} at
        $\theta_B$.
  \item Rewrite it as eq.~\eqref{eq:bpl_identity} and identify
        $\theta_{\rm eff}^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:bpl_point_mass_limit}
\textbf{(The point-mass limit.)}
Hold the projected mass inside the break fixed at $\pi\thetaBH^2$.
Show that the inner branch becomes
$\alpha=(\thetaBH^2/\theta)(\theta/\theta_B)^{2-t_1}$ and tends to the
point-mass deflection as $t_1\to2$, even though the central convergence
becomes more singular.  What happens to $\kappa_B$, and to
$\theta_{\rm eff}^2$, in the same limit?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:bpl_sis_limit}
\textbf{(Isothermal limits.)}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Show that $t_1=t_2=1$ gives a constant deflection on both
        branches: a SIS with $\thetaE=2\kappa_B\theta_B$.
  \item For $t_2=1$ and $1<t_1<2$, show that outside the break the
        lens is \emph{exactly} a SIS plus a point mass, and use
        eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_sis_pm_plus} to write the outer image of a
        source at $\ysrc$ in closed form.  Evaluate it for
        $\theta_B=0.1$, $\kappa_B=5$, $t_1=3/2$, $\ysrc=0.3$, and
        confirm the image lies outside the break.
  \item What does $t_1<1$ correspond to?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
