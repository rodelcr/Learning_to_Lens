\noindent
In Module~1d we derived the effective potential for geodesic motion in
Schwarzschild spacetime, and in Module~1e we obtained the deflection
angle $\alphahat = 4\Gnewton M / (\speedoflight^2 b)$ via the
weak-field / Born approximation, showing how the factor of 2 over
Newtonian gravity arises from the equal contributions of temporal and
spatial curvature.  Now we derive this same result rigorously from the
\textit{exact} null geodesic orbit equation in the full Schwarzschild
metric.  This is

lens equation, every magnification formula, and every time delay expression
ultimately rests on it.

We also derive the Shapiro time delay, which provides the second
observable consequence of light propagation through a gravitational field,
and compute the deflection for several astrophysically relevant systems:
the Sun (Eddington's 1919 test), Jupiter, and a typical galaxy lens.

\section{Introduction: Null Geodesics and the Deflection Problem}
\label{sec:deflection_intro}

Light follows \textbf{null geodesics} in curved spacetime: paths for which
$ds^2 = 0$ at every point.  In Module~1d we showed that for a photon in
the equatorial plane of the Schwarzschild metric, the conserved energy~$E$
and angular momentum~$L$ reduce the equations of motion to a single radial
equation (cf.\ eqs.~\ref{eq:conserved_E}--\ref{eq:conserved_L} and the
null geodesic orbit equation~\eqref{eq:photon_orbit} from Module~1d):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\phi}{dr} = \pm \frac{L}{r^2}
    \left[\frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2}
    - \frac{L^2}{r^2}\!\left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\right)
    \right]^{-1/2} ,
    \label{eq:photon_orbit_eq}
\end{equation}
where $\RS = 2\Gnewton M / \speedoflight^2$ is the Schwarzschild radius
(Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.87).


\begin{derivation}[Where the orbit equation comes from]
Module~1d works in units with $\speedoflight = 1$.  Restoring $\speedoflight$,
the two Killing constants of a photon with affine parameter~$\lambda$ are
\[
    E = \left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\right)\speedoflight^2\,\dot t , \qquad
    L = r^2\,\dot\phi , \qquad (\dot{} \equiv d/d\lambda),
\]
and the null condition $g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu \dot x^\nu = 0$ in the equatorial
plane reads
\[
    -\left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\right)\speedoflight^2\,\dot t^{\,2}
    + \left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\right)^{-1}\dot r^{\,2} + r^2\dot\phi^{\,2} = 0 .
\]
\textit{Step 1 (eliminate $\dot t$, $\dot\phi$).}  Substitute
$\dot t = E/[\speedoflight^2(1-\RS/r)]$ and $\dot\phi = L/r^2$ and multiply
through by $(1-\RS/r)$:
\[
    \dot r^{\,2} = \frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2}
    - \frac{L^2}{r^2}\left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\right).
\]
\textit{Step 2 (chain rule).}  $d\phi/dr = \dot\phi/\dot r = (L/r^2)/\dot r$,
which is eq.~\eqref{eq:photon_orbit_eq}; the $\pm$ is the sign of $\dot r$
(incoming or outgoing leg).  The same chain rule applied to $t$ gives
eq.~\eqref{eq:dt_dr} below: $dt/dr = \dot t/\dot r$.
(Checked symbolically in \texttt{problems\_02.wl}, check A1.)
\end{derivation}
The physical setup is as follows.  A photon approaches a mass~$M$ from
infinity, reaches a distance of closest approach~$r_0$, and then escapes
back to infinity.  The total change in the azimuthal angle~$\phi$ is
$\Delta\phi$; in flat spacetime this would be~$\pi$ (a straight line), so
the \textbf{deflection angle} is
\begin{equation}
    \alphahat = \Delta\phi - \pi \,.
    \label{eq:deflection_def}
\end{equation}
Our goal in this chapter is to evaluate $\Delta\phi$ and show that, in the
weak-field limit,
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \alphahat = \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2\, b}
    }
    \label{eq:alphahat_main}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the impact parameter.  This is the fundamental result of
gravitational lensing.


\begin{annotation}[Units, size, and which angle this is]
\textit{Dimensions.}  $\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2$ is a length, so
$\alphahat = 4\Gnewton M/(\speedoflight^2 b) = 2\RS/b$ is a pure number (radians).
This is the fastest way to evaluate it: the Sun has $\RS \approx 2.95$~km, a
$10^{12}\,\Msun$ galaxy has $\RS \approx 2.95\times10^{15}$~m, and the deflection
is twice the Schwarzschild radius divided by the impact parameter.

\textit{Size.}  For a galaxy at $b = 10$~kpc the expansion parameter is
$m/b = \Gnewton M/(\speedoflight^2 b) \approx 4.8\times10^{-6}$, so every
correction of relative order $m/b$ dropped in this chapter is negligible
for galaxy and cluster lensing.  (Numbers: checks A12a--d.)

\textit{Which angle.}  $\alphahat$ is the \emph{physical} bending angle at the
lens: the angle between the incoming and outgoing asymptotes of one ray.  It is
\emph{not} the angular offset you see on the sky.  The lens equation of Module~4
uses the \emph{reduced} deflection
$\alpha = (\Dds/\Ds)\,\alphahat$ (eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced_deflection}), and that
distance factor is where all of the cosmology of Module~3 enters.
\end{annotation}

\section{Soldner's Newtonian Calculation (1801)}
\label{sec:soldner}

Before Einstein, Johann Georg von Soldner (1801) computed the deflection
of light using Newtonian gravity, treating a photon as a corpuscle of
mass~$m$ traveling at speed~$\speedoflight$.

\begin{remark}
Soldner's calculation predates general relativity by over a century.
It is both historically important and pedagogically useful: it gives
exactly \textit{half} the correct GR answer, and understanding
\textit{why} illuminates the role of spatial curvature.
\end{remark}

Consider a particle approaching a mass~$M$ on a hyperbolic trajectory with
velocity~$v_\infty = \speedoflight$ at infinity.  The Newtonian equations
of motion in polar coordinates yield the orbit equation
\begin{equation}
    r(\phi) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\phi} \,,
    \label{eq:newtonian_orbit}
\end{equation}
where $p = L^2/(GM)$ and $e = \sqrt{1 + 2\mathcal{E}L^2/(G^2 M^2)}$ is the
eccentricity (with $\mathcal{E} = \half \speedoflight^2$ for an unbound
particle at speed~$\speedoflight$).

The asymptotic directions correspond to $r \to \infty$, i.e.,
$\cos\phi = -1/e$.  The total angle swept is $\Delta\phi = \pi + 2\delta$,
where $\sin\delta = 1/e$.  For large impact parameter ($b \gg \RS$), the
eccentricity is large and $\delta \approx 1/e \ll 1$, giving
\begin{equation}
    \alphahat_{\mathrm{Newton}} = 2\delta \approx \frac{2}{e}
    = \frac{2\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2\, b} \,.
    \label{eq:soldner_deflection}
\end{equation}


\begin{derivation}[Soldner's orbit, step by step]
\textit{Step 1 (Binet equation).}  For a central force per unit mass
$-\Gnewton M/r^2$ with specific angular momentum $L = r^2\dot\phi$, the variable
$u = 1/r$ obeys $u'' + u = \Gnewton M/L^2$ (primes are $d/d\phi$).  Its solution
with periapse at $\phi = 0$ is
\[
    u(\phi) = \frac{\Gnewton M}{L^2}\,(1 + e\cos\phi)
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    r(\phi) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\phi}, \quad p = \frac{L^2}{\Gnewton M}.
\]
\textit{Step 2 (energy fixes $e$).}  With $\dot r = -L\,u'$, the specific
energy is $\mathcal{E} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\phi^2) - \Gnewton M u
= \tfrac{L^2}{2}(u'^2 + u^2) - \Gnewton M u$.  Inserting $u(\phi)$, every
$\phi$-dependence cancels and
$\mathcal{E} = (\Gnewton M)^2(e^2-1)/(2L^2)$, i.e.
$e^2 = 1 + 2\mathcal{E}L^2/(\Gnewton M)^2$.  For the corpuscle,
$L = b\speedoflight$ (speed times lever arm at infinity) and
$\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12\speedoflight^2$, so
$e^2 = 1 + b^2\speedoflight^4/(\Gnewton M)^2$.

\textit{Step 3 (asymptotes).}  $r \to \infty$ when $\cos\phi = -1/e$, i.e.\ at
$\phi_\infty = \pm\arccos(-1/e) = \pm(\pi/2 + \delta)$ with $\sin\delta = 1/e$.
The swept angle between the incoming and outgoing asymptotes is
$2\arccos(-1/e) = \pi + 2\delta$, and the deflection is the excess over~$\pi$,
$\alphahat_{\rm Newton} = 2\delta$.

\textit{Step 4 (expand).}  For $e \gg 1$, $\delta = \arcsin(1/e) \approx 1/e
\approx \Gnewton M/(b\speedoflight^2)$, giving
eq.~\eqref{eq:soldner_deflection}.
(Checks A16a--c.)
\end{derivation}
\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:soldner}
Derive eq.~\eqref{eq:soldner_deflection} in detail.  Start from the
Newtonian orbit equation for a massless particle with $v_\infty =
\speedoflight$ and impact parameter~$b$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Show that the semi-latus rectum is $p = b^2 \speedoflight^2 /
          (\Gnewton M)$ and the eccentricity is
          $e = \sqrt{1 + b^2 \speedoflight^4 / (\Gnewton M)^2}$.
    \item For $b \gg \Gnewton M / \speedoflight^2$, show that
          $\alphahat_{\mathrm{Newton}} = 2\Gnewton M /
          (\speedoflight^2 b)$.
\end{enumerate}
\textit{Hint:} The angular momentum is $L = b\speedoflight$ and the energy
is $\mathcal{E} = \half \speedoflight^2$.
\end{exercise}

\section{The GR Deflection Angle --- Full Derivation}
\label{sec:gr_deflection}

We now follow Congdon \& Keeton Sec.~3.4.1 (eqs.~3.87--3.96) to derive
the deflection angle in general relativity.  The result will be
$\alphahat = 4\Gnewton M / (\speedoflight^2 b)$ --- exactly \textbf{twice}
the Newtonian prediction.

\subsection{The Impact Parameter}
\label{sec:impact_parameter}

The \textbf{impact parameter} $b$ is defined as the perpendicular distance
between the undeflected ray (the asymptotic straight line at $r \to \infty$)
and the mass~$M$.  From the conserved quantities, it is related to $E$ and
$L$ by (Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.90):
\begin{equation}
    b = \frac{\speedoflight\, L}{E} \,.
    \label{eq:impact_parameter}
\end{equation}


\begin{derivation}[Why $b = \speedoflight L/E$]
Far from the mass the ray is a straight line at perpendicular distance~$b$ from
the origin: $r\sin\phi = b$, or $u = 1/r = \sin\phi/b$.  Along that line
$d\phi/dr \to b/r^2$ as $r \to \infty$ (because $\sin\phi \to 0$ and
$\cos\phi \to \pm1$).  The orbit equation~\eqref{eq:photon_orbit_eq} gives,
for $r \to \infty$ where the bracket tends to $E^2/\speedoflight^2$,
\[
    r^2\,\frac{d\phi}{dr} \;\longrightarrow\;
    L\left(\frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2}\right)^{-1/2} = \frac{\speedoflight L}{E}.
\]
Matching the two asymptotic forms gives $b = \speedoflight L/E$.  Note that $b$
is defined entirely at infinity; it knows nothing about how close the ray
actually gets.  (Check A2.)
\end{derivation}
\subsection{Distance of Closest Approach}
\label{sec:closest_approach}

At the turning point $r = r_0$, the radial velocity vanishes:
$dr/d\lambda = 0$.  From the radial equation of motion, this gives
(Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.89):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2} - \frac{L^2}{r_0^2}
    \left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r_0}\right) = 0 \,.
    \label{eq:turning_point}
\end{equation}
Substituting $b = \speedoflight L / E$ and defining $m \equiv \Gnewton M /
\speedoflight^2 = \RS/2$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \frac{r_0^2}{b^2} = 1 - \frac{2m}{r_0} \,.
    \label{eq:r0_implicit}
\end{equation}
In the weak-field limit $m/r_0 \ll 1$, we can solve perturbatively:
$r_0 \approx b\left(1 - m/b + \cdots\right)$, so to leading order
$r_0 \approx b$.


\begin{annotation}[Trap: impact parameter $b$ versus closest approach $r_0$]
These are two different lengths.  $b$ is the perpendicular offset of the
\emph{asymptote} (a property of the ray at infinity); $r_0$ is the turning point
of the \emph{actual} curved ray.  Gravity pulls the ray in, so $r_0 < b$.
Solving eq.~\eqref{eq:r0_implicit} order by order with the ansatz
$r_0 = b + c_1 m + c_2 m^2/b$ (insert into $r_0^3 = b^2(r_0 - 2m)$ and set the
$m^1$ and $m^2$ coefficients to zero) gives
\[
    r_0 = b - m - \frac{3}{2}\,\frac{m^2}{b} + \order{m^3/b^2}
    \qquad (\text{check A3}).
\]
At first order in $m/b$ it does not matter which one you put in the
denominator of $\alphahat$, because the difference is $\order{m^2/b^2}$.  At
second order it does: the $m$ shift in $r_0$ converts the $\varepsilon = m/r_0$
coefficient into the $m/b$ coefficient (see the annotation after
eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_second_order}).

In the thin-lens formalism of Module~4 the ``impact parameter'' is the
lens-plane position $\xi = \Dd\,\theta$ at which the ray crosses the lens plane;
for galaxy lenses $m/b \sim 10^{-6}$, so $b$, $r_0$ and $\xi$ are
interchangeable there.
\end{annotation}
\subsection{The Total Azimuthal Change}
\label{sec:azimuthal_integral}

By symmetry, the total angle swept from $r = \infty$ (incoming) to
$r = \infty$ (outgoing) is twice the angle from $r_0$ to infinity:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\phi = 2 \int_{r_0}^{\infty}
    \frac{L}{r^2}
    \left[\frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2}
    - \frac{L^2}{r^2}\!\left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\right)
    \right]^{-1/2} dr \,.
    \label{eq:delta_phi_integral}
\end{equation}
Substituting $b = \speedoflight L/E$ and simplifying
(Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.92):
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\phi = 2 \int_{r_0}^{\infty}
    \frac{dr}{r^2}
    \left[\frac{1}{b^2}
    - \frac{1}{r^2}\!\left(1 - \frac{2m}{r}\right)
    \right]^{-1/2} .
    \label{eq:delta_phi_simplified}
\end{equation}


\begin{derivation}[Pulling $L$ through the square root]
Write the integrand of eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_phi_integral} as
\[
    \frac{L}{r^2}\left[\frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2}
    - \frac{L^2}{r^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\Bigr)\right]^{-1/2}
    = \frac{1}{r^2}\left[\frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2L^2}
    - \frac{1}{r^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\Bigr)\right]^{-1/2},
\]
moving $L$ inside as $L = (L^2)^{1/2}$ (all quantities positive).  Then
$E^2/(\speedoflight^2L^2) = 1/b^2$ by eq.~\eqref{eq:impact_parameter}, and
$\RS = 2m$.  The lower limit $r_0$ is where the bracket vanishes,
eq.~\eqref{eq:turning_point}.  (Check A4.)
\end{derivation}
\subsection{Change of Variables}
\label{sec:substitution}

Following Congdon \& Keeton, we introduce the substitution $u = r_0 / r$
(so $u = 0$ at $r = \infty$ and $u = 1$ at $r = r_0$):
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\phi = 2 \int_0^1
    \frac{du}{\sqrt{\displaystyle
    \frac{r_0^2}{b^2} - u^2 + 2m\,\frac{u^3}{r_0}}} \,.
    \label{eq:delta_phi_u}
\end{equation}
Using eq.~\eqref{eq:r0_implicit} to replace $r_0^2/b^2 = 1 - 2m/r_0$
(Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.93):
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\phi = 2 \int_0^1
    \frac{du}{\sqrt{\displaystyle
    (1 - u^2) - \frac{2m}{r_0}(1 - u^3)}} \,.
    \label{eq:delta_phi_u2}
\end{equation}


\begin{derivation}[The substitution $u = r_0/r$ in full]
\textit{Step 1 (Jacobian).}  $r = r_0/u$, so $dr = -(r_0/u^2)\,du$ and
$1/r^2 = u^2/r_0^2$.  Hence $dr/r^2 = -du/r_0$; the minus sign is absorbed by
swapping the limits ($r: r_0 \to \infty$ becomes $u: 1 \to 0$).

\textit{Step 2 (bracket).}  $1/b^2 - (u^2/r_0^2)(1 - 2mu/r_0)$.  Pulling
$1/r_0^2$ out of the bracket turns its $-1/2$ power into a factor $r_0$, which
cancels the $1/r_0$ from Step~1:
\[
    \Delta\phi = 2\int_0^1 \frac{du}{\sqrt{r_0^2/b^2 - u^2 + 2m\,u^3/r_0}} .
\]
\textit{Step 3 (use the turning point).}  Replace $r_0^2/b^2 = 1 - 2m/r_0$:
$1 - 2m/r_0 - u^2 + 2m u^3/r_0 = (1-u^2) - (2m/r_0)(1-u^3)$, which is
eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_phi_u2}.  The point of the substitution is that the
integration range is now fixed, $[0,1]$, and the whole $m$-dependence sits in one
small parameter $\varepsilon = m/r_0$.  (Check A5.)
\end{derivation}
\subsection{Weak-Field Expansion}
\label{sec:weak_field_expansion_deflection}

We define the small parameter $\varepsilon \equiv m/r_0 \ll 1$ and expand
the integrand to first order in~$\varepsilon$.  Writing
$f(u) = (1-u^2) - 2\varepsilon(1 - u^3)$, we use
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} \approx
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}}
    + \frac{\varepsilon(1 - u^3)}{(1 - u^2)^{3/2}}
    + \order{\varepsilon^2} \,.
    \label{eq:integrand_expansion}
\end{equation}
Therefore (Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.94):
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\phi \approx 2\int_0^1 \frac{du}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}}
    + 2\varepsilon \int_0^1 \frac{1 - u^3}{(1-u^2)^{3/2}}\, du
    + \order{\varepsilon^2} \,.
    \label{eq:delta_phi_expanded}
\end{equation}


\begin{derivation}[The first-order expansion, and why it is safe]
Write $f = A - 2\varepsilon B$ with $A = 1-u^2$, $B = 1-u^3$.  Then
\[
    f^{-1/2} = A^{-1/2}\Bigl(1 - 2\varepsilon\frac{B}{A}\Bigr)^{-1/2}
    = A^{-1/2}\Bigl(1 + \varepsilon\frac{B}{A} + \tfrac{3}{2}\varepsilon^2\frac{B^2}{A^2}
      + \cdots\Bigr),
\]
using $(1-x)^{-1/2} = 1 + x/2 + 3x^2/8 + \cdots$ with $x = 2\varepsilon B/A$.
The first-order term is $\varepsilon B/A^{3/2}$, eq.~\eqref{eq:integrand_expansion}.
(Checks A6, A9b.)

\textit{Is the expansion uniform?}  Both $A$ and $B$ vanish at the turning point
$u = 1$, so one might worry that $x = 2\varepsilon B/A$ is not small there.  It
is: $B/A = (1+u+u^2)/(1+u) \to 3/2$ as $u \to 1$ (check A6b), so
$x \le 3\varepsilon$ on the whole interval.  The remaining singularity is the
integrable $(1-u)^{-1/2}$ of the zeroth-order term, inherited by every order.
\end{derivation}
\subsection{Evaluating the Integrals}
\label{sec:integral_evaluation}

\noindent\textbf{Zeroth-order integral:}  With the substitution
$u = \sin\theta$:
\begin{equation}
    I_0 = 2\int_0^1 \frac{du}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}}
    = 2\left[\arcsin u\right]_0^1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \pi \,.
    \label{eq:I0}
\end{equation}
This is the flat-spacetime result: no deflection.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{First-order integral:}  We need
(the $m/r_0$ term of Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.94):
\begin{equation}
    I_1 = 2\int_0^1 \frac{1 - u^3}{(1 - u^2)^{3/2}}\, du \,.
    \label{eq:I1_def}
\end{equation}
Factor the numerator: $1 - u^3 = (1 - u)(1 + u + u^2)$ and
$1 - u^2 = (1-u)(1+u)$, so
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1 - u^3}{(1-u^2)^{3/2}}
    = \frac{(1-u)(1 + u + u^2)}{(1-u)^{3/2}(1+u)^{3/2}}
    = \frac{1 + u + u^2}{(1-u)^{1/2}(1+u)^{3/2}} \,.
    \label{eq:integrand_factored}
\end{equation}
The bare integral $\int_0^1 (1-u^3)(1-u^2)^{-3/2}\, du$ evaluates
to~$2$ (as verified in Mathematica --- see
\mathlink{02_Light_Deflection/deflection_angle.wl}{deflection\_angle.wl}),
so
\begin{equation}
    I_1 = 2 \times 2 = 4 \,.
    \label{eq:I1_result}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
The evaluation of $\int_0^1 (1-u^3)(1-u^2)^{-3/2}\, du = 2$ is a good
exercise in integration techniques.  One approach: split
$1 + u + u^2 = (1 + u)^2 - u$ and evaluate each piece separately via
trigonometric/hyperbolic substitution.  The Mathematica script verifies
this symbolically.
\end{remark}


\begin{derivation}[The bare integral $=2$ by hand]
Substitute $u = \sin\vartheta$, $du = \cos\vartheta\,d\vartheta$,
$(1-u^2)^{3/2} = \cos^3\vartheta$, $\vartheta \in [0,\pi/2]$:
\[
    \int_0^1 \frac{1-u^3}{(1-u^2)^{3/2}}\,du
    = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{1 - \sin^3\vartheta}{\cos^2\vartheta}\,d\vartheta .
\]
Split the numerator with $\sin^3\vartheta = \sin\vartheta(1-\cos^2\vartheta)$:
\[
    \frac{1-\sin^3\vartheta}{\cos^2\vartheta}
    = \sec^2\vartheta - \frac{\sin\vartheta}{\cos^2\vartheta} + \sin\vartheta .
\]
Each piece has an elementary antiderivative ($\tan$, $\sec$ since
$(\sec\vartheta)' = \sin\vartheta/\cos^2\vartheta$, and $-\cos$):
\[
    F(\vartheta) = \tan\vartheta - \sec\vartheta - \cos\vartheta
    = \frac{\sin\vartheta - 1}{\cos\vartheta} - \cos\vartheta .
\]
At $\vartheta \to \pi/2$ the first fraction is $0/0$ but tends to $0$ (its
numerator vanishes quadratically: $1-\sin\vartheta \approx \cos^2\vartheta/2$),
and $\cos\vartheta \to 0$; so $F(\pi/2^-) = 0$.  At $\vartheta = 0$,
$F = 0 - 1 - 1 = -2$.  The integral is $0 - (-2) = 2$, and $I_1 = 4$.
(Checks A7a--d.)
\end{derivation}
\subsection{The Deflection Angle}
\label{sec:deflection_result}

Assembling the result (Congdon \& Keeton eqs.~3.95--3.96): using
$\Delta\phi = I_0 + \varepsilon\, I_1 + \order{\varepsilon^2}$,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\phi = \pi + 4\varepsilon + \order{\varepsilon^2}
    = \pi + \frac{4m}{r_0} + \order{m/r_0}^2 \,.
    \label{eq:delta_phi_result}
\end{equation}
Since $r_0 \approx b$ to leading order and $m = \Gnewton M / \speedoflight^2$:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \alphahat = \Delta\phi - \pi
    = \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2\, b}
    + \order{\Gnewton M / (\speedoflight^2 b)}^2
    }
    \label{eq:alphahat_result}
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{Einstein deflection angle} for a point mass in the
weak-field limit.


\begin{derivation}[Same answer, the lensing way: integrate along the unperturbed ray]
Module~1e (eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_integral}) gives the weak-field deflection as
the transverse gradient of the Newtonian potential integrated along the
\emph{undeflected} straight line (the Born approximation):
$\alphahat = (2/\speedoflight^2)\int |\nabla_\perp\Phi|\,dl$.  For a point mass
the straight path at impact parameter~$b$ has $r = \sqrt{b^2 + l^2}$ and
$\Phi = -\Gnewton M/r$, so
$|\nabla_\perp \Phi| = |\partial_b \Phi| = \Gnewton M b/(b^2+l^2)^{3/2}$.  With
$l = b\tan\psi$, $dl = b\sec^2\psi\,d\psi$:
\[
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\Gnewton M b\,dl}{(b^2+l^2)^{3/2}}
    = \frac{\Gnewton M}{b}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\psi\,d\psi
    = \frac{2\Gnewton M}{b},
    \qquad
    \alphahat = \frac{2}{\speedoflight^2}\cdot\frac{2\Gnewton M}{b}
    = \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2 b}.
\]
(Check A8.)  Two lessons.  (i) The exact-orbit route of this chapter and the
straight-line route agree at first order; the straight-line route is what every
lensing code uses.  (ii) The straight-line integral is \emph{linear} in~$\Phi$,
hence linear in the mass: the deflection of a sum of masses is the sum of the
deflections.  That linearity is what turns $\alphahat$ for one point into
$\alphahat(\vecxi) = (4\Gnewton/\speedoflight^2)\int \Sigma(\vecxi')
(\vecxi-\vecxi')/|\vecxi-\vecxi'|^2\,d^2\xi'$
(eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_integral_continuous}).
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Why replacing $r_0$ by $b$ is allowed here]
Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_phi_result} is written with $\varepsilon = m/r_0$, while the
boxed result uses $m/b$.  Since $r_0 = b - m + \cdots$, the swap changes
$4m/r_0$ by $4m^2/b^2$, which is of the same order as the terms already
dropped.  The swap is consistent at first order and \emph{only} at first order;
at second order it contributes, as shown after
eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_second_order}.
\end{annotation}

\begin{bridge}[Point-mass deflection inside a galaxy]
Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass} puts a central point mass (a
supermassive black hole) inside a power-law galaxy.  Because the weak-field
deflection is linear in the mass (previous box), the black hole simply adds its
own $\alphahat_\bullet = 4\Gnewton M_\bullet/(\speedoflight^2\xi)$ to the galaxy's
deflection at the same lens-plane position~$\xi$.  After the
$\Dds/\Ds$ rescaling of Module~4 this becomes the familiar
$\alpha_\bullet(\theta) = \thetaBH^2/\theta$, with
$\thetaBH^2 = (4\Gnewton M_\bullet/\speedoflight^2)\,\Dds/(\Dd\Ds)$.  The
$1/\xi$ divergence at the centre is the reason the point mass punctures the
otherwise smooth deflection field.
\end{bridge}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:gr_vs_newton}
Show that the GR deflection angle~\eqref{eq:alphahat_result} is exactly
twice the Newtonian prediction~\eqref{eq:soldner_deflection}.  Explain
physically why:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item The Newtonian calculation accounts for the deflection due to the
          gravitational potential (temporal curvature of the metric,
          $g_{tt}$), but ignores spatial curvature ($g_{rr}$).
    \item In GR, both $g_{tt}$ and $g_{rr}$ deviate from flat space by
          the same amount (to leading order in $m/r$), so the spatial
          curvature doubles the deflection.
\end{enumerate}
\textit{Hint:} Expand the Schwarzschild line element in isotropic
coordinates and identify the contributions from the time--time and
space--space components of the metric.
\end{exercise}


\begin{annotation}[The factor of 2 as an index of refraction]
In isotropic coordinates the weak-field metric is
$ds^2 \approx -(1 - 2m/r)\speedoflight^2dt^2 + (1 + 2m/r)\,d\mathbf{x}^2$.
Setting $ds^2 = 0$, the coordinate speed of light is
$|d\mathbf{x}/dt| = \speedoflight\sqrt{(1-2m/r)/(1+2m/r)}$, so the effective
index (Module~1e, eq.~\eqref{eq:refractive_index}) is
\[
    n = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 2m/r}{1 - 2m/r}} = 1 + \frac{2m}{r} + \order{m^2/r^2}
    \qquad (\text{check A15a}).
\]
Half of the $2m/r$ comes from $g_{tt}$ and half from $g_{xx}$.  Ray optics
bends a ray by $\int \nabla_\perp n\, dl$; for $n - 1 = 2m/r$ along the
straight path this is $4m/b$, and for the ``$g_{tt}$ only'' index
$n - 1 = m/r$ it is $2m/b$, Soldner's value (checks A15b, A15c).  Keep the
square root: dropping it doubles the index and would double both numbers.
\end{annotation}

\section{The Shapiro Time Delay}
\label{sec:shapiro_delay}

In addition to deflecting light, a gravitational field \textit{slows it
down} --- or more precisely, causes an excess travel time relative to the
flat-spacetime path.  This effect was predicted by Irwin Shapiro in 1964
and first measured using radar echoes from Mercury and Venus.

Following Congdon \& Keeton Sec.~3.4.2 (eqs.~3.97--3.110), we derive the
time delay for a photon passing near a point mass.

\subsection{Coordinate Travel Time}
\label{sec:coordinate_travel_time}

For a photon on a null geodesic, we can write $dt/dr$ using the
conserved quantities (Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.97):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dt}{dr} = \frac{E / \speedoflight^2}{(1 - \RS/r)}
    \left[\frac{E^2}{\speedoflight^2}
    - \frac{L^2}{r^2}\left(1 - \frac{\RS}{r}\right)
    \right]^{-1/2} .
    \label{eq:dt_dr}
\end{equation}
The coordinate time for the photon to travel from closest approach $r_0$ to
a radial distance~$R$ is (Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.97):
\begin{equation}
    T(R) = \int_{r_0}^{R} \frac{dt}{dr}\, dr \,.
    \label{eq:travel_time}
\end{equation}
In the weak-field limit ($m/r \ll 1$), expanding to first order gives
(Congdon \& Keeton eq.~3.100, second line; its expansion in $r_0/R$ is their eq.~3.102):
\begin{equation}
    T(R) \approx \frac{1}{\speedoflight}\sqrt{R^2 - r_0^2}
    + \frac{2m}{\speedoflight}\ln\!\left(
    \frac{R + \sqrt{R^2 - r_0^2}}{r_0}\right)
    + \frac{m}{\speedoflight}\sqrt{\frac{R - r_0}{R + r_0}} \,.
    \label{eq:travel_time_expanded}
\end{equation}


\begin{derivation}[From $dt/dr$ to eq.~\eqref{eq:travel_time_expanded}]
\textit{Step 1 (exact integrand).}  $dt/dr = \dot t/\dot r$ with
$\dot t = E/[\speedoflight^2(1-2m/r)]$ and $\dot r$ from the orbit derivation
gives eq.~\eqref{eq:dt_dr}.  Eliminate $E$ and $L$ with the turning-point
condition $E^2/\speedoflight^2 = (L^2/r_0^2)(1-2m/r_0)$:
\[
    \speedoflight\,\frac{dt}{dr} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2m}{r}\Bigr)^{-1}
    \left[1 - \frac{r_0^2}{r^2}\,\frac{1-2m/r}{1-2m/r_0}\right]^{-1/2}.
\]
\textit{Step 2 (expand to first order in $m$).}  Expanding the bracket and the
prefactor separately and collecting,
\[
    \speedoflight\,\frac{dt}{dr} = \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2-r_0^2}}
    \left[1 + \frac{2m}{r} + \frac{m\,r_0}{r(r+r_0)}\right] + \order{m^2}
    \qquad (\text{check A13a}).
\]
\textit{Step 3 (integrate term by term from $r_0$ to $R$).}
\begin{align*}
    \int_{r_0}^{R}\frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-r_0^2}} &= \sqrt{R^2-r_0^2},\\
    \int_{r_0}^{R}\frac{2m\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-r_0^2}} &=
      2m\ln\frac{R+\sqrt{R^2-r_0^2}}{r_0} \quad(= 2m\,\mathrm{arccosh}(R/r_0)),\\
    \int_{r_0}^{R}\frac{m\,r_0\,dr}{(r+r_0)\sqrt{r^2-r_0^2}} &=
      m\sqrt{\frac{R-r_0}{R+r_0}} ,
\end{align*}
the last one verified by differentiating the right side (checks A13b--d).
Dividing by $\speedoflight$ gives eq.~\eqref{eq:travel_time_expanded}.

\textit{Step 4 (what is ``excess'').}  The first term is the flat-space time
along a straight line from closest approach to radius~$R$.  For $R \gg r_0$
the excess is
$\delta T(R) \approx (2m/\speedoflight)\ln(2R/r_0) + m/\speedoflight$
(check A14a): logarithmically divergent in~$R$, which is why only
\emph{differences} of delays are physical.
\end{derivation}
\subsection{The Two Components of Time Delay}
\label{sec:delay_components}

The total time delay $\Delta T$ for a lensed photon (relative to the
unlensed path) has two contributions
(Congdon \& Keeton eqs.~3.107--3.110):
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Delta T = \underbrace{\frac{\Dd \Ds}{2\speedoflight\,\Dds}
    (\theta - \beta)^2}_{\text{geometric delay}}
    + \underbrace{\frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^3}
    \ln\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}}{|\theta|}}_{\text{Shapiro (gravitational) delay}}
    }
    \label{eq:time_delay_total}
\end{equation}
where $\Dd$, $\Ds$, $\Dds$ are the angular diameter distances from
observer to lens, observer to source, and lens to source respectively;
$\theta$ is the image position and $\beta$ the (unlensed) source position;
and $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is an arbitrary reference angle.  Changing
$\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ adds the same constant to $\Delta T$ for every
image, so it drops out of the measurable delay \emph{differences}
between images.


\begin{annotation}[Conventions hidden in eq.~\eqref{eq:time_delay_total}]
\textit{The $(1+z_d)$ factor.}  Eq.~\eqref{eq:time_delay_total} is the delay in
the rest frame of the lens.  The delay an observer measures is stretched by
cosmological time dilation, $\Delta t_{\rm obs} = (1+z_d)\,\Delta T$; Module~6
writes it with this factor in front (eq.~\eqref{eq:time_delay_function}).
Forgetting it biases $\Hubble$ by $(1+z_d)$, i.e.\ by tens of per cent.

\textit{Where the Shapiro coefficient comes from.}  Write the point-mass Fermat
potential as $(\Dd\Ds/\speedoflight\Dds)[\tfrac12(\theta-\beta)^2 -
\thetaE^2\ln|\theta|]$.  With $\thetaE^2 = (4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2)\,\Dds/(\Dd\Ds)$
the distance factors cancel in the second term, leaving exactly
$(4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3)\ln(1/|\theta|)$ (check A18): the Shapiro term
is independent of the distances; all the cosmology sits in the geometric term.

\textit{The reference angle.}  $\ln$ needs a dimensionless argument;
$\theta_{\rm ref}$ supplies the unit.  Changing it adds a constant to every image's
delay, the time-delay analogue of the freedom to add a constant to a potential.
\end{annotation}
\begin{remark}
\textbf{Geometric delay:} The lensed ray follows a longer path through
space than the direct (unlensed) ray.  This is a purely geometric effect
and is always positive.

\textbf{Shapiro (gravitational) delay:}  The gravitational potential
slows the effective speed of light near the lens.  Clocks near a mass run
slower (gravitational redshift), so the signal takes longer to traverse the
gravitational potential well.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The total time delay~\eqref{eq:time_delay_total} is closely related to the
\textbf{Fermat potential}, which we will study in detail in Module~6.  The
Fermat potential $\tau(\vectheta)$ encodes both the geometric and
gravitational delays, and its gradient gives the lens equation.  Images
form at stationary points of the Fermat potential (Fermat's principle
applied to gravitational lensing).
\end{remark}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:shapiro_sun}
Derive the Shapiro time delay for a radar signal passing near the Sun and
bouncing off a planet at distance~$R$ from the Sun.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Show that the excess round-trip travel time is approximately
          \begin{equation*}
              \Delta T \approx \frac{4\Gnewton \Msun}{\speedoflight^3}
              \ln\!\left(\frac{4 R_\oplus R}{r_0^2}\right)
          \end{equation*}
          where $R_\oplus$ is the Earth--Sun distance and $r_0$ the
          closest approach distance.
    \item For a signal grazing the Sun ($r_0 = \Rsun$) bouncing off
          Mercury at superior conjunction (Mercury behind the Sun as seen
          from Earth), compute $\Delta T$ numerically.
          You should find $\Delta T \approx 220~\mu\mathrm{s}$
          ($240~\mu\mathrm{s}$ once the non-logarithmic $+1$ of
          Exercise~\ref{ex:shapiro_radar} is kept).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}


\begin{annotation}[220 versus 240\,$\mu$s]
With $4\Gnewton\Msun/\speedoflight^3 = 19.7~\mu$s and
$\ln(4R_\oplus R/r_0^2) = 11.18$ for Mercury and a grazing ray, the leading-log
formula of part~(a) gives $19.7 \times 11.18 \approx 220~\mu$s.  The
$240~\mu$s value is $19.7 \times (11.18 + 1)$: it includes the non-logarithmic
``$+1$'' that Exercise~\ref{ex:shapiro_radar} keeps (derived in the solutions).
The ray can graze the Sun only if Mercury is behind it (superior conjunction);
at inferior conjunction Mercury lies between Earth and Sun and the path stays
$\sim 80\,\Rsun$ away.  (Checks A14c--f, A22, A24a--b.)
\end{annotation}

\section{Deflection by the Sun}
\label{sec:deflection_sun}

The first and most famous test of the GR deflection formula was
\textbf{Eddington's 1919 solar eclipse expedition}.  During a total
eclipse, stars near the Sun become visible, and their apparent positions
are shifted outward by the gravitational deflection.

For light grazing the limb of the Sun ($b = \Rsun$):
\begin{equation}
    \alphahat_\odot = \frac{4\Gnewton \Msun}{\speedoflight^2\, \Rsun}
    = \frac{4 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.989 \times 10^{30}}
          {(2.998 \times 10^{8})^2 \times 6.957 \times 10^{8}}
    \approx 8.49 \times 10^{-6}~\text{rad} \,.
    \label{eq:deflection_sun}
\end{equation}
Converting to arcseconds:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \alphahat_\odot = 1.75''
    }
    \label{eq:deflection_sun_arcsec}
\end{equation}
This is the Newtonian value $0.876''$ \textit{doubled} by general relativity.
Eddington's measurements confirmed the GR prediction (within the
substantial error bars of the time), making Einstein a global celebrity.


\begin{annotation}[Mental arithmetic for eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_sun}]
Use $\alphahat = 2\RS/b$: $\RS(\Msun) = 2.95$~km and $\Rsun = 6.957\times10^5$~km,
so $\alphahat_\odot = 5.91/(6.957\times 10^5) = 8.49\times10^{-6}$~rad; one
radian is $206\,265''$, giving $1.75''$.  The Newtonian (Soldner) value is
exactly half, $4.25\times10^{-6}$~rad $= 0.876''$, usually quoted as $0.87''$
or $0.88''$.  (Checks A12a--b, A16d.)
\end{annotation}
\begin{example}
\textbf{Deflection by Jupiter.}  Jupiter has $M_J = 1.898 \times 10^{27}$~kg
and $R_J = 7.149 \times 10^{7}$~m.  The deflection for light grazing
Jupiter's limb is:
\begin{equation}
    \alphahat_J = \frac{4\Gnewton M_J}{\speedoflight^2\, R_J}
    \approx 0.016'' = 16~\text{mas} \,.
    \label{eq:deflection_jupiter}
\end{equation}
This is small but measurable with modern astrometry (e.g., the Gaia
satellite, which achieves $\sim 10~\mu$as precision).
\end{example}

    \caption{Deflection angle $\alphahat$ as a function of impact

\section{Deflection by Galaxies}
\label{sec:deflection_galaxies}

The astrophysical power of gravitational lensing comes from the fact that
the deflection angles produced by \textit{galaxies} are of order
arcseconds --- large enough to produce multiple images, arcs, and rings
that are observable with ground-based and space-based telescopes.

For a galaxy of mass $M = 10^{12}\,\Msun$ with a typical scale of
$b \sim 10$~kpc~$\approx 3.09 \times 10^{20}$~m:
\begin{equation}
    \alphahat \sim \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2\, b}
    = \frac{4 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 10^{12} \times 1.989 \times 10^{30}}
          {(2.998 \times 10^{8})^2 \times 3.09 \times 10^{20}}
    \approx 3.9''
    \label{eq:deflection_galaxy}
\end{equation}
The corresponding Einstein radius (which we will derive properly in
Module~4) is a few arcseconds, depending on the distance configuration.

\begin{remark}
The Einstein radius $\thetaE$ for a point mass lens at cosmological
distances is:
\begin{equation}
    \thetaE = \sqrt{\frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2}
    \frac{\Dds}{\Dd\,\Ds}} \,.
    \label{eq:einstein_radius_deflection}
\end{equation}
For a $10^{12}\,\Msun$ galaxy at $z_d \sim 0.3$ with a source at
$z_s \sim 1$, this gives $\thetaE \approx 2.4''$
(Exercise~\ref{ex:einstein_radius_numerical}).\footnote{Earlier versions of
this remark quoted $\thetaE \sim 1''$; with the concordance distances of
Module~3 ($D_d = 919$, $D_s = 1652$, $D_{ds} = 1055$~Mpc) the value is
$2.38''$.}  Multiple images form
whenever the source lies within $\sim \thetaE$ of the optical axis ---
this is the regime of \textbf{strong gravitational lensing}.  We will
develop this quantitatively in Modules~4--5.
\end{remark}


\begin{derivation}[Eq.~\eqref{eq:einstein_radius_deflection} from $\alphahat$]
In angular coordinates the lens equation (Module~4) is
$\beta = \theta - (\Dds/\Ds)\,\alphahat(\Dd\theta)$.  For a point mass
$\alphahat(\xi) = 4\Gnewton M/(\speedoflight^2\xi)$ and $\xi = \Dd\theta$:
\[
    \beta = \theta - \frac{\Dds}{\Ds}\,\frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2\Dd\,\theta}.
\]
A source exactly behind the lens ($\beta = 0$) is imaged onto the ring
$\theta^2 = (4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2)\,\Dds/(\Dd\Ds)$, which is
eq.~\eqref{eq:einstein_radius_deflection} (check A17).  For
$M = 10^{12}\,\Msun$, $z_d = 0.3$, $z_s = 1$ in the concordance cosmology the
distances of Module~3 give $\thetaE \approx 2.4''$
(Exercise~\ref{ex:einstein_radius_numerical}; check A25).
\end{derivation}

\begin{bridge}[One physical deflection, two source planes]
In a double-source-plane lens (Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}) light from two
sources at $z_{s1} < z_{s2}$ passes the same main deflector.  The physical
deflection $\alphahat(\xi)$ at a given lens-plane position is the same for both;
only the distance factor differs.  For a common $\alphahat$ the ratio of the two
reduced deflections is
\[
    \frac{\alpha_1}{\alpha_2} = \frac{D_{ls_1}/D_{s_1}}{D_{ls_2}/D_{s_2}}
    = \frac{D_{ls_1}D_{s_2}}{D_{s_1}D_{ls_2}} \equiv \Brat
    \qquad(\text{check A19}),
\]
the cosmology-dependent ratio of four angular-diameter distances that the
compound-lens chapters measure.  Module~3 shows how to compute each of the
four.
\end{bridge}

\section{Exact vs.\ Weak-Field Deflection}
\label{sec:exact_vs_approx}

The weak-field formula $\alphahat = 4m/b$ is a first-order approximation
valid when $b \gg \RS$.  For photons passing closer to the mass, higher-order
corrections become important.  The \textit{exact} deflection angle is
obtained by evaluating the integral~\eqref{eq:delta_phi_u2} numerically
(or using elliptic integrals), without the weak-field expansion.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../Figures/02_Light_Deflection/exact_vs_approx.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison of the exact deflection angle (solid blue, from
    numerical integration) and the weak-field approximations
    $\alphahat = 4m/b$ (dashed red, first order) and including the
    second-order correction (dot-dashed green), as a function of $b/\RS$.
    The lower panel shows the relative error.  The first-order
    approximation has $\sim 15\%$ error at $b = 10\,\RS$ and reaches
    $1\%$ accuracy only for $b \gtrsim 150\,\RS$.  At the photon sphere
    $b = 3\sqrt{3}\,\RS/2$, the exact deflection diverges (the photon
    orbits the mass).  Generated by \texttt{deflection\_angle.wl}.}
    \label{fig:exact_vs_approx}
\end{figure}

The second-order correction has been computed by several authors; the
result is (see, e.g., Keeton \& Petters 2005):
\begin{equation}
    \alphahat = \frac{4m}{b} + \frac{15\pi}{4}\left(\frac{m}{b}\right)^2
    + \order{m/b}^3 \,.
    \label{eq:deflection_second_order}
\end{equation}
The critical impact parameter below which the photon is captured (i.e.,
spirals into the mass) is $b_{\mathrm{crit}} = 3\sqrt{3}\,m =
3\sqrt{3}\,\RS/2$.  This corresponds to the photon sphere derived in
Module~1d.


\begin{derivation}[Where $15\pi/4$ and $3\sqrt{3}\,m$ come from]
\textit{Second order in $\varepsilon = m/r_0$.}  Keep the
$\tfrac32\varepsilon^2B^2/A^{5/2}$ term of the expansion (derivation box after
eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_phi_expanded}).  Its integral is finite (the integrand
behaves as $(1-u)^{-1/2}$ at $u=1$) and
\[
    2\int_0^1 \frac{3}{2}\,\frac{(1-u^3)^2}{(1-u^2)^{5/2}}\,du
    = \frac{15\pi}{4} - 4,
    \qquad
    \alphahat = 4\varepsilon + \Bigl(\frac{15\pi}{4} - 4\Bigr)\varepsilon^2 + \cdots
\]
\textit{Convert to $m/b$.}  With $r_0 = b - m - \tfrac32 m^2/b$,
$\varepsilon = (m/b)(1 + m/b + \cdots)$, so $4\varepsilon = 4m/b + 4(m/b)^2 +
\cdots$.  The $+4(m/b)^2$ cancels the $-4$, leaving
eq.~\eqref{eq:deflection_second_order}: $\alphahat = 4m/b + (15\pi/4)(m/b)^2$
(checks A9a--c).

\textit{Capture.}  The turning-point condition gives
$b^2 = r_0^3/(r_0 - 2m)$.  A ray with impact parameter $b$ has a turning point
only if this equation has a root; $b^2(r_0)$ has its minimum where
$d(b^2)/dr_0 = 0$, at $r_0 = 3m$ (the photon sphere), with
$b_{\rm crit}^2 = 27m^2$, i.e.\ $b_{\rm crit} = 3\sqrt3\,m$ (checks A11a--b).
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[A rule of thumb for the error of $4m/b$]
The ratio of the second-order to the first-order term is
$(15\pi/16)(m/b) = (15\pi/32)/(b/\RS)$.  This predicts a 1\% error at
$b/\RS \approx 147$ and $14.7\%$ at $b/\RS = 10$; the exact numerical errors are
$1.0\%$ (crossing $1\%$ between $b/\RS = 140$ and $150$) and $15.3\%$.  So the
first neglected term accounts for almost all of the error, and the slow
convergence quoted in the caption of Fig.~\ref{fig:exact_vs_approx} is just the
large coefficient $15\pi/16 \approx 2.9$.  (Checks A10a--c.)
\end{annotation}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:deflection_summary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Light follows null geodesics in Schwarzschild spacetime.  The
          orbit equation~\eqref{eq:photon_orbit_eq} reduces to an integral
          for the total azimuthal angle change~$\Delta\phi$.

    \item \textbf{Newtonian prediction} (Soldner, 1801):
          $\alphahat_{\mathrm{Newton}} = 2\Gnewton M /
          (\speedoflight^2 b)$.  This accounts for only the
          \textit{temporal} curvature of spacetime.

    \item \textbf{GR prediction} (Einstein, 1915):
          $\alphahat = 4\Gnewton M / (\speedoflight^2 b)$.
          The factor of~$2$ enhancement comes from \textit{spatial}
          curvature, which contributes equally to the deflection.

    \item \textbf{Shapiro time delay:} A gravitational field causes an
          excess travel time $\Delta T$ with geometric and gravitational
          components.  This is connected to the Fermat potential
          (Module~6).

    \item \textbf{Solar deflection:} $\alphahat_\odot = 1.75''$, confirmed
          by Eddington (1919) and modern measurements to $< 0.01\%$.

    \item \textbf{Galaxy-scale lensing:} $\alphahat \sim 1''$--$4''$
          for $\sim 10^{12}\,\Msun$ galaxies at $b \sim 10$~kpc,
          producing multiple images, arcs, and Einstein rings in strong
          lensing.

    \item The weak-field approximation $\alphahat = 4m/b$ converges
          slowly; the error is $\sim 15\%$ at $b = 10\,\RS$ and drops
          below $1\%$ only for $b \gtrsim 150\,\RS$.  The exact
          deflection diverges at the photon sphere ($b_{\mathrm{crit}} =
          3\sqrt{3}\,\RS/2$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Problems}
\label{sec:deflection_problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:soldner_full}
\textbf{(Soldner's Newtonian deflection.)}
Derive the Newtonian deflection angle for a ``corpuscle of light''
traveling at speed $\speedoflight$ past a mass $M$ at impact parameter $b$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Write the Newtonian orbit equation and identify the semi-latus
          rectum $p$ and eccentricity $e$ in terms of $b$, $M$, $\Gnewton$,
          and $\speedoflight$.
    \item Show that the deflection angle is
          $\alphahat_{\mathrm{Newton}} = 2\Gnewton M /
          (\speedoflight^2 b)$ in the weak-field limit.
    \item Compute the Newtonian prediction for the solar deflection.  You
          should get $0.876''$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:factor_of_two}
\textbf{(The factor of 2: GR vs.\ Newton.)}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Starting from the result $\Delta\phi = \pi + 4m/r_0$, show that
          the GR deflection angle is $\alphahat = 4\Gnewton M /
          (\speedoflight^2 b)$.
    \item The Newtonian result uses only $g_{tt} \approx -(1 - 2\Phi/
          \speedoflight^2)$ where $\Phi = -\Gnewton M/r$.  In GR, the
          spatial part of the Schwarzschild metric also curves:
          $g_{rr} \approx 1 + 2\Gnewton M/(\speedoflight^2 r)$.
          Argue qualitatively (or using the isotropic form of the metric)
          that the spatial curvature contributes an \textit{equal} amount
          of deflection, doubling the Newtonian result.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:deflections_numerical}
\textbf{(Deflection for Sun, Jupiter, and galaxy.)}
Compute $\alphahat$ for:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Light grazing the Sun: $M = 1.989 \times 10^{30}$~kg,
          $b = \Rsun = 6.957 \times 10^{8}$~m.
    \item Light grazing Jupiter: $M = 1.898 \times 10^{27}$~kg,
          $b = R_J = 7.149 \times 10^{7}$~m.
    \item A $10^{12}\,\Msun$ galaxy at $b = 10$~kpc.
\end{enumerate}
Express all answers in arcseconds.  Verify with
\solnlink{02_Light_Deflection/problems\_02.wl}{problems\_02.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:shapiro_radar}
\textbf{(Shapiro time delay for radar echoes.)}
A radar signal is sent from Earth, passes near the Sun at closest approach
$r_0$, bounces off Mercury, and returns.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Using eq.~\eqref{eq:travel_time_expanded}, show that the excess
          round-trip time is
          \begin{equation*}
              \Delta T \approx
              \frac{4\Gnewton \Msun}{\speedoflight^3}
              \left[\ln\!\left(\frac{4\, R_\oplus\, R_{\mathrm{Merc}}}{r_0^2}
              \right) + 1\right]
          \end{equation*}
          where $R_\oplus = 1.496 \times 10^{11}$~m and
          $R_{\mathrm{Merc}} = 5.79 \times 10^{10}$~m are the
          Earth--Sun and Mercury--Sun distances.
    \item For $r_0 = \Rsun$, compute $\Delta T$ in microseconds.
    \item The first radar measurement of the effect \citep{shapiro_1968}
          gave $\Delta T = 200 \pm 20~\mu$s; the prediction is
          \citet{shapiro_1964}.  By how many
          standard deviations does it differ from your result, and what
          could account for the difference?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
