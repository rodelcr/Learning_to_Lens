\begin{quote}
\textit{The lensing properties of model gravitational lenses are especially easy to

\hfill --- Narayan \& Bartelmann (1997), Sect.~3.3.1
\end{quote}

\noindent
In Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation} we derived the lens equation
$\vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta)$ as a geometric
consequence of light deflection.  In Chapter~\ref{ch:light_deflection}
we computed the Shapiro time delay experienced by photons passing
through a gravitational potential, and in
Chapter~\ref{ch:linearized_gravity} we introduced the effective
refractive index of a weak gravitational field.

In this chapter we show that the lens equation itself emerges from a
variational principle: images form at stationary points of the
\textbf{arrival-time surface}.  This elegant reformulation ---
Fermat's principle for gravitational lensing --- not only provides a
deeper understanding of image formation, but also yields
\textbf{observable time delays} between multiple images.  These time
delays are directly proportional to $\Hubble^{-1}$, making
multiply-imaged variable sources (such as quasars) a powerful and
independent probe of the Hubble constant.

\section{Introduction: From Geometry to a Variational Principle}
\label{sec:fermat_intro}

The lens equation $\vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta)$ was
derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation} by tracing light rays through
the lensing geometry.  While this approach is direct and intuitive, it
does not explain \textit{why} light ``chooses'' particular paths through
the lens plane, nor does it immediately reveal the relative arrival times
of multiple images.

A deeper perspective comes from \textbf{Fermat's principle}: among all
possible paths from source to observer, light follows those for which the
travel time is \textit{stationary} (a minimum, maximum, or saddle point
of the arrival time).  In classical optics, Fermat's principle explains
Snell's law and the focusing properties of lenses.  In gravitational
lensing, it does far more:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item It \textbf{derives} the lens equation as the stationarity
          condition $\nabla_{\vectheta}\, t = 0$.
    \item It \textbf{classifies} multiple images by the nature of the
          stationary point (minimum, saddle, or maximum), which
          determines their parity and arrival order.
    \item It produces \textbf{observable time delays} between multiple
          images, which can be used to measure the Hubble constant.
\end{enumerate}

The key quantity is the \textbf{time delay function} (or
\textbf{arrival-time surface}) $t(\vectheta)$, which gives the arrival
time of a photon traveling from a source at $\vecbeta$ via the lens
plane at $\vectheta$ to the observer.  Images form at the stationary
points of $t(\vectheta)$, and the differences in $t$ between stationary
points give the time delays.

\section{The Time Delay Function}
\label{sec:time_delay_function}

Consider a photon emitted by a source at angular position $\vecbeta$ in
the source plane.  The photon passes through the lens plane at angular
position $\vectheta$ and arrives at the observer.  The total travel time
relative to the unperturbed (unlensed, straight-line) path consists of
two contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geometric delay} (extra path length): The deflected ray
          travels a longer geometric path than the straight-line ray.  In
          the small-angle, thin-lens approximation, the extra path length
          corresponds to an angular excess of $\half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2$
          (see Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}, Section~\ref{sec:lensing_geometry}).
    \item \textbf{Shapiro (gravitational) delay}: The photon passes
          through the gravitational potential of the lens, experiencing the
          time delay derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:light_deflection}.  In the
          thin-lens formalism, this delay is encoded in the
          \textbf{lensing potential} $\psiL(\vectheta)$
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}).
\end{itemize}

Combining these contributions, the total arrival time is
(Narayan \& Bartelmann eq.~63; Congdon \& Keeton eqs.~4.23--4.25):
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    t(\vectheta) = \frac{(1 + z_d)}{\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\,
    \left[\half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2 - \psiL(\vectheta)\right]
    + \text{const}
    }
    \label{eq:time_delay_function}
\end{equation}
where $z_d$ is the redshift of the lens, the angular diameter distances
$\Dd$, $\Ds$, $\Dds$ are defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances},
and the constant is an irrelevant additive term that depends on the
unperturbed travel time.

The factor $(1 + z_d)$ arises from \textbf{cosmological time dilation}:
the geometric and Shapiro delays are computed in the rest frame of the
lens (at redshift $z_d$), but the time delay is \textit{observed} in
the observer's frame.  A time interval $\Delta t_{\mathrm{lens}}$ at
the lens is stretched to
$\Delta t_{\mathrm{obs}} = (1 + z_d)\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{lens}}$ in the
observer's frame, just as photon wavelengths are redshifted by the same
factor (eq.~\ref{eq:redshift_scale_factor} from
Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}; cf.\ Schneider, Ehlers \&
Falco 1992, Sec.~4.6, eqs.~4.65--4.67).
The combination $\Dd\,\Ds / \Dds$ is the \textbf{effective lensing
distance}, which encodes the geometry of the observer--lens--source
system.

\begin{derivation}[The two terms of eq.~\eqref{eq:time_delay_function}]
\textbf{Geometric term (Euclidean sketch).}  Put the observer at the origin,
the lens plane at distance $\Dd$ and the source plane at $\Ds$, and in this
sketch only take $\Ds = \Dd + \Dds$ (flat, static space).  A ray that crosses the
lens plane at $\xi = \Dd\theta$ and ends at the source position
$\eta = \Ds\beta$ is longer than the straight ray to $\eta$ by
\[
  \Delta L \simeq \frac{\xi^2}{2\Dd} + \frac{(\eta-\xi)^2}{2\Dds}
  - \frac{\eta^2}{2\Ds}
\]
(each leg of length $\sqrt{D^2+h^2}$ is expanded as $D + h^2/2D$; the
leading $D$'s cancel because $\Dd+\Dds = \Ds$ here).  Substituting
$\xi=\Dd\theta$, $\eta=\Ds\beta$ and eliminating $\Ds-\Dds=\Dd$, the
coefficients of $\theta^2$, $\beta^2$ and $\theta\beta$ each reduce to
$\Dd\Ds/(2\Dds)$, so
\[
  \Delta L = \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{2\,\Dds}\,|\vectheta-\vecbeta|^2 .
\]
Dividing by $\speedoflight$ gives the geometric delay in the lens frame;
multiplying by $(1+z_d)$ converts it to the observer frame.  In an
expanding universe the same form holds with angular-diameter distances
(the general argument is the one cited in the main text, SEF Sec.~4.6);
the Euclidean sketch is only meant to show where the combination
$\Dd\Ds/\Dds$ comes from.

\textbf{Gravitational term.}  The Shapiro delay along the ray is
$t_{\rm grav} = -(2/\speedoflight^3)\int\Phi\,dz$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:light_deflection}),
and the lensing potential is
$\psiL = \frac{\Dds}{\Dd\Ds}\frac{2}{\speedoflight^2}\int\Phi\,dz$
\citep[eq.~48]{narayan_lectures_1997}.  Eliminating $\int\Phi\,dz$:
\[
  t_{\rm grav} = -\frac{1}{\speedoflight}\,\frac{\Dd\Ds}{\Dds}\,\psiL ,
\]
the same prefactor as the geometric term, so the two terms can share one
bracket.
(Both steps checked symbolically: \texttt{problems\_06.wl}, A1--A2.)
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Units, signs, and what ``const'' hides]
\emph{Units.}  $\tau$ is in rad$^2$ and $\Dd\Ds/(\speedoflight\Dds)$ is a time,
so $t$ is a time.  If $\tau$ is quoted in arcsec$^2$, convert first
($1''^{\,2} = 2.35\times10^{-11}$\,rad$^2$); forgetting this is the most common
numerical slip in Exercise~\ref{ex:H0_from_time_delay}.
For $z_d=0.3$, $z_s=1$ (flat $\Lambda$CDM, $\Hubble=70$, $\Omegam=0.3$) the
\emph{time-delay distance} $(1+z_d)\Dd\Ds/\Dds \approx 1870$\,Mpc, and each
arcsec$^2$ of $\Delta\tau$ is worth $\approx 52$ days (check A15).

\emph{Sign of $\psiL$.}  $\psiL$ is only defined up to an additive constant
(its line-of-sight integral diverges for most models), so the statement
``$\psiL>0$'' carries no physics; only differences of $\tau$ matter.  What is
physical is the \emph{shape}: $d\psiL/d\theta = \alpha > 0$ for every
attractive axisymmetric lens (check A13), so $-\psiL$ is \emph{largest at the
lens centre}.  Rays through the deeper part of the potential are delayed
\emph{more} by the Shapiro term, never less --- the Shapiro effect is always
a delay.
\end{annotation}

\begin{definition}[Fermat potential]
The dimensionless quantity
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta) = \half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2
    - \psiL(\vectheta)
    }
    \label{eq:fermat_potential}
\end{equation}
is called the \textbf{Fermat potential} (or \textbf{time delay
potential}).  The arrival time is proportional to $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
    t(\vectheta) = \frac{(1 + z_d)}{\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\, \tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta)
    + \text{const} \,.
    \label{eq:t_tau_relation}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The Fermat potential has a simple physical interpretation:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The first term $\half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2$ is the
          \textbf{geometric delay}: it penalizes paths where the ray in the
          lens plane ($\vectheta$) is far from the straight-line direction
          ($\vecbeta$).
    \item The second term $-\psiL(\vectheta)$ is the
          \textbf{gravitational (Shapiro) delay}.  Because $\psiL$ is
          defined only up to an additive constant, its sign carries no
          physical meaning; its shape does.  For any attractive
          axisymmetric lens $d\psiL/d\theta = \alpha > 0$, so $-\psiL$ is
          largest at the lens centre: photons passing through a deeper
          part of the potential are delayed \textit{more}, as for the
          Shapiro delay of Chapter~\ref{ch:light_deflection}.\footnote{An
          earlier version of this remark said that $\psiL>0$ makes the
          Shapiro term \emph{reduce} the arrival time.  That has the sign
          backwards: the Shapiro delay $(2/\speedoflight^3)\int|\Phi|\,dl$
          is positive and grows with $|\Phi|$
          \citep[eq.~3]{narayan_lectures_1997}.}  For a point mass,
          $\psiL = \thetaE^2\ln|\vectheta|$ and $-\psiL\to+\infty$ at the
          origin, so the arrival-time surface has an infinite peak there
          instead of a smooth maximum.
\end{itemize}
The competition between geometric and gravitational delay determines the
structure of the arrival-time surface and hence the number and types of
images.
\end{remark}

\section{Fermat's Principle and the Lens Equation}
\label{sec:fermat_principle}

\textbf{Fermat's principle} states that images form at stationary points
of the arrival time $t(\vectheta)$, or equivalently, at stationary points
of the Fermat potential $\tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta)$:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\vectheta}\, t(\vectheta) = 0
    \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
    \nabla_{\vectheta}\, \tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta) = 0 \,.
    \label{eq:fermat_condition}
\end{equation}

Computing the gradient of $\tau$:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{\vectheta}\, \tau &= \nabla_{\vectheta}\left[
    \half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2 - \psiL(\vectheta)\right]
    \nonumber \\
    &= (\vectheta - \vecbeta) - \nabla\psiL(\vectheta) \,.
    \label{eq:grad_tau}
\end{align}
Setting this to zero:
\begin{equation}
    \vectheta - \vecbeta - \nabla\psiL(\vectheta) = 0
    \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
    \vecbeta = \vectheta - \nabla\psiL(\vectheta) \,.
    \label{eq:lens_eq_from_fermat}
\end{equation}
Since the reduced deflection angle is $\vecalpha(\vectheta) = \nabla\psiL(\vectheta)$
(from Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}), this is precisely
the \textbf{lens equation}:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta)
    }
    \label{eq:lens_eq_fermat}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
This is a remarkable result: the lens equation, which we previously derived
from ray-tracing geometry, follows directly from the
\textbf{stationarity of the arrival time}.  Fermat's principle provides
a variational derivation of the fundamental equation of gravitational
lensing.  The connection between the gradient of the lensing potential and
the deflection angle, $\vecalpha = \nabla\psiL$, was established in
Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification} from the Poisson equation
$\nabla^2\psiL = 2\kappab$.
\end{remark}

\begin{derivation}[$\nabla\tau = 0$ in components]
Write $\half|\vectheta-\vecbeta|^2 = \half\sum_b(\theta_b-\beta_b)^2$.  The
chain rule gives
$\partial_{\theta_a}\half\sum_b(\theta_b-\beta_b)^2 = \sum_b(\theta_b-\beta_b)\,\delta_{ab}
= \theta_a-\beta_a$, because $\vecbeta$ is held fixed (the source does not
move when we vary the trial path).  Hence
$\partial_a\tau = \theta_a-\beta_a-\partial_a\psiL$, and setting both
components to zero is the vector lens equation with
$\alpha_a = \partial_a\psiL$.  Nothing about the lens was assumed: this holds
for any $\psiL$ (checked for a generic $\psiL(\theta_1,\theta_2)$,
\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A3a).
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Stationary, not minimal]
Fermat's principle selects \emph{stationary} paths.  Minima, saddles and
maxima of $\tau$ are all real images; a common misreading is ``light takes
the quickest path'', which would predict a single image.  The trial paths
$\vectheta$ are kinked at the lens plane and are not themselves light rays;
only the stationary ones are.
\end{annotation}

\section{Classification of Images: Morse Theory}
\label{sec:morse_theory}

At each stationary point $\vectheta_i$ of the Fermat potential (i.e.,
at each image position), we can characterize the nature of the
stationary point by the \textbf{Hessian matrix} of $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
    T_{ab} = \frac{\partial^2 \tau}{\partial\theta_a\,\partial\theta_b}
    = \delta_{ab} - \frac{\partial^2\psiL}{\partial\theta_a\,\partial\theta_b}
    = \Amat_{ab} \,,
    \label{eq:hessian_tau}
\end{equation}
where $\Amat_{ab}$ is the \textbf{Jacobian matrix} of the lens mapping
(the inverse magnification matrix, from
Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}).

\begin{derivation}[Hessian of $\tau$ = Jacobian $\Amat$]
Differentiate $\partial_a\tau = \theta_a-\beta_a-\partial_a\psiL$ once more:
$\partial_b(\theta_a-\beta_a) = \delta_{ab}$ and
$\partial_b\partial_a\psiL \equiv \psiL_{,ab}$.  So
$T_{ab} = \delta_{ab}-\psiL_{,ab}$.  With
$\psiL_{,11} = \kappab+\gamma_1$, $\psiL_{,22} = \kappab-\gamma_1$,
$\psiL_{,12} = \gamma_2$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}),
\[
  T = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\kappab-\gamma_1 & -\gamma_2\\ -\gamma_2 & 1-\kappab+\gamma_1\end{pmatrix}
  = \Amat ,
\]
whose eigenvalues are $1-\kappab\mp|\gammaL|$.  The \emph{curvature} of the
arrival-time surface is therefore the inverse magnification matrix: a
nearly flat direction of $\tau$ is a strongly magnified direction of the
image.  (\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A3b.)
\end{derivation}

The eigenvalues of $T_{ab}$ at a stationary point determine the
\textit{type} of the image.  There are exactly three possibilities,
classified by \textbf{Morse theory}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Type I image --- Minimum of $\tau$:}
    Both eigenvalues of $T$ are positive.  Equivalently,
    $\det(\Amat) > 0$ and $\mathrm{tr}(\Amat) > 0$.  The magnification
    $\mu = 1/\det(\Amat) > 0$ (positive parity: the image is not
    mirror-reflected).  This image corresponds to the \textit{minimum}
    of the arrival-time surface and therefore \textbf{arrives first}.

    \item \textbf{Type II image --- Saddle point of $\tau$:}
    One eigenvalue is positive and one is negative.  Equivalently,
    $\det(\Amat) < 0$.  The magnification $\mu = 1/\det(\Amat) < 0$
    (negative parity: the image is mirror-reflected).

    \item \textbf{Type III image --- Maximum of $\tau$:}
    Both eigenvalues of $T$ are negative.  Equivalently,
    $\det(\Amat) > 0$ and $\mathrm{tr}(\Amat) < 0$.  The magnification
    $\mu = 1/\det(\Amat) > 0$ (positive parity).  This image
    corresponds to the \textit{maximum} of the arrival-time surface and
    therefore \textbf{arrives last}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{derivation}[From eigenvalue signs to $\det\Amat$ and $\mathrm{tr}\,\Amat$]
For a real symmetric $2\times2$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$:
$\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det\Amat$ and $\lambda_1+\lambda_2 = \mathrm{tr}\,\Amat$
(A4a,b).  Therefore
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\det\Amat<0$ $\Leftrightarrow$ opposite signs $\Leftrightarrow$ saddle (Type~II);
  \item $\det\Amat>0$ and $\mathrm{tr}\,\Amat>0$ $\Leftrightarrow$ both positive
        $\Leftrightarrow$ minimum (Type~I) (A4c);
  \item $\det\Amat>0$ and $\mathrm{tr}\,\Amat<0$ $\Leftrightarrow$ both negative
        $\Leftrightarrow$ maximum (Type~III).
\end{itemize}
Now use $\mathrm{tr}\,\Amat = 2-\nabla^2\psiL = 2(1-\kappab)$ (A3c).  A maximum
needs $\mathrm{tr}\,\Amat<0$, i.e.\ $\kappab>1$ \emph{at the image}: Type~III
images can only sit where the lens is locally supercritical, which in
practice means the dense centre.  So the third image of a cored lens sits
at the centre.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Which image arrives first, and parity]
Because the prefactor $(1+z_d)\Dd\Ds/(\speedoflight\Dds)$ is positive, ordering
in $\tau$ is ordering in time: variability appears first in the image at the
lowest stationary point.  For a circular lens this is the image farthest from
the lens centre \citep[Sect.~3.3.2]{narayan_lectures_1997}.  A frequent
error is to assume the \emph{brighter} image leads; brightness is set by
$1/\det\Amat$, arrival by $\tau$, and the two are not tied together in general.
Parity is $\mathrm{sign}(\det\Amat)$: saddles are mirror images, so a
resolved saddle image of an extended source is flipped relative to the
minimum.
\end{annotation}

\begin{remark}[The arrival-time surface determines image topology]
The topology of the arrival-time surface $\tau(\vectheta)$ completely
determines the image structure.  Each minimum, saddle, and maximum of
$\tau$ corresponds to an image.  When a source crosses a caustic, two
stationary points of $\tau$ merge and annihilate (or are created from
nothing) --- always one saddle and one extremum, preserving the
constraint $N_{\min} + N_{\max} - N_{\mathrm{saddle}} = 1$ (a
consequence of Morse theory on the plane).  This topological constraint
is why images always appear and disappear in pairs with opposite parity,
as explored in detail in Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Burke's Odd-Number Theorem]
\label{thm:odd_number}
For a \textbf{transparent} lens (i.e., a lens with a smooth, bounded
surface mass density $\Sigma(\vectheta)$ that falls off sufficiently
rapidly at large $|\vectheta|$), the total number of images is always
\textbf{odd}.  Specifically, if we denote by $n_{\mathrm{min}}$,
$n_{\mathrm{sad}}$, and $n_{\mathrm{max}}$ the numbers of minima,
saddle points, and maxima of $\tau$, then:
\begin{equation}
    n_{\mathrm{min}} - n_{\mathrm{sad}} + n_{\mathrm{max}} = 1 \,.
    \label{eq:odd_number}
\end{equation}
This is a topological result from Morse theory.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
The smoothness hypothesis is essential.  Burke's theorem does
\emph{not} apply to a point mass lens: $\Sigma$ is a Dirac
delta function and $\psiL = \thetaE^2\ln|\vectheta|$ diverges at the
origin, so the origin is a singularity of $\tau$ rather than a true
stationary point.  A point mass therefore produces exactly two images
($\theta_\pm$ from Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}), not three.  For an
extended lens with a smooth core (such as a non-singular isothermal
sphere, Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}), the central singularity is replaced by a genuine
maximum of $\tau$ and a corresponding third image appears, typically
highly demagnified.  The singular isothermal sphere itself is
intermediate: $\Sigma\!\propto\!1/\theta$ is finite at every $\theta>0$
but diverges at the origin, again removing the central image while
keeping the two outer images.
\end{remark}

\begin{derivation}[Why the count is odd: a Poincar\'e--Hopf sketch]
Burke's original argument is a heuristic based on the Poincar\'e--Hopf index
theorem \citep{burke_1981}; the rigorous statement is the Odd Number Image
Theorem of \citet[Theorem~11.5]{petters_singularity_2001}.  The idea:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Far from the lens, $\psiL$ grows more slowly than $|\vectheta|^2$
        (finite mass), so $\nabla\tau\simeq\vectheta$ points radially outward on
        a large circle.  Its winding number there is $+1$.
  \item Around each isolated stationary point the winding number (index) of
        $\nabla\tau$ is $+1$ for a minimum or maximum and $-1$ for a saddle.
  \item If $\nabla\tau$ is smooth inside the circle, the outer winding equals the
        sum of the indices: $n_{\min}+n_{\max}-n_{\rm sad} = 1$.  Since
        $N = n_{\min}+n_{\max}+n_{\rm sad} = 1 + 2n_{\rm sad}$, $N$ is odd.
\end{enumerate}
For a point mass, $\nabla\tau$ is singular at the origin and the singularity
itself carries index $+1$ (near $\vectheta=0$, $\nabla\tau\simeq-\thetaE^2\vectheta/|\vectheta|^2$,
a radial field).  Numerically, for $y=0.5$: winding $+1$ on a large circle, $+1$
around the minimum, $-1$ around the saddle, $+1$ around the origin (check A14).
The singularity ``uses up'' the index of the missing maximum, leaving
$n_{\min}-n_{\rm sad}+n_{\max} = 0$.  The general statement for $g$
point-like (infinite) singularities is
$n_{\min}+n_{\max} = n_{\rm sad}-g+1$ \citep[Theorem~11.4]{petters_singularity_2001}.
For the SIS the origin is not a stationary point but a cone-shaped peak of
$\tau$; \citet[Sect.~3.1]{narayan_lectures_1997} describe it as a third image
of zero flux at $\theta=0$.
\end{derivation}

\begin{bridge}[Burke's theorem and a central point mass]
Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images} asks how many images a galaxy with a central
supermassive black hole makes.  The hypothesis that fails is the
\emph{smooth, bounded} $\Sigma$: a point mass punctures the lens plane with an
infinite singularity of $\psiL$, so the count obeys the $g=1$ version above,
$N = 2(n_{\min}+n_{\max})$, which is even.  The central image of a cored lens
is the Type~III \emph{maximum} of $\tau$ (it needs $\kappab>1$, see the
derivation box on image types); adding the point mass changes what happens
near that maximum, which is where Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images} starts.
\end{bridge}

\section{The Arrival-Time Surface}
\label{sec:arrival_time_surface}

The arrival-time surface is the three-dimensional plot of
$\tau(\vectheta)$ as a function of the two-dimensional image-plane
position $\vectheta = (\theta_1, \theta_2)$ for a fixed source position
$\vecbeta$.  This is the iconic visualization of Fermat's principle in
gravitational lensing.

\subsection{Point Mass Lens}

For a point mass lens with Einstein radius $\thetaE$, the lensing
potential is (from Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}):
\begin{equation}
    \psiL(\vectheta) = \thetaE^2\, \ln|\vectheta| \,.
    \label{eq:psi_point_mass_fermat}
\end{equation}
The Fermat potential becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta) = \half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2
    - \thetaE^2\, \ln|\vectheta| \,.
    \label{eq:tau_point_mass}
\end{equation}

For a source at $\vecbeta = (\beta, 0)$ with $\beta > 0$ (placing the
source along the $\theta_1$-axis without loss of generality by the
azimuthal symmetry of the lens), the arrival-time surface
$\tau(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ has exactly \textbf{two} stationary points
in the region $|\vectheta| > 0$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{minimum} (Type~I) at $\vectheta = (\theta_+, 0)$,
          where $\theta_+ = (\beta + \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2})/2$ is
          the primary image from eq.~\eqref{eq:image_positions}.  This
          image arrives \textit{first}.
    \item A \textbf{saddle point} (Type~II) at
          $\vectheta = (\theta_-, 0)$, where
          $\theta_- = (\beta - \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2})/2$ is the
          secondary image.  This image arrives \textit{second}.
\end{itemize}

The point $\vectheta = \mathbf{0}$ is a \emph{singularity} of $\psiL$,
not a true stationary point of $\tau$, so the point-mass lens has only
the two images $\theta_\pm$ above.  A softened mass profile
(Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}) replaces this singularity with a
smooth maximum and produces a corresponding (highly demagnified) third
image.

\begin{derivation}[Checking ``minimum'' and ``saddle'' for the point mass]
In units of $\thetaE$, on the axis $\vectheta=(x,0)$ the Hessian of
eq.~\eqref{eq:tau_point_mass} is diagonal,
\[
  T = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(1+\frac{1}{x^2},\;1-\frac{1}{x^2}\right),
  \qquad \det\Amat = 1-\frac{1}{x^4}
\]
(radial eigenvalue first; \texttt{problems\_06.wl} A5).  The radial
eigenvalue is always positive.  The tangential one is positive for $|x|>1$
and negative for $|x|<1$.  Since $x_+>1$ and $|x_-|<1$ for every $y>0$, $x_+$
is a minimum and $x_-$ a saddle: the Einstein ring $|x|=1$ separates the
two image types.  Near the origin $\tau\simeq-\ln|\vectheta|\to+\infty$, so
there is no finite maximum to be an image.
\end{derivation}

\subsection{The Einstein Ring as a Degenerate Minimum}

When the source is exactly behind the lens ($\vecbeta = 0$), the
Fermat potential becomes circularly symmetric:
\begin{equation}
    \tau(\vectheta, \mathbf{0}) = \half|\vectheta|^2
    - \thetaE^2\, \ln|\vectheta| \,.
\end{equation}
The stationarity condition $d\tau/d|\vectheta| = 0$ gives
$|\vectheta| = \thetaE$, which is the Einstein ring.  The minimum
of $\tau$ is no longer an isolated point but a \textbf{circle} of
stationary points --- the Einstein ring is a degenerate minimum of
the arrival-time surface.

\section{Observable Time Delays}
\label{sec:observable_time_delays}

The arrival time at each image $\vectheta_i$ is given by
eq.~\eqref{eq:time_delay_function}.  The \textbf{time delay} between
two images $i$ and $j$ is the difference in their arrival times:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Delta t_{ij} = t(\vectheta_i) - t(\vectheta_j)
    = \frac{(1 + z_d)}{\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\,
    \left[\tau(\vectheta_i, \vecbeta) - \tau(\vectheta_j, \vecbeta)\right]
    }
    \label{eq:time_delay_between_images}
\end{equation}

This quantity is \textbf{directly observable}: if the source is variable
(as quasars are), the light curves measured at the two image positions
will show the same variability pattern, but shifted by $\Delta t_{ij}$.
By cross-correlating the light curves, one can extract $\Delta t_{ij}$
to high precision.

\begin{remark}
The time delay $\Delta t_{ij}$ depends on three ingredients:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item The \textbf{cosmological distances} $(1+z_d)\,\Dd\,\Ds/\Dds$,
          which encode the expansion history of the universe.
    \item The \textbf{image positions} $\vectheta_i$, $\vectheta_j$,
          which are directly observed.
    \item The \textbf{lensing potential} $\psiL(\vectheta)$, which must
          be modeled from the observed lens properties (mass distribution,
          image positions, fluxes, etc.).
\end{enumerate}
Given items (2) and (3), a measurement of $\Delta t_{ij}$ constrains
the distance combination in item (1) --- and hence the cosmological
parameters.
\end{remark}

\begin{annotation}[Only differences are observable]
The additive constant in eq.~\eqref{eq:time_delay_function} (and any constant
added to $\psiL$) cancels in $\Delta t_{ij}$, so absolute arrival times are
never measured.  The sign convention to remember: $\Delta t_{ij}>0$ means
image $i$ arrives \emph{after} image $j$.  Papers differ on whether
$\Delta t_{AB}$ means $t_A-t_B$ or $t_B-t_A$, so always check which one is meant
before comparing numbers.
\end{annotation}

\section{Time Delay and the Hubble Constant}
\label{sec:time_delay_H0}

The key cosmological application of time delays is the measurement of
the Hubble constant $\Hubble$.  The distance combination
$\Dd\,\Ds / \Dds$ is an angular diameter distance ratio that scales
inversely with the Hubble constant:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds} \propto \frac{1}{\Hubble} \,.
    \label{eq:distance_H0_scaling}
\end{equation}
This follows from the definition of angular diameter distances in
Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}: all comoving distances scale as
$\speedoflight / \Hubble$ times a dimensionless integral over the
expansion history.

\begin{derivation}[$\Dd\Ds/\Dds\propto 1/\Hubble$, step by step]
In a flat universe, $D(z_1,z_2) = (\speedoflight/\Hubble)\,[\chi(z_2)-\chi(z_1)]/(1+z_2)$
with $\chi(z) = \int_0^z dz'/E(z')$ dimensionless.  Then
\[
  \frac{\Dd\Ds}{\Dds}
  = \frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble}\,
    \frac{\dfrac{\chi_d}{1+z_d}\,\dfrac{\chi_s}{1+z_s}}{\dfrac{\chi_s-\chi_d}{1+z_s}}
  = \frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble}\,\frac{\chi_d\,\chi_s}{(1+z_d)(\chi_s-\chi_d)} ,
\]
the $(1+z_s)$ factors cancelling (\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A10g).  The
fraction depends on $z_d$, $z_s$ and the density parameters, but not on
$\Hubble$.  Hence $\Delta t\propto\Hubble^{-1}$ at fixed $\Delta\tau$.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[$\Dds\neq\Ds-\Dd$]
Comoving distances add along a line of sight in a flat universe
($\chi_{ds} = \chi_s-\chi_d$), but angular-diameter distances do not.  For
$z_d=0.3$, $z_s=1$: $\Dds/(\Ds-\Dd)\approx1.44$ (check A10c).  Writing
$\Dds = \Ds-\Dd$ is the classic error that turns a correct $\Hubble$ pipeline into
a wrong one.
\end{annotation}

Therefore, the time delay between images scales as:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Delta t \propto \Hubble^{-1}
    }
    \label{eq:Dt_H0}
\end{equation}

This is the basis of \textbf{time-delay cosmography}: by measuring
$\Delta t$ from monitoring campaigns and modeling the lens potential
$\psiL$, one can determine $\Hubble$ \textit{independently of the
cosmic distance ladder}.  The method is sometimes called ``one-step''
distance measurement, because it converts an observed time delay
directly into a physical distance.

\begin{remark}
To extract $\Hubble$ from a time delay measurement, one needs:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item A precise measurement of $\Delta t$ (from light curve
          monitoring of a multiply-imaged variable source).
    \item The lens and source redshifts $z_d$, $z_s$.
    \item A model for the lens mass distribution $\psiL(\vectheta)$,
          constrained by the observed image positions, fluxes, and
          extended image structure.
    \item A cosmological model (usually flat $\Lambda$CDM with $\Omegam$
          fixed) to convert the distance ratio into $\Hubble$.
\end{enumerate}
The main systematic uncertainty is the lens model degeneracy (the
``mass-sheet degeneracy''), which we will encounter in later chapters.
\end{remark}

\begin{derivation}[Why the mass-sheet degeneracy hits $\Hubble$ directly]
Transform $\kappab\to\lambda\kappab+(1-\lambda)$, i.e.\
$\psiL_\lambda = \lambda\psiL+\tfrac{1-\lambda}{2}|\vectheta|^2$, and rescale the
unobservable source position $\vecbeta_\lambda = \lambda\vecbeta$.  Then
\begin{align*}
  \tau_\lambda &= \half|\vectheta-\lambda\vecbeta|^2-\lambda\psiL-\tfrac{1-\lambda}{2}|\vectheta|^2\\
  &= \lambda\left[\half|\vectheta|^2-\vectheta\cdot\vecbeta-\psiL\right]+\tfrac{\lambda^2}{2}|\vecbeta|^2
   = \lambda\,\tau(\vectheta,\vecbeta)+f(\vecbeta),
\end{align*}
where $f$ does not depend on $\vectheta$ (\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A9a).  So
$\nabla\tau_\lambda = \lambda\nabla\tau$: same image positions (A9b), and
$\Delta\tau_\lambda = \lambda\,\Delta\tau$ between any two images.
\end{derivation}

\begin{bridge}[$\Delta t\propto\lambda$: the mass-sheet transformation]
Chapter~\ref{ch:msd_kinematics} uses this scaling.  The images
cannot tell $\lambda$ apart, but the predicted delay is $\Delta t\propto\lambda/\Hubble$.
So for a fixed measured $\Delta t$ the inferred $\Hubble$ scales as $\lambda$.
Some information that is \emph{not} image positions has to fix $\lambda$:
stellar kinematics (Chapter~\ref{ch:msd_kinematics}) or a second source
plane (Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}).  The degeneracy itself goes back to
\citet{falco_gorenstein_shapiro_1985}.
\end{bridge}

\subsection{Observational Milestones}

Time-delay cosmography has been applied to a growing number of
multiply-imaged quasars:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Q0957+561} (Walsh, Carswell \& Weymann 1979): the first
          discovered gravitational lens.  The time delay ($\Delta t \approx
          417$~days) was measured after years of photometric monitoring.
    \item \textbf{B1608+656}: a four-image lens system with three
          independent time delays, providing strong constraints.
    \item \textbf{H0LiCOW} (2017--present): a systematic program
          combining precise time delays from COSMOGRAIL monitoring with
          detailed lens models and line-of-sight structure analysis.
          The H0LiCOW~XIII result (Wong et al.\ 2020; six lenses) gives
          $\Hubble = 73.3^{+1.7}_{-1.8}$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$,
          in agreement with local distance ladder measurements and in
          some tension with the CMB value ($\Hubble \approx 67$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$
          from Planck).  The successor \textbf{TDCOSMO} programme
          (Birrer et al.\ 2020) performed an independent analysis that
          explicitly marginalises over the mass-sheet degeneracy and
          obtained a less precise but consistent result
          ($\Hubble = 74.5^{+5.6}_{-6.1}$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Example: Point Mass Time Delay}
\label{sec:point_mass_time_delay}

We now compute the time delay for a point mass lens explicitly.  This
illustrative calculation demonstrates the method and gives a sense of
the physical scales involved.

\subsection{Fermat Potential for the Point Mass}

Using the lensing potential $\psiL(\vectheta) = \thetaE^2 \ln|\vectheta|$
and working in units of $\thetaE$ (i.e., defining $x = \theta/\thetaE$
and $y = \beta/\thetaE$), the Fermat potential along the symmetry axis
(where both images lie for a point mass) is:
\begin{equation}
    \tau(x) = \half(x - y)^2 - \ln|x| \,,
    \label{eq:tau_point_mass_1d}
\end{equation}
where we have set $\thetaE = 1$ in the logarithmic term.

The two image positions are $x_\pm = (y \pm \sqrt{y^2 + 4})/2$.

\subsection{Time Delay Computation}

The Fermat potential at the two images is:
\begin{align}
    \tau(x_+) &= \half(x_+ - y)^2 - \ln x_+ \,, \\
    \tau(x_-) &= \half(x_- - y)^2 - \ln|x_-| \,.
\end{align}
The dimensionless time delay is $\Delta\tau = \tau(x_-) - \tau(x_+)$
(since $\tau(x_-) > \tau(x_+)$, the saddle image arrives later).

After algebraic simplification (verified in
\mathlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/time_delay_function.wl}{time\_delay\_function.wl}),
the result is:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Delta\tau = \frac{y\sqrt{y^2 + 4}}{2}
    + \ln\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{y^2 + 4} + y}{\sqrt{y^2 + 4} - y}\right)
    }
    \label{eq:delta_tau_point_mass}
\end{equation}
where $y = \beta / \thetaE$ is the dimensionless source position.

\begin{derivation}[The ``algebraic simplification'' behind eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_tau_point_mass}]
Use three properties of the roots $x_\pm = (y\pm\sqrt{y^2+4})/2$ of
$x^2-yx-1=0$ (Vieta): $x_++x_-=y$, $x_+x_-=-1$, $x_+-x_-=\sqrt{y^2+4}$
(A6a--c).  The first gives $x_+-y=-x_-$ and $x_--y=-x_+$ (A6d), so the
geometric terms swap:
\[
  \Delta\tau = \half(x_--y)^2-\half(x_+-y)^2-\ln|x_-|+\ln x_+
  = \half(x_+^2-x_-^2)+\ln\frac{x_+}{|x_-|}.
\]
Factor $x_+^2-x_-^2 = (x_++x_-)(x_+-x_-) = y\sqrt{y^2+4}$.  For the logarithm,
$|x_-| = (\sqrt{y^2+4}-y)/2$, so
$x_+/|x_-| = (\sqrt{y^2+4}+y)/(\sqrt{y^2+4}-y)$ (A6e,f).  A compact equivalent
is $\ln[\ldots] = 2\,\mathrm{arcsinh}(y/2)$ (A6g).
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Sanity checks on $\Delta\tau(y)$]
(i) $\Delta\tau(0)=0$: a source on axis gives a symmetric ring, so there is no delay.
(ii) $d\Delta\tau/dy = \sqrt{y^2+4}>0$ (A6h), so the delay grows monotonically
with offset.  (iii) For small $y$, $\Delta\tau = 2y+y^3/12+\order{y^5}$ (A6j).
The leading term $2y$ is the same as the SIS result $\Delta\tau = 2\beta\thetaE$
of Section~\ref{sec:sis_time_delay} in units of $\thetaE^2$ (A8d).  Near the
ring, the delay is set mostly by the source offset times the ring size and
only weakly by the profile.
\end{annotation}

The physical time delay is:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t = \frac{(1 + z_d)}{\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\, \thetaE^2\, \Delta\tau \,.
    \label{eq:delta_t_point_mass}
\end{equation}
Using $\thetaE^2 = 4\Gnewton M / (\speedoflight^2) \cdot \Dds / (\Dd\,\Ds)$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t = \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^3}\, (1 + z_d)\, \Delta\tau
    = \frac{2\RS}{\speedoflight}\, (1 + z_d)\, \Delta\tau \,,
    \label{eq:delta_t_schwarzschild}
\end{equation}
where $\RS = 2\Gnewton M / \speedoflight^2$ is the Schwarzschild
radius.\footnote{The factor 2 in the second equality follows from
$4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3 = 2\RS/\speedoflight$.  Earlier versions of these
notes wrote $\RS/\speedoflight$ here and in
Example~\ref{ex:point_mass_numerical}, which halved the delay.}

\begin{annotation}[Unit check: $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3 = 2\RS/\speedoflight$]
Substituting $\thetaE^2 = (4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2)\,\Dds/(\Dd\Ds)$, all distances
cancel and the prefactor is $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3$ (A7a).  With
$\RS = 2\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^2$, this is $2\RS/\speedoflight$, not $\RS/\speedoflight$ (A7b).
For $M=10^{12}\,\Msun$, $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3\approx1.97\times10^7$\,s
$\approx228$\,d (A7c).  The prefactor $(1+z_d)\,4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3\,\Delta\tau$
with $z_d=0.3$, $y=0.5$ gives $\Delta t\approx2.59\times10^7$\,s $\approx300$\,d
(A7d), the value in Example~\ref{ex:point_mass_numerical}.  Writing the prefactor
as $\RS/\speedoflight$ would halve it (A7e).  To four decimals the ratio in the
logarithm is $1.6404$, so the log is $0.4949$ and $\Delta\tau(0.5)=1.0103$ (A17b).
In this form the distances cancel, so a point-mass delay measures $M$
independently of cosmology.
\end{annotation}

\begin{example}
\label{ex:point_mass_numerical}
\textbf{Numerical time delay for a galaxy-mass point lens.}

Consider a lens mass $M = 10^{12}\,\Msun$ at $z_d = 0.3$ with a source
at $\beta = 0.5\,\thetaE$ ($y = 0.5$).  The Schwarzschild radius is:
\begin{equation*}
    \RS = \frac{2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times
    10^{12} \times 1.989 \times 10^{30}}{(2.998 \times 10^8)^2}
    \approx 2.95 \times 10^{15}~\text{m}
    \approx 0.096~\text{pc} \,.
\end{equation*}
For $y = 0.5$: $\sqrt{y^2 + 4} = \sqrt{4.25} \approx 2.0616$.
\begin{align*}
    \Delta\tau &= \frac{0.5 \times 2.0616}{2}
    + \ln\!\left(\frac{2.0616 + 0.5}{2.0616 - 0.5}\right) \\
    &= 0.5154 + \ln(1.6404)
    = 0.5154 + 0.4949 = 1.0103 \,.
\end{align*}
The time delay is:
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta t = \frac{2 \times 2.95 \times 10^{15}}{2.998 \times 10^8}
    \times 1.3 \times 1.0103
    \approx 2.59 \times 10^{7}~\text{s}
    \approx 300~\text{days} \,.
\end{equation*}
This is a realistic order of magnitude --- observed time delays in
galaxy-scale lens systems range from days to a year or so.  Computed and
verified in
\mathlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/time_delay_function.wl}{time\_delay\_function.wl}.
\end{example}

    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{../Figures/06_Fermat_Time_Delays/time_delay_vs_beta.pdf}
    \caption{Dimensionless time delay $\Delta\tau$ between the two images
    of a point mass lens as a function of the source position
    $y = \beta / \thetaE$.  The time delay increases with the source

\section{Example: SIS Time Delay}
\label{sec:sis_time_delay}

As a preview for Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models} (axially symmetric lens models), we state the
time delay for the \textbf{singular isothermal sphere} (SIS).  The SIS
is characterized by its velocity dispersion $\sigma_v$, and its Einstein
radius is $\thetaE = 4\pi\,(\sigma_v/\speedoflight)^2\,\Dds/\Ds$.

For a source inside the Einstein ring ($\beta < \thetaE$), the SIS
produces two images at positions $\theta_\pm = \beta \pm \thetaE$
(both on the same side of the lens for $\theta_+$, opposite for
$\theta_-$).  The lensing potential is
$\psiL(\vectheta) = \thetaE\,|\vectheta|$, and the time delay between
the two images is:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Delta t_{\mathrm{SIS}} = \frac{(1 + z_d)}{\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\,
    \frac{\thetaE^2}{2}\left(
    \frac{\theta_+^2 - \theta_-^2}{\thetaE^2}
    \right)
    = \frac{(1 + z_d)}{2\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\,
    \left(\theta_+^2 - \theta_-^2\right)
    }
    \label{eq:time_delay_SIS}
\end{equation}
Since $\theta_+^2 - \theta_-^2 = (\theta_+ + \theta_-)(\theta_+ - \theta_-)
= 2\beta \cdot 2\thetaE = 4\beta\,\thetaE$, this simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t_{\mathrm{SIS}} = \frac{2(1 + z_d)}{\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\, \beta\,\thetaE \,.
    \label{eq:time_delay_SIS_simple}
\end{equation}
For the SIS, the time delay is proportional to the source position ---
the further the source from the optical axis, the larger the time delay.
This simple relation makes the SIS a useful ``first approximation'' for
time-delay estimates.

\begin{derivation}[$\tau$ at the two SIS images]
With $\psiL = \thetaE|\theta|$ on the axis and $0<\beta<\thetaE$:
\begin{align*}
  \tau(\theta_+) &= \half\thetaE^2-\thetaE(\beta+\thetaE) = -\half\thetaE^2-\beta\thetaE,\\
  \tau(\theta_-) &= \half\thetaE^2-\thetaE(\thetaE-\beta) = -\half\thetaE^2+\beta\thetaE,
\end{align*}
using $|\theta_+-\beta| = |\theta_--\beta| = \thetaE$ and $|\theta_-| = \thetaE-\beta$.
The geometric terms are equal and cancel, so the whole delay comes from the
potential: $\Delta\tau = 2\beta\thetaE = \half(\theta_+^2-\theta_-^2)$
(A8a--c).  Both images lie at the same distance $\thetaE$ from the source
position.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[SIS: $\sigma_v$, $\thetaE$, and the factor $\Dds/\Ds$]
$\thetaE = 4\pi(\sigma_v/\speedoflight)^2\,\Dds/\Ds$ contains the ratio
$\Dds/\Ds$ only, with no separate $\Dd$, unlike the point-mass
$\thetaE\propto\sqrt{\Dds/(\Dd\Ds)}$.  A handy normalisation is
$\alphahat = 1.4''\,(\sigma_v/220\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})^2$
\citep[eq.~44]{narayan_lectures_1997} (A12).  Then $\thetaE$ is that number
times $\Dds/\Ds<1$.  Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models} derives all of this.
\end{annotation}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:fermat_summary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{time delay function}
          $t(\vectheta) \propto \tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta)$
          gives the arrival time of a photon passing through the lens
          plane at $\vectheta$.  The \textbf{Fermat potential}
          $\tau = \half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2 - \psiL(\vectheta)$
          combines the geometric delay (extra path length) and the
          Shapiro delay (gravitational time delay from the lensing
          potential).

    \item \textbf{Fermat's principle}: images form at stationary points
          of $\tau(\vectheta)$.  The condition $\nabla_{\vectheta}\tau = 0$
          recovers the lens equation
          $\vecbeta = \vectheta - \nabla\psiL = \vectheta - \vecalpha$.

    \item Images are classified by \textbf{Morse theory}:
          \textbf{Type~I} (minimum, positive parity, arrives first),
          \textbf{Type~II} (saddle, negative parity),
          \textbf{Type~III} (maximum, positive parity, arrives last).
          \textbf{Burke's theorem}: the number of images is always odd
          for a transparent lens.

    \item The \textbf{arrival-time surface} $\tau(\theta_1, \theta_2)$
          visualizes the image-forming process.  For a point mass lens,
          it has a minimum and a saddle point (plus a singular maximum
          at the lens center).

    \item The \textbf{time delay between images}
          $\Delta t_{ij} \propto (1+z_d)\,\Dd\Ds/\Dds \times
          [\tau(\vectheta_i) - \tau(\vectheta_j)]$
          is directly observable by monitoring variable sources.

    \item $\Delta t \propto \Hubble^{-1}$: measuring time delays and
          modeling the lens potential gives $\Hubble$ independently of
          the distance ladder.  This is \textbf{time-delay cosmography},
          implemented by programs such as H0LiCOW/TDCOSMO.

    \item For a point mass lens, the dimensionless time delay is
          $\Delta\tau = y\sqrt{y^2+4}/2 + \ln[(\sqrt{y^2+4}+y)/(\sqrt{y^2+4}-y)]$,
          where $y = \beta/\thetaE$.  The physical time delay for a
          galaxy-mass lens is of order days to a year or so.

    \item For the SIS, $\Delta t_{\mathrm{SIS}} \propto \beta\,\thetaE$,
          a simple linear dependence on the source position.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problems}
\label{sec:fermat_problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:fermat_lens_eq}
\textbf{(Deriving the lens equation from Fermat's principle.)}
Starting from the Fermat potential
$\tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta) = \half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2
- \psiL(\vectheta)$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Compute the gradient $\nabla_{\vectheta}\,\tau$.
    \item Set $\nabla_{\vectheta}\,\tau = 0$ and show that this gives
          the lens equation
          $\vecbeta = \vectheta - \nabla\psiL(\vectheta)
          = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta)$.
    \item Explain physically why images form at stationary points of
          the arrival time.
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\solnlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/problems\_06.wl}{problems\_06.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:point_mass_arrival}
\textbf{(Arrival times for a point mass lens.)}
Consider a point mass lens with Einstein radius $\thetaE = 1''$ and a
source at $\beta = 0.5\,\thetaE$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Find the two image positions $\theta_+$ and $\theta_-$.
    \item Compute the Fermat potential $\tau$ at each image position
          using $\tau(x) = \half(x - y)^2 - \ln|x|$ with $y = 0.5$,
          where $x = \theta / \thetaE$.
    \item Compute the dimensionless time delay
          $\Delta\tau = \tau(x_-) - \tau(x_+)$.
    \item For a lens mass $M = 10^{12}\,\Msun$ at $z_d = 0.3$, compute
          the physical time delay $\Delta t$ in days.
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\solnlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/problems\_06.wl}{problems\_06.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:typeI_first}
\textbf{(Type~I image arrives first.)}
For a point mass lens with source at $\beta > 0$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Show that $\tau(\theta_+) < \tau(\theta_-)$, i.e., the
          minimum-image (Type~I) has a smaller value of $\tau$ than the
          saddle-image (Type~II).
    \item Verify this by computing $\tau(\theta_\pm)$ for
          $\beta = 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0$ (in units of $\thetaE$) and
          checking that $\tau(\theta_+) < \tau(\theta_-)$ in all cases.
    \item Explain why this implies the Type~I image arrives
          \textit{before} the Type~II image.
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\solnlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/problems\_06.wl}{problems\_06.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:H0_from_time_delay}
\textbf{(Measuring $\Hubble$ from time delays.)}
A multiply-imaged quasar has a measured time delay $\Delta t = 100$~days
between two images.  The lens model gives a dimensionless Fermat
potential difference of $\tau_1 - \tau_2 = 2.0~\text{arcsec}^2$.  The
lens is at $z_d = 0.3$ and the source is at $z_s = 1.0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Using the relation
          $\Delta t = (1+z_d)/\speedoflight \times \Dd\Ds/\Dds
          \times (\tau_1 - \tau_2)$,
          solve for $\Dd\Ds/\Dds$.
    \item For a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology with $\Omegam = 0.3$,
          show that $\Dd\Ds/\Dds \propto 1/\Hubble$ and compute the
          proportionality constant (hint: use the dimensionless distance
          ratios from Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}).
    \item Determine $\Hubble$ in km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$.  Compare
          with the H0LiCOW result and the Planck value.
\end{enumerate}
\textit{This is a simplified version of the analysis performed in real
time-delay cosmography.}
Verify with
\solnlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/problems\_06.wl}{problems\_06.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:arrival_surface_plot}
\textbf{(Plotting the arrival-time surface.)}
Using Mathematica (or your preferred software):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Plot the arrival-time surface $\tau(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ for a
          point mass lens with source at
          $\vecbeta = (0.3, 0)\,\thetaE$.  Use $\thetaE = 1$.
    \item Identify the two stationary points on the surface.  Label them
          as ``minimum'' and ``saddle.''
    \item Make a contour plot of $\tau$ and overlay the critical curve
          (the Einstein ring $|\vectheta| = \thetaE$).
    \item Verify that the stationary points agree with the analytical
          image positions $\theta_\pm$ from the lens equation.
\end{enumerate}
See
\mathlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/arrival_time_surface.nb}{arrival\_time\_surface.nb}
and
\solnlink{06_Fermat_Time_Delays/problems\_06.wl}{problems\_06.wl}.
\end{exercise}
