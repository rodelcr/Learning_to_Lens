\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:fermat_lens_eq}: Deriving the Lens Equation from Fermat's Principle}

(a) The Fermat potential is
$\tau(\vectheta, \vecbeta) = \half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2
- \psiL(\vectheta)$.  Taking the gradient with respect to $\vectheta$:
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla_{\vectheta}\,\tau
    = \nabla_{\vectheta}\!\left[\half|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2\right]
    - \nabla_{\vectheta}\,\psiL(\vectheta)
    = (\vectheta - \vecbeta) - \nabla\psiL(\vectheta) \,.
\end{equation*}
The first term follows from
$\nabla_{\vectheta}|\vectheta - \vecbeta|^2 = 2(\vectheta - \vecbeta)$.

\begin{derivation}[The gradient of $|\vectheta-\vecbeta|^2$]
$|\vectheta-\vecbeta|^2 = (\theta_1-\beta_1)^2+(\theta_2-\beta_2)^2$.  Then
$\partial_{\theta_1}$ of this is $2(\theta_1-\beta_1)$ and $\partial_{\theta_2}$ is
$2(\theta_2-\beta_2)$; $\vecbeta$ is a fixed parameter.  Collecting gives
$2(\vectheta-\vecbeta)$, and the $\half$ in $\tau$ cancels the 2.  For the
point mass ($\thetaE=1$), $\partial_{\theta_1}\tau = \theta_1-\beta_1-\theta_1/|\vectheta|^2$,
which is the component form of $\vecbeta = \vectheta-\vectheta/|\vectheta|^2$
(\texttt{problems\_06.wl}, Exercise 6.1 block and A3a).
\end{derivation}

(b) Setting $\nabla_{\vectheta}\,\tau = 0$:
\begin{equation*}
    (\vectheta - \vecbeta) - \nabla\psiL(\vectheta) = 0
    \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
    \vecbeta = \vectheta - \nabla\psiL(\vectheta) \,.
\end{equation*}
Since $\nabla\psiL(\vectheta) = \vecalpha(\vectheta)$ (the reduced
deflection angle), this is the lens equation
$\vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta)$.  \hfill $\square$

(c) Fermat's principle states that light follows
paths of stationary travel time.  Among all possible positions
$\vectheta$ in the lens plane through which a photon could pass, only
those for which the arrival time is stationary (not changing to first
order under small displacements $\delta\vectheta$) correspond to actual
light paths.  These are the image positions.  The geometric delay
(longer path) and gravitational delay (Shapiro effect) compete, and
images form where their combined effect is stationary.

\begin{annotation}[A second way to see (c)]
Regard $\tau$ as the phase of a wave, $\propto\nu\,t(\vectheta)$.  In the
Fresnel--Kirchhoff picture every point of the lens plane contributes, and
contributions cancel by rapid phase oscillation except near stationary
points, where the phase changes only at second order.  The stationary-phase
points are the geometric-optics images, and the second-order (Hessian)
term sets their amplitudes.  That Hessian is $\Amat$ (main text,
eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian_tau}), which is why $|\mu| = 1/|\det\Amat|$ is
consistent with this picture.
\end{annotation}

% ----- Exercise 6.2 -----
\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:point_mass_arrival}: Arrival Times for a Point Mass Lens}

(a) With $\thetaE = 1''$ and $\beta = 0.5''$, the image
positions are:
\begin{align*}
    \theta_+ &= \frac{0.5 + \sqrt{0.25 + 4}}{2}
    = \frac{0.5 + 2.0616}{2} = 1.2808'' \,, \\
    \theta_- &= \frac{0.5 - 2.0616}{2} = -0.7808'' \,.
\end{align*}

(b) In units of $\thetaE$ ($x = \theta/\thetaE$, $y = 0.5$):
$x_+ = 1.2808$, $x_- = -0.7808$.
\begin{align*}
    \tau(x_+) &= \half(1.2808 - 0.5)^2 - \ln(1.2808) \\
    &= \half(0.7808)^2 - 0.2475
    = 0.3048 - 0.2475 = 0.0573 \,, \\
    \tau(x_-) &= \half(-0.7808 - 0.5)^2 - \ln(0.7808) \\
    &= \half(1.2808)^2 - (-0.2475)
    = 0.8202 + 0.2475 = 1.0677 \,.
\end{align*}

(c) The dimensionless time delay is:
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta\tau = \tau(x_-) - \tau(x_+) = 1.0677 - 0.0573 \approx 1.0103 \,.
\end{equation*}
(The unrounded values give $1.01032$; the last digit of the rounded
difference is not significant.)
This matches the analytic formula:
$\Delta\tau = y\sqrt{y^2+4}/2 + \ln[(\sqrt{y^2+4}+y)/(\sqrt{y^2+4}-y)]
= 0.5154 + 0.4949 = 1.0103$.  \checkmark

\begin{derivation}[Shortcut for (b)--(c)]
Because $x_+ + x_- = y$, we have $x_+-y = -x_-$ and $x_--y = -x_+$.  The
geometric term at one image is the squared position of the other image:
$\half(x_+-y)^2 = \half x_-^2 = 0.3048$ and $\half(x_--y)^2 = \half x_+^2 = 0.8202$.
Also $x_+x_- = -1$, so $\ln|x_-| = -\ln x_+$: the two logarithms are
equal and opposite.  Hence
$\Delta\tau = \half(x_+^2-x_-^2)+2\ln x_+$, which is the analytic formula
term by term (\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A6a--e).  To four decimals,
$\ln x_+ = \ln 1.28078 = 0.2475$, so $2\ln x_+ = 0.4949$,
$\tau(x_+) = 0.0573$, and $\Delta\tau = 0.5154+0.4949 = 1.0103$ (A17a,b).
\end{derivation}

(d) For $M = 10^{12}\,\Msun$, $z_d = 0.3$:
\begin{align*}
    \RS &= \frac{2\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2}
    = \frac{2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times
    1.989 \times 10^{42}}{(2.998 \times 10^8)^2}
    \approx 2.953 \times 10^{15}~\text{m} \,, \\
    \Delta t &= \frac{2\RS}{\speedoflight}\,(1 + z_d)\,\Delta\tau
    = \frac{2 \times 2.953 \times 10^{15}}{2.998 \times 10^8}
    \times 1.3 \times 1.0103 \\
    &\approx 2.587 \times 10^{7}~\text{s}
    \approx 299.5~\text{days} \,.
\end{align*}
The prefactor is $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3 = 2\RS/\speedoflight$, not
$\RS/\speedoflight$; an earlier version of this solution dropped the factor 2
and quoted $149.7$~days.

\begin{annotation}[Factor of 2 in (d)]
The prefactor is $(1+z_d)\,\Dd\Ds\thetaE^2/(\speedoflight\Dds) = (1+z_d)\,4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3$,
and $4\Gnewton M/\speedoflight^3 = 2\RS/\speedoflight$ (\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A7a,b).
With the correct factor,
$\Delta t = 1.97\times10^7\,\mathrm{s}\times1.3\times1.0103\approx2.59\times10^7$\,s
$\approx300$\,d (A7d).  Using $\RS/\speedoflight$ instead would give a value
low by a factor 2 (A7e).  A quick way to catch this kind of slip: $\RS/\speedoflight$ for $10^{12}\,\Msun$ is $\approx114$\,d, so any
point-mass delay with $\Delta\tau\sim1$ and $z_d\sim0.3$ must be a few hundred
days.
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:typeI_first}: Type~I Image Arrives First}

(a) For a point mass lens, $\tau(x) = \half(x - y)^2 - \ln|x|$.
The image at $x_+$ is the primary image (minimum of $\tau$), and
$x_-$ is the secondary image (saddle point).  Since $\tau\to+\infty$
both as $|\vectheta|\to\infty$ and as $\vectheta\to0$, $\tau$ has a global
minimum, which must be a stationary point of minimum type.  The only such
point is $x_+$, so $\tau(x_+)<\tau(x_-)$ for any $y>0$.  (Being a local
minimum alone would not be enough: in general a minimum need not lie below
a saddle elsewhere on the surface.)

More explicitly: $\Delta\tau = y\sqrt{y^2+4}/2 +
\ln[(\sqrt{y^2+4}+y)/(\sqrt{y^2+4}-y)]$.  Both terms are positive for
$y > 0$: the first term is manifestly positive, and the argument of the
logarithm exceeds 1 since $\sqrt{y^2+4} + y > \sqrt{y^2+4} - y > 0$.
Therefore $\Delta\tau > 0$, i.e., $\tau(x_-) > \tau(x_+)$.
\hfill $\square$

\begin{annotation}[Is ``by definition'' enough?]
The explicit formula gives a second, independent proof.  An even shorter
route: $\Delta\tau(0) = 0$ and $d\Delta\tau/dy = \sqrt{y^2+4}>0$
(\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A6h,i), so $\Delta\tau>0$ for every $y>0$.
\end{annotation}

(b) Numerical verification:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rcccc}
\toprule
$y = \beta/\thetaE$ & $\tau(x_+)$ & $\tau(x_-)$ & $\Delta\tau$ \\
\midrule
0.1  & $0.4025$  & $0.6025$  & $0.2001$ \\
0.5  & $0.0573$  & $1.0677$  & $1.0103$ \\
1.0  & $-0.2902$ & $1.7902$  & $2.0805$ \\
2.0  & $-0.7956$ & $3.7956$  & $4.5912$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In all cases $\tau(x_+) < \tau(x_-)$.  \checkmark\ (An earlier version of
this table had wrong entries in the $y=0.1$, $1$ and $2$ rows; the values
above follow from $\tau(x)=\half(x-y)^2-\ln|x|$ at $x_\pm$.)

(c) Since $t(\vectheta) \propto \tau(\vectheta)$ (with a
positive proportionality constant), a smaller $\tau$ means a smaller
arrival time.  Therefore the Type~I image (minimum of $\tau$) arrives
before the Type~II image (saddle point of $\tau$).  The time delay
between them is $\Delta t \propto \Delta\tau > 0$.

\begin{annotation}[Checking the table in (b)]
The rows follow from $\tau(x) = \half(x-y)^2-\ln|x|$ at
$x_\pm = (y\pm\sqrt{y^2+4})/2$ (\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A17d).
A quick consistency test catches wrong rows:
$\Delta\tau/y$ should start at 2 and grow slowly, and here it is $2.001$,
$2.021$, $2.080$, $2.296$.  As $y\to0$, $\Delta\tau\to2y$, which matches the
SIS result in units of $\thetaE^2$ (A6j, A8d).  At $y\geq1$ one finds
$\tau(x_+)<0$; the sign of $\tau$ has no meaning, only differences do.
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:H0_from_time_delay}: Measuring $\Hubble$ from Time Delays}

(a) From the time delay formula:
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta t = \frac{(1 + z_d)}{\speedoflight}\,
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}\, (\tau_1 - \tau_2) \,.
\end{equation*}
Converting $\tau_1 - \tau_2 = 2.0~\text{arcsec}^2$ to radians:
$2.0 \times (1/206265)^2 \approx 4.70 \times 10^{-11}~\text{rad}^2$.
Converting $\Delta t = 100$~days $= 8.640 \times 10^6$~s.
With $z_d = 0.3$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds}
    = \frac{\Delta t \cdot \speedoflight}{(1 + z_d) \cdot (\tau_1 - \tau_2)}
    = \frac{8.640 \times 10^6 \times 2.998 \times 10^8}
    {1.3 \times 4.70 \times 10^{-11}}
    \approx 4.24 \times 10^{25}~\text{m} \,.
\end{equation*}
Converting to Mpc: $4.24 \times 10^{25} / 3.086 \times 10^{22}
\approx 1374$~Mpc.

\begin{annotation}[Units in (a)]
$\tau$ must be in rad$^2$: $2.0''^{\,2} = 2.0/206265^2 = 4.70\times10^{-11}$\,rad$^2$
(\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A10a).  If $\tau$ is left in arcsec$^2$ the
``distance'' comes out $4\times10^{10}$ times too small.  The result is the
time-delay distance divided by $(1+z_d)$; for orientation, a fiducial
$\Hubble=70$ cosmology gives $(1+z_d)\Dd\Ds/\Dds\approx1870$\,Mpc at these
redshifts (A15), close to $1.3\times1374 = 1786$\,Mpc.  The $\approx5\%$
difference is what moves $\Hubble$ from 70 to $\approx73$.
\end{annotation}

(b) For a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology with $\Omegam = 0.3$, the
distances scale as $D \propto \speedoflight / \Hubble$.  Writing the
dimensionless comoving distance $\chi(z) = \int_0^z dz'/E(z')$ with
$E(z) = \sqrt{\Omegam(1+z)^3 + \OmegaL}$, numerical integration gives
$\chi_d \approx 0.279$, $\chi_s \approx 0.771$, $\chi_{ds} \approx 0.493$.
Combining with $\Dd = (\speedoflight/\Hubble)\chi_d/(1+z_d)$,
$\Ds = (\speedoflight/\Hubble)\chi_s/(1+z_s)$, and
$\Dds = (\speedoflight/\Hubble)\chi_{ds}/(1+z_s)$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\Dd\,\Ds}{\Dds} = \frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble}\,
    \frac{\chi_d\,\chi_s}{(1+z_d)\,\chi_{ds}}
    = \frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble} \times \frac{0.279 \times 0.771}
        {1.3 \times 0.493}
    \approx \frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble} \times 0.336 \,.
\end{equation*}

\begin{derivation}[Where the $(1+z)$ factors go]
\[
  \frac{\Dd\Ds}{\Dds} = \frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble}\;
  \frac{\chi_d}{1+z_d}\cdot\frac{\chi_s}{1+z_s}\cdot\frac{1+z_s}{\chi_{ds}}
  = \frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble}\,\frac{\chi_d\chi_s}{(1+z_d)\chi_{ds}}
\]
(\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A10g).  The formula $\Dds = (\speedoflight/\Hubble)\chi_{ds}/(1+z_s)$
with $\chi_{ds} = \chi_s-\chi_d$ holds only for $\Omega_k=0$ (A10b).
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[More digits]
Direct integration gives $\chi_d = 0.279$, $\chi_s = 0.771$,
$\chi_{ds} = 0.493$, so the dimensionless factor is $0.336$
(\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A10d), and the final answer is
$\Hubble = 73.3$\,km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$ (A10e).  As a cross-check of
$\Dds\neq\Ds-\Dd$: in units of $\speedoflight/\Hubble$, $\Dd = 0.215$,
$\Ds = 0.386$, $\Dds = 0.246$, whereas $\Ds-\Dd = 0.171$.
\end{annotation}

(c) Combining:
\begin{equation*}
    \Hubble = \frac{\speedoflight \times 0.336}
    {\Dd\,\Ds/\Dds}
    = \frac{2.998 \times 10^8 \times 0.336}{4.24 \times 10^{25}}
    \approx 2.38 \times 10^{-18}~\text{s}^{-1} \,.
\end{equation*}
Converting to km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$:
\begin{equation*}
    \Hubble \approx 2.38 \times 10^{-18} \times 3.086 \times 10^{19}
    \approx 73~\text{km\,s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} \,.
\end{equation*}

\begin{remark}
This simplified single-lens, single-time-delay calculation gives
$\Hubble \approx 73$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$, which sits squarely on
the H0LiCOW/TDCOSMO value
$\Hubble = 73.3^{+1.7}_{-1.8}$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$ and the local
distance-ladder SH0ES result
$\Hubble = 73.0 \pm 1.0$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$, but is in tension with
the Planck CMB value $\Hubble \approx 67.4$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$.
The agreement here is partly a matter of the chosen numbers: the
$\Delta t$ and $\tau_1 - \tau_2$ correspond to a representative
realistic lens system.  Real time-delay cosmography combines many such
systems with self-consistent lens modeling and mass-sheet-degeneracy
breaks.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:arrival_surface_plot}: Plotting the Arrival-Time Surface}

\textbf{(a)--(b)} The arrival-time surface
$\tau(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ for a point mass lens with
$\vecbeta = (0.3, 0)\,\thetaE$ and $\thetaE = 1$ is:
\begin{equation*}
    \tau(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \half\left[(\theta_1 - 0.3)^2 + \theta_2^2\right]
    - \ln\sqrt{\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2} \,.
\end{equation*}
The two stationary points are the image positions:
\begin{align*}
    \theta_+ &= \frac{0.3 + \sqrt{0.09 + 4}}{2} = 1.1612 \quad
    \text{(minimum of $\tau$)} \,, \\
    \theta_- &= \frac{0.3 - \sqrt{0.09 + 4}}{2} = -0.8612 \quad
    \text{(saddle point of $\tau$)} \,.
\end{align*}

(c) The contour plot shows nested contours around the minimum
at $(\theta_+, 0) = (1.1612, 0)$, and a saddle pattern at
$(\theta_-, 0) = (-0.8612, 0)$.  The Einstein ring
$|\vectheta| = \thetaE = 1$ passes between the two stationary points,
as expected (the primary image is outside $\thetaE$, the secondary
inside).

(d) These match the analytical predictions from the lens
equation $\theta_\pm = (\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2})/2$
exactly.  \checkmark

\begin{annotation}[Hessian at the two stationary points]
On the axis the Hessian is $\mathrm{diag}(1+1/x^2,\,1-1/x^2)$ (main-text
derivation box).  At $x_+ = 1.1612$: eigenvalues $(1.742,\,0.258)$, both
positive, so a minimum.  At $x_- = -0.8612$: $(2.348,\,-0.348)$, opposite
signs, so a saddle (\texttt{problems\_06.wl} A16).  The small positive
eigenvalue $0.258$ at the minimum is the tangential one: the minimum image
is magnified mostly tangentially, as arcs are.
\end{annotation}
