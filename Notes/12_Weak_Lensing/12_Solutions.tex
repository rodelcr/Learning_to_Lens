\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_mst}: Reduced Shear and the Mass-Sheet Degeneracy}

(a) Under $\kappab' = \lambda\kappab + (1-\lambda)$,
$\gammaL' = \lambda\gammaL$, we have
$1 - \kappab' = 1 - \lambda\kappab - (1-\lambda) = \lambda(1-\kappab)$.
Hence
\begin{equation*}
    g' = \frac{\gammaL'}{1 - \kappab'}
    = \frac{\lambda\gammaL}{\lambda(1-\kappab)}
    = \frac{\gammaL}{1-\kappab} = g\,. \quad \checkmark
\end{equation*}

(b) The inverse magnification transforms as
\begin{equation*}
    (\mu')^{-1} = (1-\kappab')^2 - |\gammaL'|^2
    = \lambda^2(1-\kappab)^2 - \lambda^2|\gammaL|^2
    = \lambda^2\left[(1-\kappab)^2 - |\gammaL|^2\right]
    = \lambda^2\,\mu^{-1},
\end{equation*}
so $\mu' = \lambda^{-2}\mu$. \checkmark

(c) Galaxy ellipticities measure only the reduced shear $g$
(eq.~\ref{eq:wl_expectation_eps}), which by part (a) is invariant under the
transform.  A purely local shear measurement therefore cannot distinguish
$\kappab$ from $\lambda\kappab + (1-\lambda)$.  The degeneracy can be
broken with magnification information: since
$\mu \to \lambda^{-2}\mu$, the lensing-induced change in background number
counts, $n(>S)/n_0(>S) \approx 1 + 2(\alpha-1)\kappab$, fixes the absolute
scale.  (Broadly distributed source redshifts also break it mildly, since
no single $\lambda$ leaves $g$ invariant for all source redshifts
simultaneously.)

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_mst}(c), where $1 + 2(\alpha-1)\kappab$ comes from]
Lensing maps a patch of sky of area $\Omega$ onto an area $\mu\Omega$ and
brightens each source by $\mu$.  For counts $n_0(>S) \propto S^{-\alpha}$,
\begin{equation*}
    n(>S) = \frac{1}{\mu}\,n_0\!\left(>\frac{S}{\mu}\right)
    = \frac{1}{\mu}\,\mu^{\alpha}\,n_0(>S) = \mu^{\alpha-1}\,n_0(>S)\,.
\end{equation*}
In the weak limit, $\mu^{-1} = (1-\kappab)^2 - |\gammaL|^2 = 1 - 2\kappab +
\order{\kappab^2, \gammaL^2}$, so $\mu \simeq 1 + 2\kappab$.  The binomial
rule $(1+\epsilon)^p \simeq 1 + p\epsilon$ then gives
$n/n_0 \simeq 1 + 2(\alpha-1)\kappab$.  For $\alpha = 1$ the
counts do not respond to $\mu$ at all (the same cancellation as
Exercise~\ref{ex:bias_null}), so this route to $\lambda$ needs a source
population whose count slope is far from~1.
\end{derivation}

(d) A uniform sheet $\kappab(\vectheta) = 1-\lambda$ (constant)
has deflection $\vecalpha = \nabla\psiL$ with
$\nabla^2\psiL = 2\kappab = 2(1-\lambda)$, giving
$\vecalpha = (1-\lambda)\vectheta$ (a pure rescaling), and shear
$\gammaL = \tfrac{1}{2}(\psiL_{,11} - \psiL_{,22}) + \mathrm i\psiL_{,12}
= 0$: a uniform convergence produces no shear.  Adding it to a
lens contributes the constant $(1-\lambda)$ to $\kappab$ (hence ``mass
sheet'') while leaving the observable $g$ unchanged. \checkmark

\begin{annotation}[Reduced shear, shear, and what ``measuring the shear'' means]
Under the transform, $\gammaL' = \lambda\gammaL$: the shear itself
\emph{does} change.  Only $g$ is invariant, and only $g$ is measured.  Writing
``the measured shear $\gammaL$'' and then using $\gammaL = \bar\kappa -
\kappab$ hides the degeneracy.  It also hides a bias wherever $\kappab$ is not
small.  For an SIS at $\theta = 2\thetaE$, $\kappab = \gammaL = 1/4$ but
$g = (1/4)/(3/4) = 1/3$, so treating $g$ as $\gammaL$ there is a 33\% error.
That is the regime of cluster outskirts and of galaxy--galaxy lensing close to
the lens.
\end{annotation}

\begin{bridge}[The same transform in strong lensing: Chapter~\ref{ch:msd_kinematics}]
The transform of part~(a) acts on the deflection as
$\vecalpha' = \lambda\vecalpha + (1-\lambda)\vectheta$.  It sends the source
position $\vecbeta \to \lambda\vecbeta$ and leaves every image position and
flux \emph{ratio} unchanged.  So strong-lens image configurations share the
blind spot that weak-lensing shapes have through $g$.
Chapter~\ref{ch:msd_kinematics} follows this degeneracy into multi-plane
lenses and shows how stellar kinematics, which respond to the true $\kappab$
rather than to $g$, fix~$\lambda$.
\end{bridge}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_tangential}: Tangential Shear and the Projected Mass}

(a) The mean convergence inside $\theta$:
\begin{equation*}
    \bar\kappa(\theta) = \frac{2}{\theta^2}\int_0^\theta
    \frac{\thetaE}{2\theta'}\,\theta'\,d\theta'
    = \frac{2}{\theta^2}\cdot\frac{\thetaE}{2}\cdot\theta
    = \frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\,.
\end{equation*}
Then, from eq.~\eqref{eq:wl_mean_tangential},
\begin{equation*}
    \gammaL_{\mathrm t}(\theta) = \bar\kappa - \kappab
    = \frac{\thetaE}{\theta} - \frac{\thetaE}{2\theta}
    = \frac{\thetaE}{2\theta}\,. \quad \checkmark
\end{equation*}

(b) For a point mass, $\kappab = 0$ (away from the origin) but
$\bar\kappa(\theta) = M(\theta)/(\pi\Sigmacr\Dd^2\theta^2) =
\thetaE^2/\theta^2$, so
$\gammaL_{\mathrm t} = \bar\kappa - 0 = \thetaE^2/\theta^2$.  The point-mass
tangential shear falls as $\theta^{-2}$ (its projected mass is fixed),
whereas the SIS falls only as $\theta^{-1}$ because the SIS keeps
accumulating projected mass linearly with radius
($M_{\mathrm{2D}} \propto \theta$).

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_tangential}(b), why $\bar\kappa = \thetaE^2/\theta^2$]
For a point mass the projected mass inside any $\theta > 0$ is all of $M$,
so $\bar\kappa = M/(\pi\Dd^2\theta^2\Sigmacr)$.  Insert
$\Sigmacr = \speedoflight^2\Ds/(4\pi\Gnewton\Dd\Dds)$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{M}{\pi\Dd^2\Sigmacr} = \frac{4\Gnewton M}{\speedoflight^2}\,
    \frac{\Dds}{\Dd\Ds} = \thetaE^2\,,
\end{equation*}
which is the point-mass Einstein radius of Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}.
\end{derivation}

(c) Multiply eq.~\eqref{eq:wl_mean_tangential} by $\Sigmacr$ and
use $\kappab = \Sigma/\Sigmacr$, $\bar\kappa = \bar\Sigma/\Sigmacr$:
\begin{equation*}
    \gammaL_{\mathrm t}(\theta)\,\Sigmacr
    = \Sigmacr\left[\bar\kappa(\theta) - \langle\kappab\rangle(\theta)\right]
    = \bar\Sigma(<\theta) - \Sigma(\theta)
    \equiv \Delta\Sigma(\theta)\,. \quad \checkmark
\end{equation*}
The observable tangential shear directly gives the excess surface mass
density, independent of the mass-sheet constant, since a uniform sheet
shifts $\bar\Sigma$ and $\Sigma$ equally and cancels in the difference.

(d) For the NFW halo,
$\gammaL_{\mathrm t}(x) = \bar\kappa(x) - \kappab(x)$ with
$\bar\kappa = (2\kappa_s/x^2)[\ln(x/2) + g(x)]$ and
$\kappab = \kappa_s f(x)$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models},
eq.~\ref{eq:nfw_gamma}), where $x = \theta/\theta_s$.  As $x \to 0$ both
$\kappab$ and $\bar\kappa$ diverge only logarithmically (the NFW cusp
$\rho \propto r^{-1}$ is shallower than isothermal), and the divergences
cancel in the difference: $\gammaL_{\mathrm t} \to \kappa_s/2$, a finite
central value, unlike the SIS, which diverges as $\theta^{-1}$.  At large
$x$ the density falls as $r^{-3}$, the projected mass grows only
logarithmically, and
$\gammaL_{\mathrm t} \simeq (2\kappa_s/x^2)\left[\ln(x/2) - \tfrac12\right]$
steepens toward $\theta^{-2}$ (up to the logarithm).  So $\gammaL_{\mathrm t}$ decreases
monotonically.  Its logarithmic slope changes from $\approx 0$ inside $\theta_s$
(about $-0.05$ at $x = 0.1$) through about $-0.5$ at $x = 1$ to about $-1.4$ at
$x = 10$.  This bend near the scale radius is the signature that
distinguishes NFW from the single power law of the SIS.  It is a change of
slope, not a maximum (Fig.~\ref{fig:wl_tangential};
checked in \texttt{problems\_12.wl}).

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_tangential}(d), the two asymptotes]
For small $x$, Taylor-expand $g(x) = \operatorname{arccosh}(1/x)/
\sqrt{1-x^2} = \ln(2/x) + \order{x^2\ln x}$.  Then
$\kappab/\kappa_s = f(x) = (1-g)/(x^2-1) \to g - 1 = \ln(2/x) - 1$, and
(carrying the expansion of $\ln(x/2) + g$ to order $x^2$)
$\bar\kappa/\kappa_s \to \ln(2/x) - \tfrac12$.  Both diverge like $\ln(2/x)$.
Subtracting,
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\gammaL_{\mathrm t}}{\kappa_s}
    \to \left[\ln\frac2x - \frac12\right] - \left[\ln\frac2x - 1\right]
    = \frac12\,.
\end{equation*}
For large $x$, use $\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1} \to \pi/2 - 1/x$, so
$g(x) = \pi/(2x) - 1/x^2 + \order{x^{-3}}$ and $f(x) = 1/x^2 + \order{x^{-3}}$.
Hence $\bar\kappa/\kappa_s = (2/x^2)[\ln(x/2) + \order{1/x}]$ and
$\gammaL_{\mathrm t}/\kappa_s \simeq (2/x^2)\ln(x/2) - 1/x^2
= (2/x^2)[\ln(x/2) - \tfrac12]$, the form quoted.
\end{derivation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_ks}: The Kaiser--Squires Kernel}

(a) With $\hat{\mathcal D} =
\pi(\ell_1^2 - \ell_2^2 + 2\mathrm i\ell_1\ell_2)/|\boldsymbol\ell|^2$ and
its complex conjugate,
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{\mathcal D}\,\hat{\mathcal D}^*
    = \pi^2\,\frac{(\ell_1^2 - \ell_2^2)^2 + (2\ell_1\ell_2)^2}
    {|\boldsymbol\ell|^4}
    = \pi^2\,\frac{(\ell_1^2 + \ell_2^2)^2}{|\boldsymbol\ell|^4}
    = \pi^2\,, \quad \checkmark
\end{equation*}
using $(\ell_1^2 - \ell_2^2)^2 + 4\ell_1^2\ell_2^2 =
(\ell_1^2 + \ell_2^2)^2$ and $|\boldsymbol\ell|^2 = \ell_1^2 + \ell_2^2$.

(b) Multiply the forward relation
$\hat\gammaL = \pi^{-1}\hat{\mathcal D}\hat\kappab$ by
$\hat{\mathcal D}^*$:
$\hat\gammaL\hat{\mathcal D}^* = \pi^{-1}\hat{\mathcal D}\hat{\mathcal D}^*
\hat\kappab = \pi^{-1}\pi^2\hat\kappab = \pi\hat\kappab$, so
$\hat\kappab = \pi^{-1}\hat\gammaL\hat{\mathcal D}^*$.  Transforming back to
real space (a product of transforms becomes a convolution) yields
$\kappab(\vectheta) - \kappab_0 = \pi^{-1}\!\int d^2\theta'\,
\mathcal D^*(\vectheta - \vectheta')\,\gammaL(\vectheta')$, i.e.
eq.~\eqref{eq:wl_ks_inversion}. \checkmark

(c) $\hat{\mathcal D}(\boldsymbol\ell)$ is undefined at
$\boldsymbol\ell = 0$ (the ratio $0/0$), so the $\boldsymbol\ell = 0$ mode,
the sky-averaged (DC) value of $\kappab$, is left unconstrained by
the shear.  A uniform sheet produces no shear, and in this
linear ($\gammaL \approx g$) inversion $\kappab_0$ is such a
sheet.  Since only the reduced shear $g$ is actually observed, the
freedom becomes the full transform of Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_mst}, which
rescales $\kappab$ by $\lambda$ as well as adding the sheet $(1-\lambda)$.
In the non-linear inversion, eq.~(50) of
\citet[Part~3]{schneider_kochanek_wambsganss_2006}, the undetermined
constant is equivalent to $\lambda$.

(d) With $\hat{\mathcal D} = \pi e^{2\mathrm i\beta_\ell}$, the
shear Fourier mode is
$\hat\gammaL = \pi^{-1}\hat{\mathcal D}\hat\kappab =
e^{2\mathrm i\beta_\ell}\hat\kappab$.  Then
$\langle\hat\gammaL\hat\gammaL^*\rangle =
|e^{2\mathrm i\beta_\ell}|^2\langle\hat\kappab\hat\kappab^*\rangle =
\langle\hat\kappab\hat\kappab^*\rangle$, since
$|e^{2\mathrm i\beta_\ell}| = 1$.  Hence $P_\gamma(\ell) = P_\kappa(\ell)$:
the shear and convergence power spectra are identical. \checkmark

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_ellipticity}: Complex Ellipticity}

(a) With $Q_{12} = 0$, $Q_{11} \propto a^2$, $Q_{22} \propto b^2$
and $r = b/a$:
\begin{equation*}
    |\chi| = \frac{Q_{11} - Q_{22}}{Q_{11} + Q_{22}}
    = \frac{a^2 - b^2}{a^2 + b^2}
    = \frac{1 - r^2}{1 + r^2}\,.
\end{equation*}
For $\epsilon$ the denominator carries the extra term
$2\sqrt{Q_{11}Q_{22} - Q_{12}^2} = 2\sqrt{Q_{11}Q_{22}} \propto 2ab$, so
\begin{equation*}
    |\epsilon| = \frac{a^2 - b^2}{a^2 + b^2 + 2ab}
    = \frac{(a-b)(a+b)}{(a+b)^2}
    = \frac{a - b}{a + b}
    = \frac{1 - r}{1 + r}\,. \quad \checkmark
\end{equation*}

(b) From $|\chi| = (1-r^2)/(1+r^2)$ one finds
$\sqrt{1 - |\chi|^2} = 2r/(1+r^2)$, so
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{|\chi|}{1 + \sqrt{1-|\chi|^2}}
    = \frac{(1-r^2)/(1+r^2)}{1 + 2r/(1+r^2)}
    = \frac{1 - r^2}{(1+r^2) + 2r}
    = \frac{(1-r)(1+r)}{(1+r)^2}
    = \frac{1-r}{1+r} = |\epsilon|\,.
\end{equation*}
Since $\chi$ and $\epsilon$ share the same phase, the moduli relation
extends to the complex quantities:
$\epsilon = \chi/(1+\sqrt{1-|\chi|^2})$.  Inverting,
$\chi = 2\epsilon/(1+|\epsilon|^2)$ (check:
$2|\epsilon|/(1+|\epsilon|^2)$ with $|\epsilon| = (1-r)/(1+r)$ gives
$(1-r^2)/(1+r^2) = |\chi|$). \checkmark

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_ellipticity}(b), the ``one finds'' step]
Put $|\chi|$ over a common denominator:
\begin{equation*}
    1 - |\chi|^2 = \frac{(1+r^2)^2 - (1-r^2)^2}{(1+r^2)^2}
    = \frac{4r^2}{(1+r^2)^2}\,,
\end{equation*}
using the difference of squares $(1+r^2)^2 - (1-r^2)^2 = (2)(2r^2)$.  The
positive square root (since $0 < r \le 1$) is $2r/(1+r^2)$.
\end{derivation}

(c) As $r \to 1$ write $r = 1 - \delta$ with $\delta \ll 1$.
Then $|\epsilon| = \delta/(2-\delta) \approx \delta/2$ and
$|\chi| = (2\delta - \delta^2)/(2 - 2\delta + \delta^2) \approx \delta$,
so $|\chi| \approx 2|\epsilon|$.  With randomly oriented sources,
$\langle\epsilon\rangle = g \approx \gammaL$ in the weak limit
(eq.~\ref{eq:wl_weak_chain}), and therefore
$\langle\chi\rangle/2 \approx \langle\epsilon\rangle \approx \gammaL$.
\checkmark

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_ellipticity}(c), how good is $|\chi| \approx 2|\epsilon|$?]
Exactly, with $r = 1-\delta$: $|\epsilon| = \delta/(2-\delta)$ and
$|\chi| = (2\delta-\delta^2)/(2-2\delta+\delta^2)$.  Expanding both to second
order, $|\chi| = \delta + \delta^2/2 + \order{\delta^3}$ and
$2|\epsilon| = \delta + \delta^2/2 + \order{\delta^3}$.  They agree through
\emph{second} order, so the factor 2 is better than the leading-order
argument suggests.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Which ellipticity, which shear]
$\langle\epsilon\rangle = g$ holds exactly (for $|g| \le 1$) whenever the
intrinsic orientations are random, eq.~\eqref{eq:wl_expectation_eps}.
$\langle\chi\rangle \approx 2g$ holds only in the weak limit and for nearly
round sources: to first order $\langle\chi\rangle = 2g\,(1 -
\langle|\chi^{(s)}|^2\rangle/2)$, so the mean of $\chi$ depends on the
distribution of intrinsic ellipticities
(\citealp{schneider_kochanek_wambsganss_2006}, Part~3, p.~277).  Mixing the two definitions gives a
factor-2 error in the mass, and mixing $g$ with $\gammaL$ gives a
$(1-\kappab)^{-1}$ error.  Before using a catalogue, check its convention.
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_cosmic}: Cosmic-Shear Correlation Functions}

(a) Both $\xi_+$ and $\xi_-$ are Hankel transforms of the
same function $P_\kappa(\ell)$ (with $J_0$ and $J_4$
respectively, eq.~\ref{eq:wl_xipm}).  Since the shear is a spin-2 field
derived from a single scalar potential $\psiL$, its two correlation
functions are not independent: given $\xi_-$ on all scales one can recover
$P_\kappa$ (by the inverse Hankel transform) and hence $\xi_+$, and vice
versa.  Explicitly, $\xi_+(\theta) = \xi_-(\theta) + \int_\theta^\infty
(d\vartheta/\vartheta)\,\xi_-(\vartheta)\,[4 - 12\theta^2/\vartheta^2]$.

(b) Under a parity (reflection) transformation
$\gammaL_{\mathrm t} \to \gammaL_{\mathrm t}$ but
$\gammaL_\times \to -\gammaL_\times$.  The mixed correlator therefore
transforms as $\xi_\times = \langle\gammaL_{\mathrm t}\gammaL_\times\rangle
\to -\langle\gammaL_{\mathrm t}\gammaL_\times\rangle = -\xi_\times$.  Since
the statistical properties of the cosmological density field are
parity-invariant, $\xi_\times = -\xi_\times$, hence $\xi_\times = 0$.  A
measured non-zero $\xi_\times$ (or B-mode) must arise from systematics
(PSF residuals, intrinsic alignments, etc.), so it is a useful null
test. \checkmark

(c) With $P_\kappa(\ell) = A\ell^{-1}$,
\begin{equation*}
    \xi_\pm(\theta) = \int_0^\infty \frac{d\ell\,\ell}{2\pi}\,
    J_{0,4}(\ell\theta)\,\frac{A}{\ell}
    = \frac{A}{2\pi}\int_0^\infty J_{0,4}(\ell\theta)\,d\ell
    = \frac{A}{2\pi\theta}\,,
\end{equation*}
so $\xi_+ = \xi_- = A/(2\pi\theta)$, a power law.  (Check with the relation
of part (a): $\int_\theta^\infty (d\vartheta/\vartheta)\,
(A/2\pi\vartheta)(4 - 12\theta^2/\vartheta^2) = (A/2\pi)(4/\theta -
4/\theta) = 0$, so $\xi_+ = \xi_-$ as found.)  For the steeper
$P_\kappa = A\ell^{-2}$ the integrand becomes $J_0(\ell\theta)/\ell$, and
since $J_0 \to 1$ as $\ell \to 0$ the integral diverges logarithmically:
too much power on large scales.  A realistic $P_\kappa$ turns over at
small $\ell$, which keeps $\xi_\pm$ finite.  In general, a scale-free
$P_\kappa$ gives a scale-free (power-law) correlation function.
\checkmark

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:wl_cosmic}(c), the two integrals]
For the Bessel integral, substitute $x = \ell\theta$, $d\ell = dx/\theta$:
$\int_0^\infty J_\nu(\ell\theta)\,d\ell = \theta^{-1}\int_0^\infty
J_\nu(x)\,dx = 1/\theta$, using the standard result $\int_0^\infty J_\nu(x)\,dx
= 1$ for $\nu > -1$ (here $\nu = 0$ and $4$).  So $\xi_\pm = A/(2\pi\theta)$.

For the consistency integral, term by term,
$\int_\theta^\infty d\vartheta/\vartheta^2 = 1/\theta$ and
$\int_\theta^\infty d\vartheta/\vartheta^4 = 1/(3\theta^3)$, so
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{A}{2\pi}\left[\frac{4}{\theta} - 12\theta^2\cdot\frac{1}{3\theta^3}
    \right] = \frac{A}{2\pi}\left[\frac4\theta - \frac4\theta\right] = 0\,.
\end{equation*}
\end{derivation}

(d) The aperture-mass dispersion
$\langle M_{\mathrm{ap}}^2\rangle(\theta)$ is $P_\kappa$ filtered by
$W_{\mathrm{ap}}(\eta) \propto J_4^2(\eta)/\eta^4$, a very narrow,
strongly peaked filter, so at scale $\theta$ it samples $P_\kappa$ near a
single multipole $\ell \approx 4$--$5/\theta$ (the filter
$J_4^2(\eta)/\eta^4$ peaks at $\eta = \ell\theta \approx 4.1$).  The shear dispersion
$\langle|\bar\gammaL|^2\rangle(\theta)$ uses the top-hat filter
$W_{\mathrm{TH}}(\eta) = 4J_1^2(\eta)/\eta^2$, which is broad and mixes a
wide range of $\ell$.  Hence $\langle M_{\mathrm{ap}}^2\rangle$ reflects
the shape of the power spectrum much more directly. \checkmark
