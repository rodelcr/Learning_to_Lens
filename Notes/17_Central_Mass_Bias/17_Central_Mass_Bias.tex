\chapter{How an Unmodelled Central Mass Biases a Compound Lens}
\label{ch:central_mass_bias}

\noindent
A compound lens, one deflector with two background sources at different
redshifts, measures the distance ratio
$\Brat = \Dls{d}{s_1}\,D_{\mathrm{s}_2}/(D_{\mathrm{s}_1}\,\Dls{d}{s_2})$
through the ratio of its two Einstein radii
(Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}). Because $\Brat$ depends on the
expansion history but not on $\Hubble$, it can be used to constrain the
dark-energy equation of state \citep{collett_2012}. That depends on the
lens model being right. If the deflector contains a mass component the
model cannot represent, the fit makes up for it by shifting the
parameters it does have, and the shift propagates into $\Brat$.

This chapter works through the simplest case. Massive galaxies generally
host central supermassive black holes. A radial image, which is the
observable that constrains the inner slope of a power-law model
(Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass}), forms close to the centre,
where a black hole's deflection is largest. The question is: \emph{if a
power law plus a central point mass is fit with a power law alone, how
wrong is the recovered $\Brat$?}

We answer it in five steps:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item define the Einstein radius unambiguously when a point mass is
        present (\S\ref{sec:cb_theta_e});
  \item show that the point mass is a \emph{different} fractional
        perturbation on the two source planes, so its effect does not
        cancel in the ratio (\S\ref{sec:cb_differential});
  \item split the bias in $\Brat$ into a ratio channel and a slope
        channel, and derive a fixed-slope estimate of the ratio channel
        (\S\ref{sec:cb_channels});
  \item write the general linear-response formula for the parameter
        bias and test it against an exact power-law-only fit
        (\S\ref{sec:cb_linear});
  \item derive in closed form the central mass at which the radial
        image disappears (\S\ref{sec:cb_annihilation}).
\end{enumerate}
We then separate the hidden and detected regimes
(\S\ref{sec:cb_regimes}) and propagate the bias to the equation of state
$w$ (\S\ref{sec:cb_w}). All numbers in this chapter come from generic
illustrative configurations. How large the bias is for a particular
system is left to the Problems.

\section{A Power Law with a Central Point Mass, Seen from Two Source Planes}
\label{sec:cb_setup}

Throughout, the deflector is axisymmetric, so the lens equation is
one-dimensional (Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models},
eq.~\eqref{eq:lens_eq_1d}). We use a \emph{signed} image coordinate
$\theta\in\mathbb{R}$, so that images on opposite sides of the centre
have opposite signs. The signed source position is
\begin{equation}
  \ysrc_i(\theta) = \theta - \alpha_i(\theta),
  \label{eq:cb_lens_eq}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_i$ an odd function of $\theta$. The index $i\in\{1,2\}$
labels the near ($i=1$) and far ($i=2$) source planes. The deflector is
a power law of three-dimensional slope $\gp$ ($\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$)
plus a central point mass. For $\theta>0$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \alpha_i(\theta) =
  \underbrace{\big(\thetaE^{(i)}\big)^{\gp-1}\theta^{\,2-\gp}}_{\alpha_{\mathrm{PL},i}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{\frac{\big(\thetaBH^{(i)}\big)^2}{\theta}}_{\alpha_{\bullet,i}}
  \;}
  \label{eq:cb_alpha_i}
\end{equation}
Here $\thetaE^{(i)}$ is the \emph{normalisation of the power law} on
plane~$i$, the ring radius the power law would have on its own, and
$\thetaBH^{(i)}$ is the point mass's own Einstein radius
(eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_point}). The power-law term follows from the
convergence $\kappab(\theta) = \tfrac{3-\gp}{2}(\thetaE/\theta)^{\gp-1}$
through the mean-convergence relation $\alpha = \bar\kappa\,\theta$
(eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_mean_kappa}). The projection of $\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$
to $\kappab\propto\theta^{1-\gp}$ is Exercise~\ref{ex:power_law_profile}.

\begin{aside}[Why both terms scale the same way between planes]
In the weak-field metric, the physical deflection of a ray is set by
the deflector's potential along the line of sight
(Chapter~\ref{ch:light_deflection}). It does not depend on where the ray
started. The source distance enters only when the physical deflection
$\alphahat$ is converted to the reduced deflection,
$\alpha = (\Dds/\Ds)\,\alphahat$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}). A
power law and a black hole in the same deflector therefore pick up the
\emph{same} factor $\Dds/\Ds$ on each source plane, whatever their mass
profiles.
\end{aside}

Define the distance ratio
\begin{equation}
  \Brat \equiv
  \frac{\Dls{d}{s_1}\,D_{\mathrm{s}_2}}{D_{\mathrm{s}_1}\,\Dls{d}{s_2}}
  = \frac{\Dls{d}{s_1}/D_{\mathrm{s}_1}}{\Dls{d}{s_2}/D_{\mathrm{s}_2}}
  \;<\;1 .
  \label{eq:cb_B_def}
\end{equation}
It is below unity because the far source has the larger distance factor
$\Dds/\Ds$, which at fixed $z_{\rm d}$ is proportional to the lensing
efficiency of \S\ref{sec:lensing_efficiency}. The aside then
gives
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_2(\theta) = \frac{\alpha_1(\theta)}{\Brat}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \big(\thetaE^{(2)}\big)^{\gp-1} = \frac{\big(\thetaE^{(1)}\big)^{\gp-1}}{\Brat},
  \qquad
  \big(\thetaBH^{(2)}\big)^{2} = \frac{\big(\thetaBH^{(1)}\big)^{2}}{\Brat}.
  \label{eq:cb_alpha_scaling}
\end{equation}
Solving the first relation for the power-law normalisations,
\begin{equation}
  \thetaE^{(2)} = \thetaE^{(1)}\,\Brat^{-1/(\gp-1)},
  \qquad
  \etamea \equiv \frac{\thetaE^{(1)}}{\thetaE^{(2)}} = \Brat^{1/(\gp-1)}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\;\Brat = \etamea^{\,\gp-1}\;}
  \label{eq:cb_B_eta}
\end{equation}
This is the compound-lens identity of
Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}, specialised to a power law: the ratio
of Einstein radii, raised to the slope-dependent power $\gp-1$, gives
the cosmological observable. For an isothermal deflector ($\gp=2$) it
reduces to $\Brat=\etamea$. For $\Brat<1$ and $\gp>1$ we have
$\etamea<1$, so $\ln\etamea<0$, which matters in
\S\ref{sec:cb_channels}. \annot{Verified: \texttt{central\_mass\_bias.wl}, checks A1--A2d, A5a.}

\begin{bridge}[Back to Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}: where $\Brat$ comes from]
Each factor $\Dds/\Ds$ in eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_B_def} is the lensing
efficiency of \S\ref{sec:lensing_efficiency} divided by $\Dd$, which is
common to both planes. In a flat universe every
angular-diameter distance is a comoving distance divided by $1+z$
(eqs.~\eqref{eq:DA_flat} and~\eqref{eq:Dds_flat}). The $(1+z)$ factors
and $\Hubble$ cancel in the ratio, leaving
$\Brat = (1-\chi_{\mathrm{d}}/\chi_1)/(1-\chi_{\mathrm{d}}/\chi_2)$.
This is the expression we differentiate with respect to $w$ in
\S\ref{sec:cb_w}.
\end{bridge}

We make two simplifications. First, the near source is treated as
massless. In reality it also deflects the light of the far source; the
multi-plane treatment is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}. Second, axisymmetry isolates the
mechanism. Ellipticity and external shear add parameters, but the
effect comes from the radial profile of the deflection.

\section{What ``the Einstein Radius'' Means When a Central Mass Is Present}
\label{sec:cb_theta_e}

With a point mass present, ``the Einstein radius'' could mean two
different numbers: the power-law normalisation $\thetaE^{(i)}$, or the
radius of the ring the full lens actually forms. We measure the central
mass on plane $i$ by the dimensionless fraction
\begin{equation}
  \mBH_i \equiv \frac{\big(\thetaBH^{(i)}\big)^2}{\big(\thetaE^{(i)}\big)^2}.
  \label{eq:cb_m_def}
\end{equation}
The projected mass inside radius $\theta$ is
$\pi\Sigmacr\Dd^2\theta^2\bar\kappa(\theta)$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}), and the power law alone has
$\bar\kappa(\thetaE)=1$. So $\mBH_i$ is the black-hole mass divided by
the power-law mass inside the power-law Einstein radius.

\begin{keybox}[Convention: the total-mass Einstein radius]
The physical Einstein radius on plane~$i$ is the positive solution
$\theta_{\mathrm{E,tot}}^{(i)}$ of
\begin{equation}
  \theta = \alpha_{\mathrm{PL},i}(\theta) + \alpha_{\bullet,i}(\theta),
  \label{eq:cb_thetatot}
\end{equation}
that is, the ring radius of the \emph{full} lens, inside which the mean
convergence of power law plus point mass equals one. The true
Einstein-radius ratio $\etamea_{\rm true}$ is formed from these
total-mass radii. The bias that propagates to cosmology is measured by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta\Brat}{\Brat} \equiv
  \frac{\etamea_{\rm fit}^{\,\gp_{\rm fit}-1}}{\Brat_{\rm true}} - 1 ,
  \label{eq:cb_dBB_def}
\end{equation}
where $\etamea_{\rm fit}$ and $\gp_{\rm fit}$ come from a power-law-only
fit and $\Brat_{\rm true}$ is the true distance ratio.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_dBB_def} folds the error in the ratio and the error in
the slope into one number.
\end{keybox}

Why prefer the total-mass radius? A ring, or a pair of images close to
it, measures the mass \emph{enclosed} by the ring. By
eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_mean_kappa}, $\theta=\alpha(\theta)$ is the same
statement as $\bar\kappa(\theta)=1$, whatever the profile inside. A
power-law-only fit to images near the ring will therefore return a
normalisation close to $\theta_{\mathrm{E,tot}}$, \emph{not} the true
power-law normalisation $\thetaE$. The total-mass radius is what an
observer with no model in mind would call the Einstein radius.

To see how far apart the two radii are, divide
eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetatot} by $\theta^{2-\gp}$ and work in units of
$\thetaE$:
\begin{equation}
  \Big(\frac{\theta}{\thetaE}\Big)^{\gp-1}
  = 1 + \mBH\Big(\frac{\theta}{\thetaE}\Big)^{\gp-3}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\;
  \frac{\theta_{\mathrm{E,tot}}}{\thetaE}
  = 1 + \frac{\mBH}{\gp-1} + \frac{\gp-4}{2(\gp-1)^2}\,\mBH^2 + \order{\mBH^3}
  \;}
  \label{eq:cb_thetatot_series}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[The expansion of $\theta_{\mathrm{E,tot}}$]
Write $\theta/\thetaE = 1+\epsilon$ with $\epsilon = c_1\mBH + c_2\mBH^2$,
and expand both sides of
$(1+\epsilon)^{\gp-1} = 1 + \mBH(1+\epsilon)^{\gp-3}$ in powers of $\mBH$.

\emph{First order.} The left side gives $(\gp-1)c_1\mBH$; the right side
gives $\mBH$. Hence $c_1 = 1/(\gp-1)$.

\emph{Second order.} The left side gives
$(\gp-1)c_2 + \tfrac12(\gp-1)(\gp-2)c_1^2$; the right side gives
$(\gp-3)c_1$. Hence
$c_2 = \big[(\gp-3)c_1 - \tfrac12(\gp-1)(\gp-2)c_1^2\big]/(\gp-1)
= (\gp-4)/[2(\gp-1)^2]$.

\emph{Check at $\gp=2$.} The quadratic $\theta^2-\thetaE\theta-\thetaBH^2=0$
has the root
$\theta_{\mathrm{E,tot}} = \tfrac12\big(\thetaE+\sqrt{\thetaE^2+4\thetaBH^2}\big)
= \thetaE(1+\mBH-\mBH^2+\dots)$, which matches $c_1=1$, $c_2=-1$
(Exercise~\ref{ex:cb_sis_point_mass}).
\end{derivation}

For $\gp=1.9$ and $\mBH=0.01$, the exact root of
eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetatot} is $\theta_{\mathrm{E,tot}}=1.010984\,\thetaE$,
against $1.011111\,\thetaE$ from the first-order term. The point mass
enlarges the ring by about $1\%$. That looks small, but for comparison,
changing $w$ by $0.3$ changes $\Brat$ by only about $1\%$
(\S\ref{sec:cb_w}). \annot{Verified: checks B1--B3b, F4.}

\begin{annotation}[Why the shift is $\mBH/(\gp-1)$ and not $\mBH$]
The point mass adds the mass fraction $\mBH$ inside $\thetaE$, so the
mean convergence there exceeds one. The ring has to grow until the mean
convergence falls back to one. For a power law,
$\bar\kappa_{\rm PL}\propto\theta^{1-\gp}$: growing the ring by a
fraction $\delta\theta/\theta$ lowers $\bar\kappa_{\rm PL}$ by the
fraction $(\gp-1)\,\delta\theta/\theta$. That decrease has to cancel the
excess $\mBH$, so $\delta\theta/\theta = \mBH/(\gp-1)$. A shallow profile
($\gp\to1$) has a mean convergence that falls slowly with radius, so its
ring has to move further.
\end{annotation}

\section{The Central Mass Is a Different Perturbation on Each Plane}
\label{sec:cb_differential}

The black hole and the power law scale the same way between planes
(eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_alpha_scaling}), so one might expect the black hole to
drop out of the ratio. It does not, because the two \emph{Einstein
radii} scale with different powers of $\Brat$. From
eqs.~\eqref{eq:cb_alpha_scaling}--\eqref{eq:cb_B_eta},
\begin{equation}
  \mBH_2
  = \frac{\big(\thetaBH^{(1)}\big)^2/\Brat}{\big(\thetaE^{(1)}\big)^2\,\Brat^{-2/(\gp-1)}}
  = \mBH_1\,\Brat^{-1+2/(\gp-1)}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\;\mBH_2 = \mBH_1\,\Brat^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)} < \mBH_1\;}
  \label{eq:cb_m2_m1}
\end{equation}
The inequality holds for all $0<\Brat<1$ and $1<\gp<3$, where the
exponent is positive. The black hole is a \emph{smaller} fractional
perturbation on the far plane. For $\Brat=0.8$, $\mBH_2/\mBH_1 = 0.594$
at $\gp=1.6$ and $0.761$ at $\gp=1.9$. \annot{Verified: checks A3a--A3c.}

\begin{keybox}[The bias is differential]
By eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetatot_series}, the black hole moves each
total-mass Einstein radius outward by the fraction $\mBH_i/(\gp-1)$.
Since $\mBH_1>\mBH_2$, the near ring moves further than the far one, and
the ratio $\etamea$ changes. For the total-mass radii, equal fractional
perturbations ($\mBH_2=\mBH_1$) would leave $\etamea$ unchanged. A fit
does not return the total-mass radii, though. The two planes have
different image configurations (here only the far plane has a radial
image), so they respond differently even to the same fractional
perturbation. In the worked example of \S\ref{sec:cb_worked} at
$\mBH_1=10^{-4}$, forcing $\mBH_2=\mBH_1$ makes the fitted bias $1.87$
times \emph{larger}, not zero. \annot{Verified: check E13.}
\end{keybox}

There is a second reason the black hole matters more than its mass
fraction suggests. The radial critical curve of the pure power law lies
where the radial eigenvalue $\lambda_r = 1-\deriv{\alpha}{\theta}$
vanishes. (This is the eigenvalue $\lambda_+=1-\kappab+|\gammaL|$ of
eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalues}, written for an axisymmetric lens.) For the
power law $\lambda_r = 1-(2-\gp)(\thetaE/\theta)^{\gp-1}$, so
\begin{equation}
  \theta_r = \thetaE\,(2-\gp)^{1/(\gp-1)} .
  \label{eq:cb_theta_r}
\end{equation}
At $\gp=1.9$, $\theta_r = 0.077\,\thetaE$. A black hole with $\mBH=0.01$
has $\thetaBH=\sqrt{\mBH}\,\thetaE = 0.1\,\thetaE$, which is
\emph{larger} than $\theta_r$. A $1\%$ perturbation in mass is a
perturbation of order unity in deflection at the place where the radial
image forms. (For $\gp=1.6$, $\theta_r=0.217\,\thetaE$.)
\annot{Verified: checks A4a--A4d, A5b.}

\begin{annotation}[A two-line sanity check]
Take an isothermal deflector, $\gp=2$. Then $(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)=1$ and
$\mBH_2=\Brat\,\mBH_1$. Equivalently,
$\thetaE^{(2)}=\thetaE^{(1)}/\Brat$, while
$\thetaBH^{(2)}=\thetaBH^{(1)}/\sqrt{\Brat}$. The isothermal Einstein
radius grows in proportion to the distance factor $\Dds/\Ds$, but a
point mass's Einstein radius grows only as its square root.
\end{annotation}

\section{Two Channels for the Bias in \texorpdfstring{$\Brat$}{B}}
\label{sec:cb_channels}

A power-law-only fit returns some $\etamea_{\rm fit}$ and
$\gp_{\rm fit}$, from which the observer computes
$\Brat_{\rm fit}=\etamea_{\rm fit}^{\,\gp_{\rm fit}-1}$. Take the
logarithm of eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_B_eta} and form its total differential
with respect to the two fitted quantities:
\begin{equation}
  \ln\Brat = (\gp-1)\ln\etamea
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\;
  \frac{\delta\Brat}{\Brat}
  = \underbrace{(\gp-1)\,\frac{\delta\etamea}{\etamea}}_{\text{ratio channel}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{\ln\etamea\;\delta\gp}_{\text{slope-drag channel}}
  \;}
  \label{eq:cb_two_channels}
\end{equation}
\annot{Verified: checks C1--C2.} The two terms have different physical
origins.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Ratio channel.] $(\gp-1)\,\delta\etamea/\etamea$. The black hole
        moves the two Einstein radii by different fractions
        (\S\ref{sec:cb_differential}), so the ratio itself changes. The
        near ring moves out more, so this channel tends to increase
        $\etamea$ and therefore $\Brat$.
  \item[Slope-drag channel.] $\ln\etamea\,\delta\gp$. The fitted slope is
        set mainly by the radial image
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass}), which forms where the
        black hole's deflection is comparable to the power law's. With
        the black hole missing from the model, the fit accounts for the
        extra central deflection by changing $\gp$. The map
        $\etamea\mapsto\Brat$ depends on the slope, so that error passes
        straight into $\Brat$. Because $\ln\etamea<0$, a \emph{steeper}
        fitted slope ($\delta\gp>0$) \emph{lowers} $\Brat$.
\end{description}
The two channels can have opposite signs. Which one wins depends on the
configuration, in particular on how far $\etamea$ is from unity: the
slope channel carries the factor $\ln\etamea$, which vanishes when the
two planes coincide (Exercise~\ref{ex:cb_two_channels}).

\subsection{A fixed-slope, on-ring estimate}
\label{sec:cb_boe}

Suppose the slope is \emph{known}, for example from independent
information, so that only the ratio channel acts. Suppose also that the
images lie close enough to the rings for the fit to return the
total-mass radii. Applying eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetatot_series} on each
plane then gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta\etamea}{\etamea}\bigg|_{\gp\ \rm fixed}
  \simeq \frac{1+\mBH_1/(\gp-1)}{1+\mBH_2/(\gp-1)} - 1
  \simeq \frac{\mBH_1-\mBH_2}{\gp-1}
  = \frac{\mBH_1\big(1-\Brat^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}\big)}{\gp-1},
  \label{eq:cb_boe}
\end{equation}
and so
$\delta\Brat/\Brat|_{\gp\ \rm fixed}\simeq \mBH_1\big(1-\Brat^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}\big)$.
For the illustrative values $\gp=1.9$, $\mBH_1=0.01$, $\Brat=0.8$:
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\delta\etamea}{\etamea}\simeq 2.65\times10^{-3},
  \qquad
  \frac{\delta\Brat}{\Brat}\bigg|_{\gp\ \rm fixed}\simeq 2.39\times10^{-3}.
\end{equation*}
Solving eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetatot} exactly on both planes gives
$\delta\etamea/\etamea = 2.58\times10^{-3}$ for the total-mass radii, so
here the linearisation in $\mBH$ is accurate to $3\%$.
\annot{Verified: checks B4a--B4d.}

\begin{keybox}[Limits of the fixed-slope estimate]
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_boe} gives the ratio channel for images on the rings.
It does not predict the bias of a real fit, and need not even give its
order of magnitude, for three reasons.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item It contains only the ratio channel. When the slope is free and
        constrained by a radial image, the slope-drag channel can be
        larger and of opposite sign (\S\ref{sec:cb_linear}).
  \item It assumes the fit recovers the total-mass radii, which is
        exactly true only for images on the ring. For the lens of the
        worked example in \S\ref{sec:cb_worked}, a fixed-slope fit to the
        tangential image pairs agrees with eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_boe} to
        $0.3\%$ when each source is $0.02$ of its plane's $\thetaE$ off
        axis. The disagreement grows steadily with the offset and reaches
        a factor of $1.75$ at $0.3\,\thetaE$. When the two sources sit at
        \emph{different} fractional offsets, the per-plane shifts can
        nearly cancel: at the offsets of the worked example ($0.15$ and
        $0.3$), a fixed-slope fit returns only $0.084$ of
        eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_boe}.
  \item It is first order in $\mBH$. Near the annihilation boundary of
        \S\ref{sec:cb_annihilation} the response is strongly nonlinear.
\end{enumerate}
A systematic treatment of first-order estimators and their range of
validity is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:perturbative_estimators}.
\annot{Verified: checks E9a--E9b, E9d, E9e.}
\end{keybox}

\section{Linear Response: the Bias of a Fit That Cannot Represent the Truth}
\label{sec:cb_linear}

The general tool for working out what a misspecified model does is
linear response. Collect the observed image positions in a vector
$\mathbf{o}$, and \emph{all} the parameters of the power-law-only model,
including the unobserved source positions, in $\mathbf{p}$. For our
axisymmetric compound lens,
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\mathbf{o}=(\theta_{A1},\theta_{B1},\theta_{A2},\theta_{B2},\theta_{R2})$:
        the main image and counter-image on each plane, plus the radial
        image on the far plane;
  \item $\mathbf{p}=(\thetaE^{(1)},\thetaE^{(2)},\gp,\ysrc_1,\ysrc_2)$.
\end{itemize}
The central image produced by the black hole is assumed too faint to
detect (\S\ref{sec:cb_regimes}).

\begin{annotation}[Why the near-plane radial image is left out of $\mathbf{o}$]
For a power law with $\gp<2$, a source inside the radial caustic has a
main image, a counter-image and a radial image
(Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass}). The black hole changes this.
The radial image now exists only for sources in an \emph{annulus} of the
source plane, and it comes with a faint central image next to the black
hole. For a source inside the inner edge of the annulus, the radial and
central images have merged and vanished. The annulus narrows as $\mBH$
grows and closes completely at the annihilation boundary of
\S\ref{sec:cb_annihilation}. Since $\mBH_1>\mBH_2$, the near plane loses
its radial image first. For the near source in the worked example below,
the radial image is either very close to the centre and strongly
demagnified (smallest $\mBH_1$) or already gone (the larger $\mBH_1$),
so we use only the tangential pair on that plane.
\end{annotation}

Expand the true image positions to first order in the near-plane mass
fraction:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{o}(\mBH_1) = \mathbf{o}(0) + \mathbf{s}\,\mBH_1 + \order{\mBH_1^2},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{s} \equiv \pderiv{\mathbf{o}}{\mBH_1}\bigg|_{\mBH_1=0}.
\end{equation}
The vector $\mathbf{s}$ is the response of each image to the black hole
with all other parameters held fixed. The power-law-only model predicts
$\mathbf{o}_{\rm PL}(\mathbf{p})$, and the fit chooses
$\mathbf{p}_0+\delta\mathbf{p}$ so that
$\mathbf{o}_{\rm PL}(\mathbf{p}_0+\delta\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{o}(\mBH_1)$.
Since $\mathbf{o}_{\rm PL}(\mathbf{p}_0)=\mathbf{o}(0)$, linearising both
sides gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \delta\mathbf{p} = \mathsf{J}^{-1}\,\mathbf{s}\;\mBH_1 + \order{\mBH_1^2},
  \qquad
  \mathsf{J} \equiv \pderiv{\mathbf{o}_{\rm PL}}{\mathbf{p}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{p}_0}
  \;}
  \label{eq:cb_linear_response}
\end{equation}
In words: the parameter bias is the inverse of the model's Jacobian
applied to the part of the signal the model cannot produce. Putting the
$(\thetaE^{(1)},\thetaE^{(2)},\gp)$ components of $\delta\mathbf{p}$
into eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_two_channels} gives $\delta\Brat/\Brat$ to first
order.

\begin{derivation}[Computing $\mathbf{s}$ and $\mathsf{J}$ by implicit differentiation]
Each observed image $\theta_k$ on plane $i$ satisfies the lens equation
$\ysrc_i(\theta_k;\mathbf{p},\mBH_1) = \ysrc_i$, where the left side is
eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_lens_eq} evaluated at the image and the right side is
the source position. Differentiate with respect to any quantity $q$:
\begin{equation*}
  \ysrc_i'(\theta_k)\,\pderiv{\theta_k}{q} + \pderiv{\ysrc_i(\theta_k)}{q}
  = \pderiv{\ysrc_i}{q}\bigg|_{\rm source}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \pderiv{\theta_k}{q}
  = \frac{1}{\ysrc_i'(\theta_k)}
    \left(\pderiv{\ysrc_i}{q}\bigg|_{\rm source}-\pderiv{\ysrc_i(\theta_k)}{q}\right).
\end{equation*}
\emph{Black hole} ($q=\mBH_1$, source fixed). In units
$\thetaE^{(1)}=1$, eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_alpha_scaling} gives
$\partial\ysrc_i(\theta)/\partial\mBH_1 = -1/\theta$ on plane~1 and
$-1/(\Brat\theta)$ on plane~2.

\emph{Model parameters.} The source term is $1$ when $q$ is that plane's
source position and $0$ otherwise; the lens term is the derivative of
$\ysrc_{\rm PL}$ with respect to $\thetaE^{(i)}$ or $\gp$.

Every row is divided by $\ysrc_i'(\theta_k)$, which in axisymmetry is
exactly the radial eigenvalue $\lambda_r = 1-\deriv{\alpha}{\theta}$ at
the image.
\end{derivation}

\begin{bridge}[Back to Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}: why the radial image dominates]
The factor $1/\ysrc'(\theta_k)=1/\lambda_r$ in every row of $\mathbf{s}$
and $\mathsf{J}$ is the radial part of the magnification in
eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_eigenvalues}. A tangential image has $\lambda_r$ of
order unity, so the black hole moves it by roughly the extra deflection
$\thetaBH^2/\theta$. A radial image lies near the radial critical curve,
where $\lambda_r\to0$, so the same small change in deflection moves it
by much more. This is why the radial image both pins down the slope and
carries most of the bias.
\end{bridge}

\paragraph{Counting constraints.} The power-law-only model has five
parameters, and there are five observed positions. The fit is therefore
\emph{exactly determined}: whatever the true lens, a power law
reproduces the data perfectly, as long as a solution exists. The
residuals carry no information about the missing black hole. This is
the sense in which the bias is \emph{hidden}. More images, or extended
arcs, make the problem overdetermined. Whether the residuals then reveal
the missing mass depends on how well a change of slope can mimic the
extra $1/\theta$ deflection over the radial range the data cover.

\subsection{Worked example: exact fit versus linear response}
\label{sec:cb_worked}

Take $\gp=1.6$, $\Brat=0.8$, and units $\thetaE^{(1)}=1$. Put the
sources at $\ysrc_1=0.15\,\thetaE^{(1)}$ and $\ysrc_2=0.3\,\thetaE^{(2)}$.
Generate the five image positions with the black hole included. Then
find the power law that reproduces them by minimising the source-plane
mismatch
\[
  \chi^2(\thetaE^{(1)},\thetaE^{(2)},\gp)=
  \big[\ysrc_{\rm PL}(\theta_{A1})-\ysrc_{\rm PL}(\theta_{B1})\big]^2

  +\big[\ysrc_{\rm PL}(\theta_{A2})-\ysrc_{\rm PL}(\theta_{R2})\big]^2

\begin{annotation}[What the worked example does and does not show]
It shows that the mechanism works, that

the slope channel can dominate. It does \emph{not} give a typical bias
for real lenses. The axisymmetric, exactly determined fit is an extreme
case in which the radial image alone fixes $\gp$. Real fits include
ellipticity, shear and extended arcs, and the size of the slope response
(and in principle its sign) depends on all of them. The method carries
over unchanged; the coefficients do not.
\end{annotation}

\section{When the Central Mass Destroys the Radial Image}
\label{sec:cb_annihilation}

The radial image exists only while the far plane still has a radial
critical curve, that is, while $\lambda_r=\ysrc'(\theta)=0$ has a
positive root. Work in units of that plane's power-law Einstein radius
($\thetaE=1$) and write $u\equiv\mBH$ for its central mass fraction. For
$\theta>0$,
\begin{align}
  \ysrc(\theta)   &= \theta - \theta^{2-\gp} - \frac{u}{\theta}, \notag\\
  \ysrc'(\theta)  &= 1 - (2-\gp)\,\theta^{1-\gp} + \frac{u}{\theta^{2}},
  \label{eq:cb_yp}\\
  \ysrc''(\theta) &= (2-\gp)(\gp-1)\,\theta^{-\gp} - \frac{2u}{\theta^{3}}.
  \label{eq:cb_ypp}
\end{align}
Without the black hole ($u=0$), $\ysrc'\to-\infty$ as $\theta\to0$ and
$\ysrc'\to1$ at large $\theta$, so $\ysrc'$ vanishes at $\theta_r$
(eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_theta_r}). With $u>0$ the term $u/\theta^2$ dominates
at small $\theta$ and sends $\ysrc'\to+\infty$. Now $\ysrc'$ is positive
at both ends and dips in between. For small $u$ the dip goes below zero
and $\ysrc'$ has two zeros: the (shifted) radial critical curve, and an
inner radial critical curve. A point mass alone has none
($\lambda_r=1+u/\theta^2>0$); the black hole creates the inner curve by
cutting off the power law's $\lambda_r<0$ region from inside. \annot{Verified: check D10.}
As $u$ grows the dip
rises. At a critical value $u_{\rm crit}$ its minimum just touches zero;
the two critical curves merge and disappear. Beyond that, $\ysrc'>0$
everywhere, and there is no radial critical curve, no radial caustic and
no radial image.

The boundary is therefore a \emph{double root}, a point where both
$\lambda_r$ and its derivative vanish:
\begin{equation}
  \ysrc'(\theta_\star)=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \ysrc''(\theta_\star)=0 .
  \label{eq:cb_double_root}
\end{equation}
Multiplying eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_ypp} by $\theta^3$ shows that $\ysrc''=0$
is linear in $\theta^{3-\gp}$:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\star^{\,3-\gp} = \frac{2u}{(2-\gp)(\gp-1)} .
  \label{eq:cb_thetastar_u}
\end{equation}
Now substitute $u = \tfrac12(2-\gp)(\gp-1)\theta_\star^{3-\gp}$ into
$\ysrc'(\theta_\star)=0$. The term $u/\theta_\star^2$ becomes
$\tfrac12(2-\gp)(\gp-1)\theta_\star^{1-\gp}$, the same power of
$\theta_\star$ as the middle term, and the two combine:
\begin{equation}
  1 - (2-\gp)\Big[1-\frac{\gp-1}{2}\Big]\theta_\star^{1-\gp} = 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\;\theta_\star = \left[\frac{(2-\gp)(3-\gp)}{2}\right]^{1/(\gp-1)}\;}
  \label{eq:cb_thetastar}
\end{equation}
Then, from eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetastar_u},
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \mBH_{\rm crit}(\gp) = \frac{(2-\gp)(\gp-1)}{2}
  \left[\frac{(2-\gp)(3-\gp)}{2}\right]^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}
  \;}
  \label{eq:cb_mcrit}
\end{equation}
This is the mass fraction on the plane where the radial image forms,
measured against that plane's own $\thetaE$. Converting to the near-plane
fraction with eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_m2_m1},
\begin{equation}
  \mBH_{1,\rm crit}(\gp,\Brat) = \mBH_{\rm crit}(\gp)\;\Brat^{(\gp-3)/(\gp-1)} .
  \label{eq:cb_m1crit}
\end{equation}
\annot{Verified: checks D1--D3c. The closed form agrees with two independent numerical routes, a root-find of eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_yp} along the curve~\eqref{eq:cb_thetastar_u} and a bisection on $\min_\theta\lambda_r=0$, to better than $10^{-30}$ and $10^{-12}$ respectively \annot{checks D4, D5}.}

\begin{annotation}[Reading eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetastar}]
Since $(3-\gp)/2<1$, eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetastar} can be written as
$\theta_\star=\theta_r\,[(3-\gp)/2]^{1/(\gp-1)}<\theta_r$. When the
critical curves merge, they do so \emph{inside} the radial critical curve
of the pure power law. The black hole pushes the radial curve inward
until it meets the inner critical curve coming outward.
\end{annotation}

Table~\ref{tab:cb_mcrit} and Figure~\ref{fig:cb_annihilation} show the
consequences. (At $\gp=3/2$ everything is rational:
$\theta_\star=9/64$ and $\mBH_{\rm crit}=27/4096$.)
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\mBH_{\rm crit}\to0$ both as $\gp\to2$ (an isothermal profile has
        no radial critical curve to destroy) and as $\gp\to1$. In between
        it has a single maximum, $\mBH_{\rm crit}=0.0066$ at
        $\gp\approx1.48$. For an axisymmetric lens, no slope tolerates a
        central mass above $0.66\%$ of the power-law mass inside $\thetaE$
        on the radial-image plane.
        \annot{Verified: checks D7a, D7b.}
        External shear raises the boundary. Along the axis of a shear
        $\gamma_{\rm e}$ on the radial-image plane,
        $\lambda_r = 1-\gamma_{\rm e}-\deriv{\alpha}{\theta}$, and
        rescaling $\theta$ maps this onto the axisymmetric problem with
        $\mBH_{\rm crit}$ multiplied by
        $(1-\gamma_{\rm e})^{-(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}$, a factor $1.28$ for
        $\gamma_{\rm e}=0.1$ at $\gp=1.6$.
        \annot{Verified: checks D9a, D9b.}
  \item Near-isothermal deflectors are fragile. At $\gp=1.9$ and
        $\Brat=0.8$, a near-plane fraction of only $0.0017$ removes the
        far-plane radial image. The illustrative $\mBH_1=0.01$ of
        \S\ref{sec:cb_boe} is far above the boundary: that lens has no
        radial image, the slope-drag channel has no radial image to act
        through, and only the ratio channel remains.
  \item Below the boundary $\ysrc'$ has two zeros on $\theta>0$; above it,
        none. \annot{Verified: check D8 at $\gp=1.6$.}
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Two edges of the same effect]
The influence of central black holes on the central images of galaxy
lenses was studied by \citet{mao_witt_koopmans_2001}. For compound-lens
cosmography the effect cuts both ways. It \emph{limits} which systems
can show a radial image at all: at fixed near-plane fraction and
$\Brat$, the dashed curve of Figure~\ref{fig:cb_annihilation} favours
shallower inner slopes. And a system whose radial image \emph{is}
observed lies below the boundary, possibly close to it, where
Figure~\ref{fig:cb_bias_vs_m} shows the bias growing faster than
linearly.
\end{remark}

\section{Hidden and Detected Central Masses}
\label{sec:cb_regimes}

Besides moving the radial image, the point mass produces its own faint
image very close to the centre (Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images}). How bright
that image is decides which of two regimes the observer is in.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hidden regime.} The central image is below the detection
        limit. The observer sees the tangential images and the radial
        image, fits a power law, gets a perfect fit
        (\S\ref{sec:cb_linear}), and obtains a biased $\Brat$
        \emph{with no warning}. This is the regime that matters for
        cosmology.
  \item \textbf{Detected regime.} The black hole's own critical structure
        is large enough to produce detectable extra images. The observer
        is then \emph{forced} to add a central mass to the model, and if
        that model is correct the bias disappears. This is one way a
        central black hole can be detected through lensing. A black hole
        that measurably deforms an existing image can also force a
        central mass into the model without any extra image; the
        Abell~1201 analysis of \citet{nightingale_2023} is a case in
        point.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The point mass stands for any unmodelled central excess.}
Nothing in this chapter used the fact that the extra central mass is a
black hole, only that the model leaves it out. Seen from outside its
own radius, any axisymmetric central excess looks exactly like a point
mass. (An off-centre excess also adds higher multipoles: a unit mass
displaced by $0.1$ from the centre deflects a ray at $\theta=1$ by $11\%$
more than a central one.) \annot{Verified: check G3.}
Take a
deflector that follows the power law outside a radius $\theta_b$ and has
a steeper inner slope $\gamma_{\rm in}>\gp$ inside it, joined
continuously. For $\theta>\theta_b$ the deflection depends only on the
enclosed mass, so
\begin{equation}
  \alpha(\theta) = \alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta) + \frac{\mBH_{\rm eff}\,\thetaE^2}{\theta},
  \qquad
  \mBH_{\rm eff} = \frac{2}{\thetaE^2}\int_0^{\theta_b}
  \big[\kappab_{\rm in}(\theta')-\kappab_{\rm PL}(\theta')\big]\theta'\,\dd\theta' ,
  \label{eq:cb_meff}
\end{equation}
\emph{exactly}, and $\mBH_{\rm eff}>0$ for a steeper cusp
(Exercise~\ref{ex:cb_central_excess}). A nuclear star cluster, a
steepened stellar cusp and a black hole all bias a rigid power law in
the same way, provided the images lie outside the region containing the
excess. The converse is the practical lesson: a model with one extra
degree of freedom in the inner profile can absorb such an excess. The
bias derived in this chapter is the bias of a \emph{rigid} power law,
and its size measures how much that rigidity costs.
\annot{Verified: checks G1, G2.}

\begin{bridge}[Back to Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}: a point mass is not a mass sheet]
The mass-sheet transform of \S\ref{sec:msd} (eq.~\eqref{eq:mst_kappa})
adds a uniform sheet, whose deflection grows as $\theta$, and rescales
the source plane. Image positions are left \emph{exactly} unchanged, so
no fit to image positions can detect it. A central point mass adds a
deflection that falls as $1/\theta$. It \emph{does} move the images,
and no power law reproduces that change exactly; the fit can only
compromise, and the compromise is the bias. The two problems are
complementary. The mass sheet is invisible in the positions; the point
mass changes the positions, but in a way that a modest change of slope
largely absorbs. Independent information about the mass in the inner
region, such as the stellar kinematics discussed for the mass sheet in
Chapter~\ref{ch:msd_kinematics}, is the natural handle on both.
\end{bridge}

\section{From a Bias in \texorpdfstring{$\Brat$}{B} to a Bias in \texorpdfstring{$w$}{w}}
\label{sec:cb_w}

A fractional error in $\Brat$ turns into an error in any cosmological
parameter through the logarithmic sensitivity of $\Brat$ to that
parameter. For a constant equation of state $w$,
\begin{equation}
  \delta w = \frac{\delta\Brat/\Brat}{S_w},
  \qquad
  S_w \equiv \pderiv{\ln\Brat}{w} .
  \label{eq:cb_dw}
\end{equation}
As an illustration, take a flat universe with $\Omegam=0.3$, $w=-1$ and
redshifts $(z_{\rm d},z_{\rm s_1},z_{\rm s_2})=(0.5,1,2)$. The
flat-universe expression from the bridge in \S\ref{sec:cb_setup} gives
$\Brat=0.674$ and $S_w=-0.033$. $\Brat$ responds only weakly to $w$,
which is why sub-percent biases matter: a bias of
$\delta\Brat/\Brat=-0.5\%$ becomes $\delta w=+0.15$. In this
configuration $S_w<0$, so a $\Brat$ biased low pushes $w$ up (less
negative). The full sensitivity analysis, including how it depends on
the redshifts, is in Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_sensitivity}.
\annot{Verified: checks F1--F3.}

\begin{keybox}[A coherent systematic]
The bias is not random from lens to lens. For a population of similar
deflectors, each hosting a central black hole and observed in similar
configurations, it tends to have the same sign, because every black hole
adds positive central mass, although the size, and in some geometries
the sign, of the slope response varies (\S\ref{sec:cb_worked}).
Averaging over $N$ lenses reduces the statistical error as $N^{-1/2}$
but leaves the \emph{mean} bias unchanged,
so beyond a modest sample size the unmodelled central mass dominates the
error budget (Exercise~\ref{ex:cb_coherent}). It has to be modelled, or
bounded with independent information on the inner mass; it cannot be
averaged away.
\end{keybox}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:cb_summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A power law plus a central point mass deflects light on two
        source planes with the same distance factor, so
        $\alpha_2=\alpha_1/\Brat$,
        $\thetaE^{(2)}=\thetaE^{(1)}\Brat^{-1/(\gp-1)}$ and
        $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$.
  \item With a central mass present, the Einstein radius is defined from
        the \emph{total} enclosed mass, $\theta=\alpha_{\rm PL}+\alpha_\bullet$.
        At first order it exceeds the power-law normalisation by the
        fraction $\mBH/(\gp-1)$, where $\mBH=\thetaBH^2/\thetaE^2$.
  \item On the far plane the black hole is a smaller fractional
        perturbation: $\mBH_2=\mBH_1\Brat^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}$, which is
        less than $\mBH_1$. This asymmetry is one reason the bias does not
        cancel in the ratio; the other is that the two planes' images
        respond differently to the fit.
  \item The bias splits as
        $\delta\Brat/\Brat=(\gp-1)\delta\etamea/\etamea+\ln\etamea\,\delta\gp$.
        With the slope fixed and the images on the rings, the ratio
        channel gives $\mBH_1(1-\Brat^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)})$; away from the
        rings a fixed-slope fit can differ from this by a large factor.
        With a free slope constrained by a radial image, the slope-drag
        channel can be larger and of the opposite sign.
  \item The linear-response formula $\delta\mathbf{p}=\mathsf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\,\mBH_1$
        agrees with an exact power-law-only fit up to corrections of
        order $\mBH_1^2$. The fit residual is zero: the bias is hidden.
  \item The radial image disappears at the double root of $\lambda_r$,
        which has a closed form:
        $\theta_\star=[(2-\gp)(3-\gp)/2]^{1/(\gp-1)}$ and
        $\mBH_{\rm crit}=\tfrac12(2-\gp)(\gp-1)\theta_\star^{3-\gp}$, never
        more than $0.0066$.
  \item The bias propagates to $w$ through $\delta w=(\delta\Brat/\Brat)/S_w$.
        Its mean is coherent across a sample and does not average down.
\end{itemize}

  \item Starting from eqs.~\eqref{eq:cb_yp}--\eqref{eq:cb_ypp}, eliminate

        eqs.~\eqref{eq:cb_thetastar} and~\eqref{eq:cb_mcrit}.
  \item Evaluate $\theta_\star$ and $\mBH_{\rm crit}$ at $\gp=1.6$, and

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cb_central_excess}
\textbf{(A cusp is a point mass from outside.)}
A deflector follows the power law
$\kappab_{\rm PL}=\tfrac{3-\gp}{2}\theta^{1-\gp}$ (units $\thetaE=1$)

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Show that for $\theta>\theta_b$ the deflection is exactly
        eq.~\eqref{eq:cb_meff}, with
        $\mBH_{\rm eff}=(3-\gp)\,\theta_b^{3-\gp}\big[1/(3-\gamma_{\rm in})-1/(3-\gp)\big]$.

        units of that plane's $\thetaE$. Evaluate $\mBH_{\rm eff}$ for

        it with $\mBH_{\rm crit}(1.9)$ of Table~\ref{tab:cb_mcrit}. Why
        does the comparison require $\theta_b<\theta_\star$?
  \item What does this imply for the statement that a central black hole
        ``biases'' a compound lens?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
