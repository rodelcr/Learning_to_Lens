\chapter{The Multi-Plane Lens Equation and Double-Source-Plane Lenses}
\label{ch:multiplane_dspl}

\noindent
Everything so far has used a single deflecting plane: one thin mass
sheet between the observer and one source.  Real lines of sight are not
so tidy.  A quasar may be lensed by two galaxies at different redshifts;
a lens galaxy may sit in front of \emph{two} background sources at
different redshifts; and the nearer of those sources is itself a galaxy
with mass, so it lenses the farther one.  This chapter, the first of
Part~V, builds the machinery for all of these situations and then
specialises it to a configuration that is a cosmological tool in its
own right: the \textbf{double-source-plane lens} (DSPL), one deflector
seen against two sources.

The plan is:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item assemble the \textbf{recursive multi-plane lens equation} for
          $N$ deflecting planes from straight-line propagation between
          deflections, and identify the lens--lens coupling it contains;
    \item specialise to one deflector and two source planes, derive the
          \textbf{distance ratio} $\Brat$, show that it lies in $(0,1)$
          and that it does not depend on $\Hubble$;
    \item connect $\Brat$ to what is actually measured, the ratio of the
          two Einstein radii $\etamea$: $\Brat=\etamea$ for an isothermal
          (SIS or SIE) deflector, $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$ for a power law;
    \item compute $\Brat(z_l,z_{s1},z_{s2};\Omegam,w)$ and its
          sensitivity to $w$ and $\Omegam$;
    \item state precisely what a DSPL fit samples, where the inner
          density slope enters, and the one shortcut that would make the
          inference circular.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why a Double Source Plane Is a Cosmological Ruler}
\label{sec:mp_why}

A single strong lens says a great deal about the \emph{mass} of the
deflector but little that is clean about \emph{cosmology}.  The Einstein
radius of a singular isothermal sphere,
$\thetaE = 4\pi(\sigma_v/c)^2\,\Dds/\Ds$
(eq.~\eqref{eq:sis_einstein_radius}), multiplies a property of the galaxy
($\sigma_v$) by a ratio of distances, and the two cannot be separated
without an independent handle on the mass.

A DSPL uses the \emph{same deflector twice}.  Two sources at redshifts
$z_{s1}<z_{s2}$ lie behind one galaxy at $z_l$.  The galaxy's mass
distribution is identical for both; only the geometry differs.  The
ratio of the two Einstein radii therefore cancels the deflector mass and
leaves a pure ratio of distances --- a dimensionless number that depends
on the shape of the expansion history, $(\Omegam,w)$, but, as we will
show, not on the Hubble constant.  The use of DSPLs to constrain the
dark-energy equation of state was developed by \citet{collett_2012};
cosmological constraints from an observed galaxy-scale DSPL were derived
by \citet{collett_auger_2014}.

\begin{annotation}[What is new compared with Parts II and III]
Nothing in this chapter needs new physics.  Every ingredient is
already on the shelf: the thin-lens deflection
(Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation}), angular-diameter distances
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}), and the isothermal and
power-law profiles (Chapters~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models},
\ref{ch:elliptical_models} and~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}).  What is new is
\emph{bookkeeping}: which distance multiplies which deflection when
there is more than one plane.  The most common error in this subject is
a wrong distance in that bookkeeping, so the chapter is deliberately
pedantic about it.
\end{annotation}

\section{Recap: The Single-Plane Lens Equation and Its Distances}
\label{sec:mp_recap}

\begin{aside}[Why light bends twice as much]
A ray passing a point mass $M$ at impact parameter $b$ is deflected by
$\alphahat = 4GM/(c^2 b)$, twice the value a Newtonian argument gives,
because the photon responds equally to the temporal ($g_{tt}$) and
spatial ($g_{ij}$) parts of the weak-field metric
$\dd s^2 = -(1+2\Phi/c^2)c^2\dd t^2 + (1-2\Phi/c^2)\dd\ell^2$.  A slow
massive particle feels only $g_{tt}$ and is deflected half as much
(Chapters~\ref{ch:linearized_gravity} and~\ref{ch:light_deflection}).
\end{aside}

For a thin mass sheet with surface density $\Sigma$, the physical
deflection at lens-plane position $\vecxi$ is the superposition
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\vecalpha}(\vecxi) = \frac{4G}{c^2}\int \Sigma(\vecxi')\,
    \frac{\vecxi-\vecxi'}{|\vecxi-\vecxi'|^2}\,\dd^2\xi' ,
    \label{eq:mp_alphahat}
\end{equation}
and with $\vecxi=\Dd\vectheta$ the lens equation of
Chapter~\ref{ch:lens_equation} (eqs.~\eqref{eq:reduced_deflection}
and~\eqref{eq:lens_equation}) reads
\begin{equation}
    \vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta),\qquad
    \vecalpha(\vectheta) \equiv \frac{\Dds}{\Ds}\,\hat{\vecalpha}(\Dd\vectheta).
    \label{eq:mp_singleplane}
\end{equation}
The factor $\Dds/\Ds$ is the \textbf{lensing efficiency}: a given mass
produces a larger reduced deflection for a more distant source.

All distances are angular-diameter distances.  In a spatially flat
universe with comoving distance $\chi(z)$,
\begin{equation}
    D_A(z_1,z_2) = \frac{\chi(z_2)-\chi(z_1)}{1+z_2},\qquad
    \chi(z)=\frac{c}{\Hubble}\int_0^{z}\frac{\dd z'}{E(z')},\qquad
    E(z)=\sqrt{\Omegam(1+z)^3+(1-\Omegam)\,\zeta(z)},
    \label{eq:mp_DA}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta(z)=\exp\!\big[3\!\int_0^z \frac{1+w(z')}{1+z'}\,\dd z'\big]$
carries the dark-energy history.  For constant $w$,
$\zeta=(1+z)^{3(1+w)}$, and $\zeta\equiv1$ for a cosmological constant
($w=-1$).  The denominator $1+z_2$ belongs to the \emph{far} endpoint.
(\texttt{multiplane\_dspl.wl} check 5a.)

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}: why $\Dds\neq\Ds-\Dd$]
Equation~\eqref{eq:mp_DA} with $z_1=z_d$, $z_2=z_s$ is
eq.~\eqref{eq:Dds_flat} of \S\ref{sec:Dds}.  Comoving distances add along
a line of sight in a flat universe; angular-diameter distances do not,
because each is divided by $(1+z)$ at its own far endpoint:
\[
  (\Ds-\Dd)-\Dds = \chi_d\Big[\frac{1}{1+z_s}-\frac{1}{1+z_d}\Big]\neq 0 .
\]
At $(z_d,z_s)=(0.5,2)$ in flat $\Lambda$CDM with $\Omegam=0.3$ and
$\Hubble=70$\,km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$: $\Dds=1097.08$\,Mpc but
$\Ds-\Dd=467.54$\,Mpc.  In this chapter three or more redshifts are in
play, and \emph{every} plane-to-plane distance must be built from
comoving differences.  Exercise~\ref{ex:mp_naive_distance} shows what
the naive subtraction does to $\Brat$.
\end{bridge}

\section{The Multi-Plane Lens Equation}
\label{sec:mp_multiplane}

\subsection{Statement}

Order the deflecting planes by redshift, $z_1<z_2<\dots<z_N$, and put
the source at $z_s>z_N$.  Let $\vectheta_j$ be the angular position, as
seen by the observer, at which the ray crosses plane $j$; $\vectheta_1$
is the observed image position.  Write $D_j\equiv D_A(0,z_j)$ and
$D_{ij}\equiv D_A(z_i,z_j)$, and let $\hat{\vecalpha}_i$ be the physical
deflection produced by the mass in plane $i$, evaluated at the physical
impact position $\vecxi_i=D_i\vectheta_i$.  Then
\citep[\S9.1.1, eqs.~9.2--9.3]{schneider_ehlers_falco_1992}
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \vectheta_{j} = \vectheta_{1} - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1}
    \frac{D_{ij}}{D_{j}}\,\hat{\vecalpha}_i\big(D_i\vectheta_i\big),
    \qquad j=2,\dots,N+1,\;}
    \label{eq:mp_multiplane}
\end{equation}
with $\vectheta_{N+1}\equiv\vecbeta$ the source position.  The equation
is \emph{recursive}: $\vectheta_2$ needs only $\vectheta_1$;
$\vectheta_3$ needs $\vectheta_1$ and $\vectheta_2$; and so on.
\citet[\S9]{schneider_ehlers_falco_1992} attribute the multiple
lens-plane formalism in part to \citet{blandford_narayan_1986}.

\begin{keybox}[The multi-plane lens equation]
A ray observed at $\vectheta_1$ reaches plane $j$ at
$\vectheta_j=\vectheta_1-\sum_{i<j}(D_{ij}/D_j)\,\hat{\vecalpha}_i(D_i\vectheta_i)$.
Each earlier plane contributes its physical deflection, weighted by its
efficiency $D_{ij}/D_j$ towards plane $j$ and evaluated where the
\emph{already deflected} ray crosses it.  For $N=1$ this is
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_singleplane}.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Derivation from straight-line propagation}

In a spatially flat FRW universe the spatial metric in comoving
coordinates is Euclidean, so between deflections a light ray is a
straight line in comoving coordinates.  Let $\vec{x}(\chi)$ be the ray's
comoving transverse position at comoving distance $\chi$ from the
observer.  It leaves the observer in direction $\vectheta_1$; at plane
$i$ (comoving distance $\chi_i$) its direction changes by
$\hat{\vecalpha}_i$.  Because the FRW metric is conformally flat, angles
are the same in comoving and proper coordinates, so the local deflection
angle can be used directly.

\begin{derivation}[Comoving ray tracing $\Rightarrow$ eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane}]
Between the observer and plane 1 the ray is $\vec{x}=\chi\,\vectheta_1$,
so $\vec{x}_1=\chi_1\vectheta_1$.  After plane 1 its direction is
$\vectheta_1-\hat{\vecalpha}_1$, so at plane 2
\[
  \vec{x}_2=\chi_1\vectheta_1+(\vectheta_1-\hat{\vecalpha}_1)(\chi_2-\chi_1)
           =\chi_2\vectheta_1-(\chi_2-\chi_1)\,\hat{\vecalpha}_1 .
\]
Each further deflection adds one more term of the same form:
\[
  \vec{x}_j=\chi_j\vectheta_1-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\chi_j-\chi_i)\,\hat{\vecalpha}_i .
\]
The angular position seen by the observer is $\vectheta_j=\vec{x}_j/\chi_j$:
\[
  \vectheta_j=\vectheta_1-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{\chi_j-\chi_i}{\chi_j}\,\hat{\vecalpha}_i .
\]
Finally, by eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_DA},
$D_{ij}/D_j=[(\chi_j-\chi_i)/(1+z_j)]/[\chi_j/(1+z_j)]=(\chi_j-\chi_i)/\chi_j$:
the two factors of $(1+z_j)$ cancel.  The deflection at plane $i$
depends on the \emph{proper} impact parameter
$\vecxi_i=\vec{x}_i/(1+z_i)=D_i\vectheta_i$.  This is
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane}.  \texttt{multiplane\_dspl.wl} checks 1a--1b
verify the identity symbolically for three deflecting planes plus a
source, with arbitrary deflections.
\end{derivation}

The geometric derivation in SEF \S9.1.1 works directly with
angular-diameter distances and does not assume flatness, so
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane} also holds in curved FRW models, with
$D_{ij}$ computed from the curved-space formula
(eq.~\eqref{eq:Dds_general}).

\subsection{The Congdon--Keeton form and the factors $\beta_{ij}$}

It is often convenient to use deflections reduced to the final source,
$\vecalpha_i\equiv(D_{is}/D_s)\,\hat{\vecalpha}_i$.  Substituting into
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane} gives
\citep[\S4.8, eqs.~4.99a--c]{congdon_principles_2018}
\begin{equation}
    \vecbeta=\vectheta_1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vecalpha_i(\vectheta_i),\qquad
    \vectheta_j=\vectheta_1-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\beta_{ij}\,\vecalpha_i(\vectheta_i),\qquad
    \beta_{ij}\equiv\frac{D_{ij}\,D_s}{D_j\,D_{is}} .
    \label{eq:mp_CK}
\end{equation}
The factor $\beta_{ij}$ converts a deflection reduced to the source
plane into one reduced to plane $j$.  It reappears in
\S\ref{sec:mp_dspl} as the DSPL distance ratio.  \annot{check 1c}

\subsection{Lens--lens coupling}

Equation~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane} is nonlinear in a way the single-plane
equation is not: the deflection at plane $i$ is evaluated at
$\vectheta_i$, which already depends on all earlier deflections.  This
\textbf{lens--lens coupling} leaves a clean signature in the Jacobian.
For two planes, let $\Psi_i\equiv\partial\vecalpha_i/\partial\vectheta_i$;
each is symmetric because each $\vecalpha_i$ is the gradient of a
potential.  The chain rule applied to eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_CK} gives
\begin{equation}
    \Amat\equiv\pderiv{\vecbeta}{\vectheta_1}
    = I-\Psi_1-\Psi_2\big(I-\beta_{12}\Psi_1\big).
    \label{eq:mp_jacobian}
\end{equation}
Its antisymmetric part is
$\tfrac12\beta_{12}\big[\Psi_2\Psi_1-(\Psi_2\Psi_1)^{T}\big]$, which
vanishes only if the two matrices commute.  So $\Amat$ is in general
\emph{not} symmetric: the multi-plane mapping is not a gradient map, and
images can be rotated as well as sheared, as SEF point out at the start
of their Chapter~9.  Setting either mass to zero restores a symmetric
Jacobian.  \annot{checks 1g--1j: for two point masses the finite-difference
Jacobian matches eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_jacobian} and its off-diagonal
elements are clearly unequal}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}: the single-plane Jacobian]
In \S\ref{sec:jacobian} the Jacobian $\Amat=\partial\vecbeta/\partial\vectheta$
was symmetric because $\vecalpha=\nabla\psiL$, so
$\Amat_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\partial_i\partial_j\psiL$, and it could be
written in terms of $\kappab$ and $\gammaL$ alone.  The extra term
$\beta_{12}\Psi_2\Psi_1$ in eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_jacobian} is what breaks
that symmetry.  It is second order in the two lensing strengths, which
is why a weak second plane is often well described by an
\emph{effective} single-plane convergence and shear --- and why that
description fails when both planes are strong.
\end{bridge}

\begin{annotation}[Is the thin-lens approximation still safe?]
Equation~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane} assumes each deflection is complete
before the next begins: the planes must be far apart compared with the
extent of each mass distribution.  SEF (\S9 introduction) adopt this
assumption for all astrophysically relevant cases but add that ``one
should keep in mind that this hypothesis remains formally untested'',
citing in a footnote a numerical two-point-mass study in which the
approximation held very well for all rays that are not strongly
demagnified.  For galaxy-scale DSPLs the planes are separated by
Gpc while the galaxies are tens of kpc across.
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Two limits every implementation should pass}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Remove the first plane's mass.}  With
          $\hat{\vecalpha}_1=0$, eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane} for the
          source becomes
          $\vecbeta=\vectheta-(D_{2s}/D_s)\,\hat{\vecalpha}_2(D_2\vectheta)$,
          the single-plane equation~\eqref{eq:mp_singleplane} for a lens
          at $z_2$.  \annot{checks 1e--1f}
    \item \textbf{Merge the planes.}  As $z_1\to z_2$, $D_{12}\to0$, so
          $\beta_{12}\to0$ and $\vectheta_2=\vectheta_1$: by
          eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_CK} the two masses act as one thin lens with
          the summed deflection.  \citet[\S4.8]{congdon_principles_2018}
          make the same remark.
\end{enumerate}
\texttt{multiplane\_dspl.wl} \annot{check 1d} also traces rays through two
point masses at $z=0.3$ and $0.6$ (source at $z=1.5$) both ways ---
explicit sequential tracing in comoving coordinates, and the recursive
angular equation --- and finds agreement to better than
$10^{-12}$\,arcsec.

\section{One Deflector, Two Source Planes}
\label{sec:mp_dspl}

Now take a single deflector at $z_l$ with physical deflection
$\hat{\vecalpha}$, and two sources at $z_{s1}<z_{s2}$.  For the moment
treat source 1 as massless; its mass is restored in
\S\ref{sec:mp_coupling}.  There is one deflecting plane, so the sum in
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane} has one term and there is no coupling.  An
image at $\vectheta$ maps to each source plane as
\begin{align}
  \vecbeta_{1} &= \vectheta - \frac{D_{l,s1}}{D_{s1}}\,\hat{\vecalpha}(D_l\vectheta)
               \equiv \vectheta - \vecalpha_{1}(\vectheta), \label{eq:mp_plane1}\\
  \vecbeta_{2} &= \vectheta - \frac{D_{l,s2}}{D_{s2}}\,\hat{\vecalpha}(D_l\vectheta)
               \equiv \vectheta - \vecalpha_{2}(\vectheta). \label{eq:mp_plane2}
\end{align}
The \emph{same} physical deflection appears in both; the reduced
deflections differ only by their efficiencies.  Hence
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\vecalpha_{2}(\vectheta)}{\vecalpha_{1}(\vectheta)}
  = \frac{D_{l,s2}/D_{s2}}{D_{l,s1}/D_{s1}}
  = \frac{1}{\Brat},
  \qquad
  \Brat \equiv \frac{D_{l,s1}\,D_{s2}}{D_{s1}\,D_{l,s2}} ,
  \label{eq:mp_Bdef}
\end{equation}
so $\vecalpha_2(\vectheta)=\vecalpha_1(\vectheta)/\Brat$ \emph{at every
$\vectheta$, for any mass distribution in the lens plane}.  \annot{check 2a}
Comparing with eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_CK}, $\Brat$ is exactly the
Congdon--Keeton factor $\beta_{ij}$ with the lens as plane $i$, the near
source as plane $j$, and the far source as ``the'' source \annot{check 2a$'$}.

\begin{keybox}[The DSPL distance ratio]
$\displaystyle \Brat = \frac{D_{l,s1}\,D_{s2}}{D_{s1}\,D_{l,s2}}$
is dimensionless, lies strictly between 0 and 1, and
$\vecalpha_2 = \vecalpha_1/\Brat$ pointwise.  All the deflector physics
cancels in $\Brat$; only the geometry --- hence the cosmology ---
survives.
\end{keybox}

\paragraph{Why $0<\Brat<1$.}  Insert eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_DA}.  Every
distance carries a factor $c/\Hubble$ and a factor $1/(1+z)$ of its far
endpoint; all of them cancel (\S\ref{sec:mp_cosmo}), leaving
\begin{equation}
  \Brat = \frac{(\chi_{s1}-\chi_l)\,\chi_{s2}}{\chi_{s1}\,(\chi_{s2}-\chi_l)}
        = \frac{1-\chi_l/\chi_{s1}}{1-\chi_l/\chi_{s2}} ,
  \qquad \chi_x\equiv\chi(z_x).
  \label{eq:mp_Bchi}
\end{equation}
With $0<\chi_l<\chi_{s1}<\chi_{s2}$ the numerator is smaller than the
denominator, and both are positive.  The second form makes the physics
plain: in a flat universe the efficiency is
$D_{l,s}/D_s=1-\chi_l/\chi_s$, which grows with source distance, so the
far source is lensed more strongly and $\Brat<1$.  \annot{check 2d}

\begin{derivation}[Monotonicity and limits of $\Brat$]
Differentiating eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bchi},
\[
  \pderiv{\Brat}{\chi_{s2}} = -\frac{\Brat\,\chi_l}{\chi_{s2}(\chi_{s2}-\chi_l)}<0,
  \qquad
  \pderiv{\Brat}{\chi_{s1}} = +\frac{\Brat\,\chi_l}{\chi_{s1}(\chi_{s1}-\chi_l)}>0 .
\]
Moving the far source back lowers $\Brat$; moving the near source back
raises it.  The limits are $\Brat\to0$ as $z_{s1}\to z_l$ (source 1 sits
on the lens, efficiency zero) and $\Brat\to1$ as $z_{s2}\to z_{s1}$ (the
two sources coincide).  Because $\chi(z\to\infty)$ is finite
($3.305\,c/\Hubble$ for flat $\Lambda$CDM with $\Omegam=0.3$), $\Brat$
does not fall to zero as $z_{s2}\to\infty$ but to a finite floor
(Exercise~\ref{ex:mp_limits}).  \annot{checks 2e--2f; Solutions 5a--5d}
\end{derivation}

\paragraph{An illustrative geometry.}  Throughout this chapter we use
$(z_l,z_{s1},z_{s2})=(0.5,\,1.0,\,2.0)$ in flat $\Lambda$CDM with
$\Omegam=0.3$; the choice is generic, made only to make numbers
concrete.  Equation~\eqref{eq:mp_Bdef} gives
\begin{equation}
  \Brat(0.5,\,1.0,\,2.0) = 0.6742 .
  \label{eq:mp_Bnum}
\end{equation}
\annot{checks 2h--2i}  Had the lens-to-source distances been built by naive
subtraction, $D_{l,s}=D_s-D_l$, one would have found $0.878$ --- a
$30\%$ error from a single bookkeeping slip \annot{check 2l}.

\section{When the Near Source Has Mass: Coupling in a DSPL}
\label{sec:mp_coupling}

Equation~\eqref{eq:mp_plane2} assumed one deflector.  In a real DSPL the
near source is a galaxy, and its mass deflects the light of the far
source: a genuine second deflecting plane at $z_{s1}$.  Apply
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane} with planes $\{l,\,s1\}$ and the far source
as the final plane.  The ray crosses plane $s1$ at
$\vectheta_{s1}=\vecbeta_1$, so
\begin{equation}
  \vecbeta_2 = \vectheta - \frac{1}{\Brat}\,\vecalpha_1(\vectheta)
             - \frac{D_{s1,s2}}{D_{s2}}\,\hat{\vecalpha}_{s1}\big(D_{s1}\vecbeta_1\big),
  \qquad \vecbeta_1 = \vectheta - \vecalpha_1(\vectheta),
  \label{eq:mp_coupling}
\end{equation}
where $\vecalpha_1$ is the main lens's deflection reduced to plane
$s1$ and $\hat{\vecalpha}_{s1}$ is the physical deflection of the near
source's own mass.  The last term is the coupling; it is evaluated at
the point $\vecbeta_1$ where the \emph{lensed} ray crosses the
near-source plane.  Setting the near source's mass to zero recovers
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_plane2}.  \annot{checks 6a--6b}

\paragraph{How large is it?}  Model both galaxies as singular isothermal
spheres with velocity dispersions $\sigma_L$ (main lens) and $\sigma_1$
(near source).  Each produces a constant physical deflection
$4\pi\sigma^2/c^2$, so the ratio of the coupling deflection to the main
lens's deflection, both reduced to the far-source plane, is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\theta_{\mathrm{E}}(s1\to s2)}{\theta_{\mathrm{E}}(l\to s2)}
  = \Big(\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_L}\Big)^{2}\,\frac{D_{s1,s2}}{D_{l,s2}}
  = \Big(\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_L}\Big)^{2}\,
    \frac{\chi_{s2}-\chi_{s1}}{\chi_{s2}-\chi_l} .
  \label{eq:mp_couplingsize}
\end{equation}
At the illustrative geometry $D_{s1,s2}/D_{s2}=0.3622$ and
$D_{l,s2}/D_{s2}=0.6354$, so the geometric factor is $0.570$.  A near
source with $\sigma_1/\sigma_L=0.4$ deflects the far source's light by
$9.1\%$ of the main lens's deflection; a $\sigma_1=100$\,km\,s$^{-1}$
galaxy at $z_{s1}=1$ has an Einstein radius of $0.10''$ for a source at
$z_{s2}=2$.  \annot{checks 6c--6f}  For galaxy-scale sources this is a
first-order term, not a small perturbation.

\begin{annotation}[Coupling breaks the clean ratio --- so it must be modelled]
With the coupling present, $\vecbeta_2$ is no longer
$\vectheta-\vecalpha_1/\Brat$: the far-source images are shaped by two
deflectors at two redshifts.  The near source's light is observed (it
forms its own arcs), so its centroid and shape are well constrained, and
its mass can be included as a second deflector.  The parameter vector of
\S\ref{sec:mpi_sampling} then gains
$\{\theta_{\mathrm{E},s1},\,q_{s1},\,\varphi_{s1},\,\vectheta_{c,s1}\}$.
The distance ratio $\Brat$ enters exactly as before, through the main
lens's term; the coupling adds new unknowns and one further ratio of
distances, $D_{s1,s2}/D_{s2}$, which is also Hubble-free.
\end{annotation}

\section{From Deflections to Einstein Radii: $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$}
\label{sec:mp_eta}

We do not measure $\hat{\vecalpha}$; we measure image positions, which
for a nearly round deflector are governed by the Einstein radius on each
source plane.  Define the measured ratio
\begin{equation}
    \etamea\equiv\frac{\thetaE^{(1)}}{\thetaE^{(2)}} .
    \label{eq:mp_eta}
\end{equation}
Because the far source is lensed more strongly,
$\thetaE^{(2)}>\thetaE^{(1)}$ and $\etamea<1$.

Take a circular power-law deflector with three-dimensional density
$\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$.  Its convergence on source plane $i$ is
$\kappab_i(\theta)=\frac{3-\gp}{2}\big(\thetaE^{(i)}/\theta\big)^{\gp-1}$
(eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_power_law}), and the circular-lens relation
$\alpha=\theta\,\bar\kappab(<\theta)$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_i(\theta)=\big(\thetaE^{(i)}\big)^{\gp-1}\,\theta^{\,2-\gp}.
  \label{eq:mp_plaw}
\end{equation}
\annot{checks 3a--3b}  Imposing $\alpha_2=\alpha_1/\Brat$ at all $\theta$
forces $\big(\thetaE^{(2)}\big)^{\gp-1}=\big(\thetaE^{(1)}\big)^{\gp-1}/\Brat$:
\begin{equation}
  \thetaE^{(2)} = \thetaE^{(1)}\,\Brat^{-1/(\gp-1)}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \boxed{\;\Brat = \etamea^{\,\gp-1}\;}
  \label{eq:mp_etaB}
\end{equation}
\annot{checks 3c--3f}

\begin{bridge}[Chapters~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models} and~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}: where eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_plaw} comes from]
For any circular lens $\alpha(\theta)=\theta\,\bar\kappab(<\theta)$
(eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_mean_kappa}).  With the power-law convergence of
\S\ref{sec:power_law_profile},
\[
  \bar\kappab(<\theta)=\frac{2}{\theta^2}\int_0^\theta
  \frac{3-\gp}{2}\Big(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta'}\Big)^{\gp-1}\theta'\,\dd\theta'
  =\Big(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\Big)^{\gp-1},
\]
so $\alpha=\thetaE^{\gp-1}\theta^{2-\gp}$ and $\alpha(\thetaE)=\thetaE$.
The isothermal case $\gp=2$ gives the constant SIS deflection
$\alpha=\thetaE$ of eq.~\eqref{eq:sis_alpha_reduced}.  The same
physical surface density on the two source planes differs only through
$\Sigmacr\propto D_s/(D_l D_{l,s})$ (eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma_cr}), which is
how the single factor $1/\Brat$ arises: $\kappab_2=\kappab_1/\Brat$.
\end{bridge}

Two consequences organise the rest of the chapter:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item For an isothermal deflector ($\gp=2$) the exponent is one and
        $\Brat=\etamea$ exactly: the ratio of Einstein radii \emph{is}
        the distance ratio \annot{check 3e}.
  \item For any other slope, recovering $\Brat$ from the measured
        $\etamea$ requires $\gp$.  At fixed $\etamea$,
        $\partial\ln\Brat/\partial\gp=\ln\etamea$, which is negative and
        independent of $\gp$ \annot{check 3g}.  An error in the inferred slope
        enters $\Brat$ through the exponent even if $\etamea$ is perfect.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{annotation}[A worked number]
At the illustrative geometry $\Brat=0.6742$.  A deflector with
$\gp=1.9$ would show $\etamea=\Brat^{1/0.9}=0.6453$, so
$\ln\etamea=-0.438$.  A slope error $\delta\gp=0.023$ alone then
produces a $1\%$ error in $\Brat$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:mp_slope_lever}).
The smaller $\etamea$ is --- the more widely separated the two source
planes --- the larger the lever arm $|\ln\etamea|$.
\end{annotation}

\section{The Cosmological Content of $\Brat$}
\label{sec:mp_cosmo}

\subsection{$\Brat$ is independent of $\Hubble$}

Insert eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_DA} into eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bdef}, writing
$\chi=(c/\Hubble)\,\tilde\chi$ with $\tilde\chi(z)\equiv\int_0^z\dd z'/E$:
\begin{equation}
  \Brat
  = \frac{\dfrac{c}{\Hubble}\dfrac{\tilde\chi_{s1}-\tilde\chi_l}{1+z_{s1}}\cdot
          \dfrac{c}{\Hubble}\dfrac{\tilde\chi_{s2}}{1+z_{s2}}}
         {\dfrac{c}{\Hubble}\dfrac{\tilde\chi_{s1}}{1+z_{s1}}\cdot
          \dfrac{c}{\Hubble}\dfrac{\tilde\chi_{s2}-\tilde\chi_l}{1+z_{s2}}}
  = \frac{(\tilde\chi_{s1}-\tilde\chi_l)\,\tilde\chi_{s2}}
         {\tilde\chi_{s1}\,(\tilde\chi_{s2}-\tilde\chi_l)} .
  \label{eq:mp_Bcancel}
\end{equation}
Numerator and denominator each contain two factors of $c/\Hubble$, one
of $(1+z_{s1})^{-1}$ and one of $(1+z_{s2})^{-1}$; all cancel.  The lens
redshift never appears in a $(1+z)$ prefactor, because no distance in
$\Brat$ ends at the lens.  \annot{checks 2b--2c.  Numerically, $\Brat$ at the
illustrative geometry is identical for $\Hubble=50$, 70 and
100\,km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$ to $10^{-12}$, check 2g}

\begin{keybox}[$\Brat$ is Hubble-free]
$\Brat$ depends on cosmology only through the \emph{shape} of the
comoving-distance function, i.e.\ through
$E(z)=\sqrt{\Omegam(1+z)^3+(1-\Omegam)\,\zeta(z)}$.  It is independent
of $\Hubble$.  DSPLs therefore probe $\Omegam$ and $w$ and say nothing
about $\Hubble$ --- the complement of time-delay cosmography, which
measures an absolute distance scale.
\end{keybox}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}: the other distance combination]
The time-delay distance of \S\ref{sec:time_delay_cosmography},
$D_{\Delta t}=(1+z_d)\Dd\Ds/\Dds$, is a product of distances with
dimensions of length; it scales as $c/\Hubble$ \annot{check 2a$''$}, which is
why time delays measure $\Hubble$.  The DSPL ratio $\Brat$ is a ratio of
two \emph{efficiencies} and is dimensionless.  One observable fixes the
absolute scale, the other the shape of the expansion history.  How the
mass-sheet degeneracy of \S\ref{sec:msd} plays out with two source planes
is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:msd_kinematics}; the question of
whether cosmological parameters can be recovered from multi-plane lens
systems at all, given such degeneracies, is posed in the title of
\citet{schneider_2014_multiplane}.
\end{bridge}

\subsection{Sensitivity to $w$ and $\Omegam$}

Define the logarithmic responses
\begin{equation}
  S_w \equiv \pderiv{\ln\Brat}{w},\qquad
  S_{\Omegam}\equiv\pderiv{\ln\Brat}{\Omegam},
  \label{eq:mp_S}
\end{equation}
evaluated at a reference cosmology.  A fractional error in the recovered
distance ratio maps, to first order, to
\begin{equation}
  \delta w = \frac{\delta\Brat/\Brat}{S_w},\qquad
  \delta\Omegam = \frac{\delta\Brat/\Brat}{S_{\Omegam}} .
  \label{eq:mp_propagate}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[Computing $S_w$ without finite differences]
From eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bcancel},
$\ln\Brat=\ln(\tilde\chi_{s1}-\tilde\chi_l)+\ln\tilde\chi_{s2}
-\ln\tilde\chi_{s1}-\ln(\tilde\chi_{s2}-\tilde\chi_l)$,
so for any cosmological parameter $p$
\[
  \pderiv{\ln\Brat}{p}=\frac{\tilde\chi'_{s1}-\tilde\chi'_l}{\tilde\chi_{s1}-\tilde\chi_l}
   +\frac{\tilde\chi'_{s2}}{\tilde\chi_{s2}}-\frac{\tilde\chi'_{s1}}{\tilde\chi_{s1}}
   -\frac{\tilde\chi'_{s2}-\tilde\chi'_l}{\tilde\chi_{s2}-\tilde\chi_l},
  \qquad
  \tilde\chi'_x\equiv\pderiv{\tilde\chi(z_x)}{p}
   =-\frac12\int_0^{z_x}\frac{\partial E^2/\partial p}{E^3}\,\dd z' .
\]
For constant $w$,
$\partial E^2/\partial w=3(1-\Omegam)(1+z)^{3(1+w)}\ln(1+z)>0$: raising
$w$ makes dark energy denser in the past, raises $E$, and shortens every
$\tilde\chi$.  Similarly
$\partial E^2/\partial\Omegam=(1+z)^3-(1+z)^{3(1+w)}>0$ for $z>0$ and
$w<0$ \annot{check 5j}.  \texttt{multiplane\_dspl.wl} evaluates these integrals
and agrees with a central finite difference of $\ln\Brat$ to better than
$10^{-7}$ \annot{checks 5b--5c}.
\end{derivation}

At the illustrative geometry in flat $\Lambda$CDM ($\Omegam=0.3$, $w=-1$):
\begin{equation}
  S_w = -0.0330,\qquad S_{\Omegam} = -0.0357 .
  \label{eq:mp_Snum}
\end{equation}
\annot{checks 5d--5e}  Both are negative: raising $w$ or $\Omegam$ shortens
the comoving distances unevenly, in a way that lowers $\Brat$.  Both are
\emph{small}.  A $1\%$ error in $\Brat$ masquerades as $|\delta w|=0.30$
at this geometry \annot{check 5g}.  That combination --- a small cosmological
signal and a large parameter response --- is why a single DSPL gives a
weak $w$ constraint, why populations of DSPLs are needed
\citep{collett_2012}, and why percent-level systematics in $\Brat$ matter
(Chapters~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias} and~\ref{ch:cosmological_sensitivity}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../Figures/14_Multiplane_DSPL/B_vs_zs2.pdf}
    \caption{The DSPL distance ratio $\Brat$ as a function of the far
    source redshift $z_{s2}$ for $z_l=0.5$, $z_{s1}=1.0$, in a flat
    cosmology with $\Omegam=0.3$, for $w=-1$ (solid) and $w=-0.7$
    (dashed).  Lower panel: the fractional difference.  $\Brat$ falls

    \label{fig:mp_B_vs_zs2}
\end{figure}

\begin{annotation}[Reading Figure~\ref{fig:mp_B_vs_zs2}]
Three lessons.  (i) As $z_{s2}\to z_{s1}$ the two curves meet at
$\Brat=1$ whatever the cosmology: two coincident sources carry no
information.  (ii) Even for a widely separated source pair the $w$
signal is under $2\%$.  (iii) The response does not change sign anywhere
on this range of $z_{s2}$, and its magnitude grows with the separation
of the two source planes.
\end{annotation}

\section[The Isothermal Ellipsoid]{The Isothermal Ellipsoid: Ratio of Einstein Radii = Distance Ratio}
\label{sec:mpi_sie}

Galaxy lenses are elliptical, not round, so it is worth seeing that the
statement $\Brat=\etamea$ survives ellipticity exactly for an isothermal
profile.

\paragraph{SIS baseline.}  For a singular isothermal sphere the physical
deflection is the constant $\alphahat=4\pi\sigma_v^2/c^2$, so on source
plane $i$
\begin{equation}
  \thetaE^{(i)} = 4\pi\,\frac{\sigma_v^2}{c^2}\,\frac{D_{l,si}}{D_{si}} .
  \label{eq:mpi_sisthetaE}
\end{equation}
The Einstein radius is \emph{linear} in the efficiency --- the special
property of the isothermal slope.

\paragraph{Adding ellipticity.}  In the normalisation of
Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models} (eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_kappa}, following
\citet{kormann_isothermal_1994}), the SIE convergence on source plane
$i$ is
\begin{equation}
  \kappab_i(\vectheta)=\frac{\thetaE^{(i)}\sqrt{q}}{2\sqrt{q^2\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2}},
  \qquad
  \thetaE^{(i)}=4\pi\frac{\sigma_v^2}{c^2}\frac{D_{l,si}}{D_{si}},
  \label{eq:mpi_siekappa}
\end{equation}
and its deflection (eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_alpha}) is $\thetaE^{(i)}$ times a
function of $(\theta_1,\theta_2,q)$ that is homogeneous of degree zero
in $\vectheta$.  Two facts follow.  First, the plane-2 deflection field is
the plane-1 field divided by $\Brat$, as it must be \annot{check 4b}.  Second,
every length scale of the lens --- the parameter $\thetaE$, the radius of
the tangential critical curve along any direction, the radius of the
circle with the same area as that curve --- is $\thetaE$ times a
coefficient that depends only on $q$ and the direction.

\paragraph{The cancellation.}  Put the same physical SIE behind two
source planes.  $\sigma_v$, $q$ and the position angle are properties of
the galaxy and identical for both, so
\begin{equation}
  \etamea=\frac{\thetaE^{(1)}}{\thetaE^{(2)}}
  =\frac{\big[4\pi\sigma_v^2/c^2\big]\,f(q)\,(D_{l,s1}/D_{s1})}
        {\big[4\pi\sigma_v^2/c^2\big]\,f(q)\,(D_{l,s2}/D_{s2})}
  =\Brat ,
  \label{eq:mpi_sieratio}
\end{equation}
where $f(q)$ is whatever $q$-dependent factor a particular Einstein-radius
convention introduces ($f=1$ for the parameter of
eq.~\eqref{eq:mpi_siekappa}).  \annot{checks 4a--4f: the numerically located
tangential critical curves on the two planes have radius ratio $\Brat$ to
$10^{-10}$ along every direction tested, for $q=0.4$, $0.7$ and $0.95$,
and so do their area-equivalent radii}

\begin{keybox}[The ratio of Einstein radii]
For an SIS or SIE the velocity dispersion, axis ratio and position angle
all cancel in $\etamea=\thetaE^{(1)}/\thetaE^{(2)}$, leaving
$\etamea=\Brat$ exactly, in any Einstein-radius convention.  For a power
law $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$, and the slope must be known.
\end{keybox}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}: the SIE in this book]
Equation~\eqref{eq:mpi_siekappa} is eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_kappa} of
\S\ref{sec:sie}, with the SIS-equivalent Einstein radius
$\thetaE=4\pi\sigma_v^2\Dds/(c^2\Ds)$.  The tangential critical curve of
\S\ref{sec:critical_curves} crosses the axes at $\thetaE/\sqrt{q}$ and
$\thetaE\sqrt{q}$; both scale with $\thetaE$ and hence, from one source
plane to the other, by the same factor $1/\Brat$.  The DSPL statement
reduces to ``the SIE has one length scale, and the efficiency multiplies
it''.
\end{bridge}

\begin{annotation}[Conventions cancel; single Einstein radii do not]
Codes define the SIE ``Einstein radius'' with the $\sqrt{q}$ in
different places (intermediate axis, major axis, area-equivalent).  Each
convention rescales $\thetaE^{(1)}$ and $\thetaE^{(2)}$ by the same
function of $q$, which cancels in $\etamea$.  A single Einstein radius is
convention-dependent; the ratio is not.  The practical rule: fit both
planes with one code and one convention, and read off the ratio.
\end{annotation}

\section{What a DSPL Fit Samples}
\label{sec:mpi_sampling}

A DSPL cosmography fit is a Bayesian fit of one lens model to both image
systems at once.  The deflector-plus-sources parameter vector is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_{\rm mod} = \big\{\,\underbrace{\thetaE^{(1)},\,\gp,\,q,\,\varphi,\,
  \gamma_{\rm ext},\,\phi_{\rm ext},\,\vectheta_c}_{\text{deflector}},\;
  \underbrace{\vecbeta_{1}}_{\text{source 1}},\;
  \underbrace{\vecbeta_{2}}_{\text{source 2}},\;
  \dots\,\big\},
  \label{eq:mpi_pmod}
\end{equation}
with $\vectheta_c$ the lens centre, $(\gamma_{\rm ext},\phi_{\rm ext})$
an external shear, and the dots standing for source-light parameters in
extended-image fits (and, when the near source is massive, its own mass
parameters, \S\ref{sec:mp_coupling}).  The two source planes are tied
together by one extra number through the plane-2 lens equation
\begin{equation}
  \vecbeta_2 = \vectheta - \frac{1}{\Brat}\,\vecalpha_1(\vectheta;\mathbf{p}_{\rm mod}),
  \label{eq:mpi_plane2fit}
\end{equation}
the same deflection field that lenses source 1, divided by $\Brat$.
That single rescaling is the only place cosmology enters a position or
pixel fit of a one-deflector DSPL.

\paragraph{Two equivalent parametrisations.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item[(a)] \textbf{Free $\Brat$.}  Append $\Brat$ to
        $\mathbf{p}_{\rm mod}$ and sample both.  The plane-2 data pull on
        $\Brat$ through eq.~\eqref{eq:mpi_plane2fit}.  Convert the $\Brat$
        posterior to $(\Omegam,w)$ afterwards with
        eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bdef}.  Cosmology never enters the likelihood.
  \item[(b)] \textbf{Free cosmology.}  Sample
        $(\mathbf{p}_{\rm mod},\Omegam,w)$ and compute
        $\Brat(z_l,z_{s1},z_{s2};\Omegam,w)$ at every step.  The
        likelihood is the same function; only the coordinates on
        parameter space, and hence the meaning of a ``flat'' prior,
        differ.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The likelihood.}  With Gaussian astrometric errors,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\text{data}\mid\mathbf{p}_{\rm mod},\Brat)\propto
  \exp\!\Big[-\tfrac12\,\chi^2\Big],\qquad
  \chi^2 = \sum_{\text{planes }i}\;\sum_{\text{images }k}
  \frac{\big|\vectheta^{\rm obs}_{ik}-\vectheta^{\rm mod}_{ik}(\mathbf{p}_{\rm mod},\Brat)\big|^2}{\sigma^2_{ik}},
  \label{eq:mpi_like}
\end{equation}
summed over both image systems.  In position-only fits the source
positions $\vecbeta_i$ are nuisance parameters, often optimised or
marginalised per plane; in extended-image fits the sum runs over pixels
and the source light is reconstructed.  Either way $\Brat$ --- or the
cosmology behind it --- is a sampled dimension of the posterior,
constrained by requiring that one deflector map both image systems back
to consistent sources.

\paragraph{Why one lens gives a curve, not a point.}  In
parametrisation (b), $\chi^2$ depends on $(\Omegam,w)$ only through the
composite $\Brat(\Omegam,w)$.  Two cosmologies with the same $\Brat$ give
identical model images and identical likelihood.  A single DSPL
therefore constrains a level curve of $\Brat$ in the $(\Omegam,w)$
plane, with local slope
\begin{equation}
  \deriv{w}{\Omegam}\bigg|_{\Brat}=-\frac{S_{\Omegam}}{S_w},
  \label{eq:mpi_degslope}
\end{equation}
which is $-1.08$ at the illustrative geometry \annot{check 5f; Solutions
9a--9d}.  Breaking the degeneracy needs several lenses with different
redshift triplets, each contributing a differently oriented band
\citep{collett_2012}.

\begin{annotation}[Shear in a two-plane fit]
An external shear generated by mass at $z_l$ is a deflection field at
the lens plane like any other, so it too scales by $1/\Brat$ between the
planes.  If the model writes the shear as a reduced quantity per plane,
the plane-2 shear is $\gamma_{\rm ext}^{(2)}=\gamma_{\rm ext}^{(1)}/\Brat$
(Exercise~\ref{ex:mpi_shape_shear}).  A shear produced by structure at
\emph{another} redshift --- in front of the lens, or between the two
source planes --- does not share that scaling and is a genuine
plane-to-plane systematic.
\end{annotation}

\section[The $\Brat$--$\gp$ Degeneracy]{The $\Brat$--$\gp$ Degeneracy: Why the Inner Slope Is Load-Bearing}
\label{sec:mpi_degeneracy}

The data constrain the image configuration, hence essentially $\etamea$
and the slope $\gp$ jointly; the cosmological number is the derived
combination $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$.  Its logarithmic differential is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta\Brat}{\Brat}
  = (\gp-1)\,\frac{\delta\etamea}{\etamea}
  \;+\; \ln\etamea\;\delta\gp .
  \label{eq:mpi_twochannel}
\end{equation}
(Solutions 8a--8d.)  Two consequences:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The posterior in $(\Brat,\gp)$ is correlated.}  If the
        images fix $\etamea$ well but $\gp$ poorly, the marginal $\Brat$
        posterior is broad.  The width of the cosmology constraint is set
        by how well the slope is pinned.
        Exercise~\ref{ex:mpi_error_budget} gives a worked budget in which
        the slope channel dominates.
  \item \textbf{The two channels have opposite signs.}  Since
        $\etamea<1$, $\ln\etamea<0$, while $\gp-1>0$.  An upward error in
        $\etamea$ raises $\Brat$; an upward error in $\gp$ lowers it.
        Correlated errors can therefore partly cancel, and an error in the
        slope can masquerade as a change in cosmology even when $\etamea$
        is measured well.
\end{enumerate}
The parts of the data most sensitive to the inner slope are those that
probe the mass profile at radii different from the tangential arcs ---
most directly a radial or central image, when one is detected
(Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images}).  The same central region is where an
unmodelled compact mass would act, which is why
Chapters~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass} and~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}
study power-law lenses with a central point mass.

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}: slopes and degeneracies again]
In \S\ref{sec:power_law_profile} the slope $\gp$ was the quantity that
lensing alone constrains poorly and that stellar kinematics helps to
pin down, and in \S\ref{sec:time_delay_cosmography} the inferred
$\Hubble$ depended on the assumed profile.  The DSPL case is the same
story with $\Brat$ in place of $\Hubble$: the distance-type observable is
clean only once the radial structure of the deflector is fixed.
\end{bridge}

\section{The Circularity Trap}
\label{sec:mpi_circular}

A tempting shortcut is fatal.  One might take a reference cosmology,
compute $\Brat_{\rm ref}$, and \emph{impose}
$\thetaE^{(2)}=\thetaE^{(1)}\,\Brat_{\rm ref}^{-1/(\gp-1)}$ (equivalently
$\etamea=\Brat_{\rm ref}^{1/(\gp-1)}$) as a constraint in the fit.  That
builds the answer into the model: the posterior would merely echo
$\Brat_{\rm ref}$.

\begin{keybox}[Never tie the two planes with a cosmological $\Brat$]
The correct inference leaves the two planes' normalisations free:
either $\thetaE^{(1)}$ and $\thetaE^{(2)}$ are both sampled, or
$\thetaE^{(1)}$ and $\Brat$ are.  $\Brat$ is then whatever value lets one
deflector fit both image systems, and only then is it a
\emph{measurement} that can agree or disagree with a cosmology.  The
relation $\etamea=\Brat^{1/(\gp-1)}$ is for \emph{interpreting} a fitted
$(\etamea,\gp)$, never for constraining the fit.
\end{keybox}

\begin{annotation}[How the trap sneaks in]
The trap is rarely set deliberately.  It appears when a simulation
pipeline that \emph{generated} mock data with
$\thetaE^{(2)}=\thetaE^{(1)}\Brat_{\rm true}^{-1/(\gp-1)}$ is reused,
unchanged, as the fitting model; or when a multi-plane ray tracer is
handed a fixed cosmology and the fit never varies it.  In
parametrisation (b) the cosmology must be a \emph{sampled} parameter; in
(a) $\Brat$ must be.  A quick test: refit mock data generated with a
different $\Brat$ and check that the posterior moves with it.
\end{annotation}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:mp_summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item With $N$ deflecting planes, a ray observed at $\vectheta_1$
        reaches plane $j$ at
        $\vectheta_j=\vectheta_1-\sum_{i<j}(D_{ij}/D_j)\hat{\vecalpha}_i(D_i\vectheta_i)$
        (eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_multiplane}), which follows from straight
        comoving rays between deflections.  Each deflection is evaluated
        at the already deflected ray (lens--lens coupling), and the
        Jacobian is no longer symmetric.
  \item For one deflector and two source planes, $\vecalpha_2=\vecalpha_1/\Brat$
        with $\Brat=D_{l,s1}D_{s2}/(D_{s1}D_{l,s2})\in(0,1)$.  In a flat
        universe
        $\Brat=(\chi_{s1}-\chi_l)\chi_{s2}/[\chi_{s1}(\chi_{s2}-\chi_l)]$,
        independent of $\Hubble$.  Plane-to-plane distances must be
        built from comoving differences, never as $D_s-D_l$.
  \item A massive near source adds a coupling term
        $-(D_{s1,s2}/D_{s2})\hat{\vecalpha}_{s1}(D_{s1}\vecbeta_1)$
        (eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_coupling}); for galaxy-scale sources it is a
        first-order effect and must be modelled.
  \item The measured ratio of Einstein radii is the distance ratio for an
        isothermal deflector ($\Brat=\etamea$; SIS or SIE, any
        ellipticity, any Einstein-radius convention) and
        $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$ for a power law.
  \item $\Brat$ is only weakly sensitive to cosmology: $S_w=-0.0330$ and
        $S_{\Omegam}=-0.0357$ at $(z_l,z_{s1},z_{s2})=(0.5,1,2)$, so a
        $1\%$ error in $\Brat$ corresponds to $|\delta w|\approx0.3$.
        One lens constrains a curve in $(\Omegam,w)$; a population
        constrains a point.
  \item A DSPL fit samples the lens model plus $\Brat$ (or the cosmology
        behind it).  The inner slope controls the width of the $\Brat$
        posterior through
        $\delta\Brat/\Brat=(\gp-1)\delta\etamea/\etamea+\ln\etamea\,\delta\gp$.
        Never fix $\thetaE^{(2)}/\thetaE^{(1)}$ from a reference
        cosmology.
\end{itemize}

The chain from data to cosmology is
\begin{equation*}
  \underbrace{\text{image positions}}_{\text{data}}
  \;\longrightarrow\;
  \underbrace{(\etamea,\gp)}_{\text{lens fit}}
  \;\xrightarrow{\;\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}\;}\;
  \underbrace{\Brat}_{\text{distance ratio}}
  \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bcancel}}\;}\;
  \underbrace{(\Omegam,\,w)}_{\text{cosmology}} .
\end{equation*}
The middle arrow needs the slope; the last arrow is Hubble-free but
weak.  Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass} develops the power-law lens
with a central point mass, and Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias} shows
how such a mass, left out of the model, biases both arrows.

\section{Problems}
\label{sec:mp_problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mp_hubble_free}
\textbf{$\Brat$ is Hubble-free.}  Write each of the four distances in
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bdef} using eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_DA} and show explicitly that
all factors of $c/\Hubble$ and $(1+z)^{-1}$ cancel, so that $\Brat$
depends only on $\{\chi_l,\chi_{s1},\chi_{s2}\}$.  Why does $z_l$ never
appear in a $(1+z)$ prefactor?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mp_isothermal}
\textbf{The isothermal ruler.}  For an isothermal deflector ($\gp=2$)
verify $\Brat=\etamea$ from eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_etaB}, and again directly
from the constant SIS deflection.  Interpret geometrically why the ratio
of Einstein radii equals the ratio of the two efficiencies.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mp_slope_lever}
\textbf{The slope lever arm.}  (a) From eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_etaB}, compute
$\partial\ln\Brat/\partial\gp$ at fixed $\etamea$ and show that it does
not depend on $\gp$.  (b) At the illustrative geometry ($\Brat=0.6742$) a
deflector has $\gp=1.9$.  What is $\etamea$, and what slope error
$\delta\gp$ produces a $1\%$ error in $\Brat$?  (c) Suppose an unmodelled
feature of the lens biases the fitted slope by $\delta\gp=+0.03$ while
$\etamea$ is measured perfectly.  What $\delta\Brat/\Brat$ results, and,
using $S_w$ from eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Snum}, what $\delta w$ would be
inferred?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mp_naive_distance}
\textbf{The cost of $D_{ds}=D_s-D_d$.}  (a) Show that
$(D_s-D_d)-D_{ds}=\chi_d[1/(1+z_s)-1/(1+z_d)]$ in a flat universe and
that it is nonzero for every $z_s>z_d>0$.  (b) Evaluate $D_d$, $D_s$,
$D_{ds}$ and $D_s-D_d$ at $z_d=0.5$, $z_s=2$ in flat $\Lambda$CDM with
$\Omegam=0.3$, $\Hubble=70$\,km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$.  (c) Compute the
``distance ratio'' that results if both lens-to-source distances in
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bdef} are built by naive subtraction, at the
illustrative geometry, and compare with eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_Bnum}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mp_limits}
\textbf{Limits of $\Brat$.}  (a) Show that $\Brat\to0$ as $z_{s1}\to z_l$
and $\Brat\to1$ as $z_{s2}\to z_{s1}$, and interpret both limits.
(b) Show that $\partial\Brat/\partial\chi_{s2}<0$.  (c) For flat
$\Lambda$CDM with $\Omegam=0.3$, $\chi(z\to\infty)$ is finite.  Compute it
in units of $c/\Hubble$ and find the floor
$\Brat(0.5,\,1.0,\,z_{s2}\to\infty)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mp_coupling}
\textbf{How big is lens--lens coupling?}  (a) Treat both the main lens
and the near source as singular isothermal spheres and derive
eq.~\eqref{eq:mp_couplingsize}.  (b) Show that the coupling vanishes as
$z_{s1}\to z_{s2}$ and tends to $(\sigma_1/\sigma_L)^2$ as
$z_{s1}\to z_l$.  (c) Evaluate the geometric factor at the illustrative
geometry and the coupling fraction for $\sigma_1/\sigma_L=0.4$ and $0.6$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mpi_shape_shear}
\textbf{What cancels in $\etamea$.}  (a) Carry a general shape factor
$f(q,\varphi)$ through eq.~\eqref{eq:mpi_sieratio} and confirm it cancels
for any $f$ common to both planes.  (b) Add an external shear produced by
mass at $z_l$.  Show that the whole lens-plane deflection field (SIE plus
shear) scales by $1/\Brat$ between the planes, so a model in which the
reduced shear differs between planes by that factor recovers
$\etamea=\Brat$ exactly.  (c) Explain why a shear produced by a perturber
between the two source planes does not cancel.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mpi_two_channel}
\textbf{Two channels.}  Derive eq.~\eqref{eq:mpi_twochannel} by taking
the logarithmic differential of $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$, and confirm
that for $\etamea<1$ and $\gp>1$ the two channels have opposite signs.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mpi_degeneracy}
\textbf{The single-lens degeneracy.}  In parametrisation (b) of
\S\ref{sec:mpi_sampling}, show that the likelihood is exactly constant
along level curves $\Brat(\Omegam,w)=\text{const}$ and that the local
slope of such a curve is $-S_{\Omegam}/S_w$.  Evaluate it at the
illustrative geometry, and check numerically that a step along that
direction changes $\Brat$ far less than the same step across it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mpi_error_budget}
\textbf{Where to spend observing time.}  A fit returns
$\etamea=0.72\pm0.01$ and $\gp=1.60\pm0.05$ with correlation coefficient
$\rho=+0.8$.  Propagate to $\sigma_\Brat/\Brat$ with
eq.~\eqref{eq:mpi_twochannel}, including the covariance.  Compare the
slope-driven and ratio-driven contributions.  Which dominates, and what
does this say about the relative value of better astrometry and a
detected radial image?  For a lens whose redshifts give $|S_w|=0.0330$,
what is $\sigma_w$?
\end{exercise}
