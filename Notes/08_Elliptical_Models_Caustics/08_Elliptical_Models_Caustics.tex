\chapter{Non-Axisymmetric Models and Critical Curves}
\label{ch:elliptical_models}

\begin{quote}
\textit{A circularly symmetric lens model is much too idealized and is unlikely to
describe real galaxies.  Therefore, considerable work has gone into developing
non-circularly symmetric models.  The breaking of the symmetry leads to
qualitatively new image configurations [\ldots]}
\hfill --- Narayan \& Bartelmann (1997), Sect.~3.5
\end{quote}

\noindent
In Chapters~\ref{ch:magnification} and~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models} we
developed the Jacobian formalism for magnification and applied it to
axisymmetric lens models: the point mass and the singular isothermal
sphere (SIS).  These models have a continuous rotational symmetry that
makes the mathematics tractable but the phenomenology limited.  The SIS,
for instance, can produce at most two images of a point source, and its
critical curve is a perfect circle.

Real galaxies, however, are elliptical.  Their mass
distributions have axis ratios $q \sim 0.5$--$0.9$, and they live in
environments that contribute external tidal shear.  Breaking circular
symmetry introduces qualitatively new phenomena.  Critical curves
deform from circles into ellipses and ovals; caustics develop a
diamond (astroid) shape with cusps and folds; sources can be
quadruply as well as doubly imaged, producing Einstein crosses and
arcs; and the topology of the image configuration (how many images
exist, their parities, and their magnifications) is governed
entirely by the position of the source relative to the caustics.

This chapter introduces the two standard non-axisymmetric models,
the SIS with external shear and the singular isothermal ellipsoid, and
then develops the theory of critical curves, caustics, and image
topology on which the rest of strong lensing theory rests.

\section{Introduction: Why Non-Axisymmetric Models Matter}
\label{sec:elliptical_intro}

The singular isothermal sphere (Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}) is
a useful starting point for galaxy-scale lensing.  Its velocity
dispersion $\sigma_v$ maps directly onto observable galaxy kinematics,
and its Einstein radius $\thetaE = 4\pi\sigma_v^2 \Dds/(\speedoflight^2
\Ds)$ sets the angular scale of lensing.  The SIS has one basic
limitation, its perfect circular symmetry.

Observations of strong lens systems show three things that an SIS
cannot account for.  Quadruple images are common: about 20--30\% of
known galaxy-scale lens systems are quads (four bright images plus a
faint central image), which an SIS cannot produce.  Giant luminous
arcs in galaxy clusters are tangentially stretched images that trace
out the critical curves of the cluster potential, and these arcs are
not perfect circles, so the mass distribution is not axisymmetric.
Finally, astrometric anomalies in lens models require ellipticity
and/or external shear to achieve acceptable fits.

The simplest way to break axial symmetry is to add a constant
\emph{external shear} to the SIS potential.  The resulting
SIS + shear model already produces diamond caustics, quad images, and
fold/cusp configurations.  The more physically motivated
\emph{singular isothermal ellipsoid} (SIE) achieves the same through
an intrinsically elliptical mass distribution and is the model used
in modern lens-modeling codes such as \texttt{lenstronomy} and
\texttt{PyAutoLens}.

\section{SIS + External Shear}
\label{sec:sis_shear}

The simplest non-axisymmetric lens model augments the SIS potential
with a constant external tidal shear $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$.
This shear arises from the tidal gravitational field of
neighboring galaxies or the cluster environment in which the lens
galaxy resides.

\subsection{Lensing Potential}

The lensing potential is
(Congdon \& Keeton eqs.~6.1--6.2):
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \psiL(\vectheta) = \thetaE\,|\vectheta|
    + \frac{1}{2}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}\,
    (\theta_1^2 - \theta_2^2)
    }
    \label{eq:psi_sis_shear}
\end{equation}
where $|\vectheta| = \sqrt{\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2}$ and we have
chosen the shear to be aligned with the $\theta_1$-axis (the general
case involves a rotation by the shear angle $\phi_\gamma$).  The
first term is the SIS potential; the second is the external shear
contribution.

\begin{annotation}[Shear angle, sign convention, units]
For a shear of strength $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$ at angle $\phi_\gamma$ the
quadratic potential is
$\psiL_\gamma = \tfrac12\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}|\vectheta|^2\cos 2(\phi-\phi_\gamma)
= \tfrac12\big[\gamma_1(\theta_1^2-\theta_2^2) + 2\gamma_2\theta_1\theta_2\big]$,
with $(\gamma_1,\gamma_2) = \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}(\cos 2\phi_\gamma,\sin 2\phi_\gamma)$;
eq.~\eqref{eq:psi_sis_shear} is the case $\phi_\gamma = 0$.  Three
conventions need care.
(i)~The components depend on $2\phi_\gamma$: $\phi_\gamma$ and
$\phi_\gamma+\pi$ are the same shear (a spin-2 quantity, like an ellipse).
(ii)~With this sign, for $\gamma_2 = 0$ the Jacobian is
$\mathrm{diag}(1-\gamma_1,\,1+\gamma_1)$, so images are stretched
\emph{along} $\phi_\gamma$.
(iii)~The perturbing mass does \emph{not} sit at $\phi_\gamma$: a distant point
mass $m$ on the $+\theta_1$ axis at distance $D$ produces, at the origin,
$\gamma_1 = -m/D^2 < 0$, $\gamma_2 = 0$, $\kappab = 0$, i.e.\
$\phi_\gamma = 90^\circ$.  Images are stretched \emph{tangentially} about the
perturber, exactly as arcs are stretched tangentially about a cluster.  Codes
and papers differ on whether they quote $\phi_\gamma$ or the direction to the
perturber; check which before comparing.
Units: $\psiL$ is in arcsec$^2$ (e.g.\ $\thetaE|\vectheta|$), $\vecalpha$ in
arcsec, $\kappab$ and $\gammaL$ are dimensionless.
\end{annotation}


\subsection{Deflection Angle}

The deflection angle components are:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_1 = \frac{\partial\psiL}{\partial\theta_1}
    = \thetaE\,\frac{\theta_1}{|\vectheta|}
    + \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}\,\theta_1 \,,
    \qquad
    \alpha_2 = \frac{\partial\psiL}{\partial\theta_2}
    = \thetaE\,\frac{\theta_2}{|\vectheta|}
    - \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}\,\theta_2 \,.
    \label{eq:alpha_sis_shear}
\end{equation}
The SIS contribution deflects radially inward; the shear contribution
stretches along $\theta_1$ and compresses along $\theta_2$ (for
$\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}} > 0$).

\subsection{Convergence and Shear}

The convergence of the SIS + shear model is the same as the pure SIS
(the external shear does not add mass):
\begin{equation}
    \kappab(\vectheta) = \frac{\thetaE}{2\,|\vectheta|} \,.
    \label{eq:kappa_sis_shear}
\end{equation}
The total shear now has contributions from both the SIS and the
external shear.  For the SIS alone,
$|\gammaL_{\mathrm{SIS}}| = \thetaE/(2|\vectheta|) = \kappab$.
The external shear adds $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$ aligned with the
coordinate axes, so the total shear field is anisotropic and
position-dependent.

\subsection{Critical Curves}

The critical curves are defined by $\det\Amat = 0$.  Start from the
Hessian of the SIS$+$shear potential
$\psiL = \thetaE|\vectheta| + \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}(\theta_1^2 - \theta_2^2)$
and let $u = \thetaE/|\vectheta|$, $g = \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$.  In
polar coordinates $(\theta_1,\theta_2) = (|\vectheta|\cos\phi,
|\vectheta|\sin\phi)$ the Jacobian entries are
\begin{equation*}
    \Amat_{11} = 1 - g - u\sin^2\phi,
    \quad
    \Amat_{22} = 1 + g - u\cos^2\phi,
    \quad
    \Amat_{12} = \Amat_{21} = u\sin\phi\cos\phi,
\end{equation*}
and a short expansion gives
\begin{equation*}
    \det\Amat \;=\; (1-g^2) - u\,(1 - g\cos 2\phi).
\end{equation*}
Setting $\det\Amat = 0$ yields the critical curve:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\phi)
    \;=\; \thetaE\,
        \frac{1 - \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}\cos 2\phi}{1 - \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^2} \,.
    }
    \label{eq:crit_sis_shear}
\end{equation}
The derivation is verified symbolically in
\mathlink{08_Elliptical_Models_Caustics/critical\_curves\_caustics.wl}{critical\_curves\_caustics.wl}.
For $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}} = 0$ this reduces to $\theta = \thetaE$
(the SIS critical circle).  For $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}} > 0$, the
critical curve is an oval: elongated along the
$\theta_2$-axis (perpendicular to the shear direction) and compressed
along $\theta_1$ (approximately elliptical for small
$\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$; the deviation from a true ellipse reaches
${\sim}1.5\%$ at $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}} = 0.1$).

\begin{derivation}[The SIS$+$shear Jacobian and its determinant, line by line]
\textit{SIS Hessian.}  With $\theta = |\vectheta|$ and
$\partial_i\theta = \theta_i/\theta$,
\[
  \partial_i(\thetaE\theta) = \thetaE\frac{\theta_i}{\theta},
  \qquad
  \partial_j\partial_i(\thetaE\theta)
  = \thetaE\Big(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\theta} - \frac{\theta_i\theta_j}{\theta^3}\Big)
  = \frac{\thetaE}{\theta^3}\big(\delta_{ij}\theta^2 - \theta_i\theta_j\big).
\]
\textit{Shear Hessian.}  $\partial_1^2\psiL_\gamma = g$,
$\partial_2^2\psiL_\gamma = -g$, $\partial_1\partial_2\psiL_\gamma = 0$.

\textit{Polar substitution.}  $\theta_2^2/\theta^3 = \sin^2\phi/\theta$,
$\theta_1^2/\theta^3 = \cos^2\phi/\theta$, $\theta_1\theta_2/\theta^3 =
\sin\phi\cos\phi/\theta$.  With $u = \thetaE/\theta$ and
$\Amat_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - \partial_i\partial_j\psiL$ this gives the three
entries quoted above (note $\Amat_{12} = -\partial_1\partial_2\psiL =
+u\sin\phi\cos\phi$).

\textit{Determinant} ($s = \sin\phi$, $c = \cos\phi$):
\begin{align*}
  \det\Amat
  &= (1-g-us^2)(1+g-uc^2) - u^2s^2c^2 \\
  &= (1-g^2) - u\big[(1+g)s^2 + (1-g)c^2\big] + u^2s^2c^2 - u^2s^2c^2 \\
  &= (1-g^2) - u\,(1 - g\cos 2\phi),
\end{align*}
using $s^2 + c^2 = 1$ and $c^2 - s^2 = \cos 2\phi$ in the bracket.  The $u^2$
terms cancel identically because the SIS Hessian has a zero eigenvalue in the
radial direction (rank one); the same fact gives $\det\Amat = 1 - 2\kappab$ for
every isothermal lens (box in \S\ref{sec:sie_deflection} below).

\textit{Solve.}  $\det\Amat = 0 \Rightarrow u = (1-g^2)/(1-g\cos2\phi)$, and
$\theta = \thetaE/u$ is eq.~\eqref{eq:crit_sis_shear}.  As a check,
$\theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(0) = \thetaE/(1+g)$ and
$\theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\pi/2) = \thetaE/(1-g)$, i.e.\ $0.909''$ and
$1.111''$ for $\thetaE = 1''$, $g = 0.1$.
\end{derivation}


\begin{remark}
The critical curve is elongated perpendicular to the shear
direction.  The reason is that the shear stretches images
along $\theta_1$, so that the magnification diverges at smaller
$\theta$ in that direction, pushing the critical curve inward along
$\theta_1$ and outward along $\theta_2$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Caustics: The Diamond (Astroid)}

The caustics are obtained by mapping the critical curves through the
lens equation $\vecbeta = \vectheta_{\mathrm{crit}} -
\vecalpha(\vectheta_{\mathrm{crit}})$.  For the SIS + shear model,
the tangential caustic takes the shape of a \emph{diamond} or
\emph{astroid}, a four-cusped curve.

In parametric form, the caustic is:
\begin{align}
    \beta_1(\phi) &= \theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\phi)\cos\phi
    - \thetaE\cos\phi - \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}\,
    \theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\phi)\cos\phi \,,
    \label{eq:caustic_sis_shear_1} \\
    \beta_2(\phi) &= \theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\phi)\sin\phi
    - \thetaE\sin\phi + \gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}\,
    \theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\phi)\sin\phi \,.
    \label{eq:caustic_sis_shear_2}
\end{align}
This curve has four cusps (at $\phi = 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2$) and
four fold segments connecting them.  The size of the diamond is
proportional to $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$: for
$\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}} = 0$, the caustic degenerates to a point
(the SIS point caustic), and for larger shear values, the diamond
grows.

\begin{derivation}[Closed form of the SIS$+$shear caustic, its cusps and area]
Insert $\theta_{\mathrm{crit}}$ into eq.~\eqref{eq:caustic_sis_shear_1} and use
$(1-g)/(1-g^2) = 1/(1+g)$:
\begin{align*}
  \beta_1 &= \cos\phi\,\big[(1-g)\theta_{\mathrm{crit}} - \thetaE\big]
  = \thetaE\cos\phi\Big[\frac{1-g\cos2\phi}{1+g} - 1\Big]\\
  &= -\frac{g\thetaE\cos\phi\,(1+\cos2\phi)}{1+g}
  = -\frac{2g\thetaE}{1+g}\cos^3\phi ,
\end{align*}
with $1+\cos2\phi = 2\cos^2\phi$.  In the same way, with
$(1+g)/(1-g^2) = 1/(1-g)$ and $1-\cos2\phi = 2\sin^2\phi$,
$\beta_2 = +\dfrac{2g\thetaE}{1-g}\sin^3\phi$.

\textit{Cusps.}  $d\beta_1/d\phi = \frac{6g\thetaE}{1+g}\cos^2\phi\sin\phi$ and
$d\beta_2/d\phi = \frac{6g\thetaE}{1-g}\sin^2\phi\cos\phi$ vanish together iff
$\sin\phi\cos\phi = 0$: the curve stops and reverses at
$\phi = 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2$ (cusps); everywhere else its tangent is
non-zero (folds).

\textit{Size.}  Semi-axes $a = 2g\thetaE/(1+g)$ along $\beta_1$ and
$b = 2g\thetaE/(1-g)$ along $\beta_2$ ($0.182''$ and $0.222''$ for
$g = 0.1$, $\thetaE = 1''$).  For the curve $(a\cos^3\phi, b\sin^3\phi)$ the
enclosed area is $\big|\oint\beta_1\,d\beta_2\big| = 3ab\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^4\phi
\sin^2\phi\,d\phi = 3\pi ab/8$, i.e.\
$3\pi g^2\thetaE^2/[2(1-g^2)] = 0.0476$~arcsec$^2$ for $g = 0.1$
(Exercise~\ref{ex:sis_shear_crit}).
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Which way does each cusp point?]
The critical point on the $+\theta_1$ axis, at $\thetaE/(1+g)$, maps to
$\beta_1 = -2g\thetaE/(1+g) < 0$: the \emph{opposite} side.  The critical
point on the $+\theta_2$ axis, at $\thetaE/(1-g)$, maps to
$\beta_2 = +2g\thetaE/(1-g) > 0$: the \emph{same} side.  The caustic is
therefore not the critical curve shrunk in place.  Both curves have their long
axis along $\theta_2$ ($b > a$), perpendicular to $\phi_\gamma$.  As
$g \to 0$, $a, b \to 2g\thetaE \to 0$: the SIS point caustic is recovered.
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Image Configurations}

The image configuration for the SIS + shear model depends on the
source position relative to the diamond caustic.  A source outside
the diamond has two images (a double): one outside the critical curve
(Type~I, positive parity) and one inside (Type~II, negative parity,
demagnified).  A source inside the diamond has four images (a quad),
two outside the critical curve with positive parity and two inside
with negative parity.  A source on a fold has three images, two of
which merge on the critical curve and are highly magnified, and a
source on a cusp has three images, with the merging triplet producing
the characteristic cusp configuration.

\begin{remark}
The SIS is singular, so there is no separate radial critical curve
or radial caustic.  The SIS + shear model therefore has only a
tangential caustic (the diamond).  A non-singular model (e.g., a
softened isothermal sphere + shear) would also have a radial caustic
enclosing the tangential one.
\end{remark}

\section{The Singular Isothermal Ellipsoid (SIE)}
\label{sec:sie}

The singular isothermal ellipsoid (SIE) is the standard
model for galaxy-scale strong lensing.  Rather than adding shear
externally, the SIE builds ellipticity directly into the mass
distribution.

\subsection{Surface Mass Density}

The SIE surface mass density is
(Kormann, Schneider \& Bartelmann 1994; Congdon \& Keeton eq.~6.13):
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Sigma(\xi_1, \xi_2)
    = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{2\Gnewton}
    \,\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{q^2\,\xi_1^2 + \xi_2^2}}
    }
    \label{eq:sie_sigma}
\end{equation}
where $q \leq 1$ is the axis ratio ($q = b/a$, with $a$ the
semi-major axis aligned along $\xi_1$), $\sigma_v$ is the velocity
dispersion, and $(\xi_1, \xi_2)$ are physical coordinates in the
lens plane.  The iso-density contours $q^2\xi_1^2 + \xi_2^2 =
\mathrm{const}$ are ellipses of axis ratio $q$ with the major axis
along $\xi_1$.  For $q = 1$ this reduces to the SIS surface mass
density $\Sigma = \sigma_v^2/(2\Gnewton\xi)$.

The factor $\sqrt{q}$ ensures that the total mass enclosed within a
given elliptical radius is consistent with the isothermal profile.

\begin{annotation}[Conventions: $q$, $\epsilon$, $e$, and the $\sqrt{q}$ normalization]
Three ellipticity variables appear in this chapter and in modeling codes: the
axis ratio $q = b/a$; the flattening $\epsilon = 1-q$ (used in
\S\ref{sec:which_multipoles}); and $e = (1-q)/(1+q)$ (the ``equivalent shear''
of Exercise~\ref{ex:sie_caustic}).  For $q = 0.7$: $\epsilon = 0.3$ but
$e = 0.176$.  Putting one where the other is meant is nearly a factor-of-two error (1.7 at $q = 0.7$).

The $\sqrt{q}$ can be absorbed into a more symmetric form,
$\kappab = \thetaE\big/\big(2\sqrt{q\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2/q}\big)$, which is
the same function as eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_kappa}.  With this normalization the
tangential critical curve encloses area $\pi\thetaE^2$ and mean convergence
exactly 1 for every $q$ (box in the critical-curve subsection below).  That is
what ``SIS-equivalent'' Einstein radius means here.

Units check: $[\sigma_v^2/(\Gnewton\xi)] =
(\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-2}})/(\mathrm{cm^3\,g^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\cdot\mathrm{cm}) =
\mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}}$, a surface density, as it should be.
\end{annotation}


Converting to angular coordinates via $\vecxi = \Dd\vectheta$ and
dividing by the critical surface density gives the dimensionless
convergence
\begin{equation}
    \kappab(\theta_1, \theta_2)
    \;=\; \frac{\Sigma}{\Sigmacr}
    \;=\; \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{2\sqrt{q^2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2}} \,,
    \label{eq:sie_kappa}
\end{equation}
where $\thetaE = 4\pi\sigma_v^2\Dds/(\speedoflight^2\Ds)$ is the
SIS-equivalent Einstein radius (the Einstein radius of the circular
isothermal sphere with the same $\sigma_v$).

\subsection{Deflection Angle}
\label{sec:sie_deflection}

The deflection angle for the SIE, first given in closed form by
Kormann, Schneider \& Bartelmann (1994, hereafter KSB94), is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \alpha_1 = \frac{\thetaE\,\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{1 - q^2}}
    \,\arctan\!\!\left(
    \frac{\theta_1\,\sqrt{1 - q^2}}
         {\sqrt{q^2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2}}\right),
    \qquad
    \alpha_2 = \frac{\thetaE\,\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{1 - q^2}}
    \,\operatorname{arctanh}\!\!\left(
    \frac{\theta_2\,\sqrt{1 - q^2}}
         {\sqrt{q^2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2}}\right).}
    \label{eq:sie_alpha}
\end{equation}
The asymmetry ($\arctan$ along the major axis, $\operatorname{arctanh}$
along the minor) is intrinsic to the elliptical geometry.  Rather
than reproducing the long convolution integral that leads to these
forms (a contour-integral computation spanning several pages of
KSB94), we give a shorter derivation that shows eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_alpha}
is consistent with the SIE convergence \eqref{eq:sie_kappa} by using
the scale-invariance of the lens.  Every step is verified symbolically
in
\mathlink{08_Elliptical_Models_Caustics/sie\_deflection.wl}{sie\_deflection.wl}.

\paragraph{Step 1: scale invariance and the lensing potential.}
The SIE convergence \eqref{eq:sie_kappa} is homogeneous of degree
$-1$ in $\vectheta$,
\begin{equation*}
    \kappab(\lambda\vectheta) = \lambda^{-1}\kappab(\vectheta)
    \qquad (\lambda > 0).
\end{equation*}
Since $\alpha_i = \partial\psiL/\partial\theta_i$ and
$\vecalpha = \kappab * \vectheta/\pi$ is a convolution with a
homogeneous-of-degree-$-1$ kernel, the deflection is homogeneous of
degree $0$ and the potential $\psiL$ is homogeneous of degree $1$.
Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions then gives
\begin{equation}
    \psiL(\theta_1, \theta_2)
    \;=\; \theta_1\,\alpha_1(\theta_1,\theta_2)
        + \theta_2\,\alpha_2(\theta_1,\theta_2) \,.
    \label{eq:sie_euler}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 2: ansatz and its structure.}
For the SIE, any deflection consistent with the elliptical symmetry
must involve only $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, and the elliptical radius
$F(\vectheta) \equiv \sqrt{q^2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2}$.
Dimensional and homogeneity requirements force the argument of the
inverse trig/hyperbolic function to be dimensionless and of degree
$0$.  Natural choices are
$\theta_1\sqrt{1-q^2}/F$ and $\theta_2\sqrt{1-q^2}/F$; the
coefficients $\sqrt{1-q^2}$ are fixed uniquely (up to sign) by the
requirement that $\alpha_i \to \thetaE\theta_i/|\vectheta|$ in the
SIS limit $q\to 1$.  This motivates the KSB94 ansatz
\eqref{eq:sie_alpha}.

\paragraph{Step 3: verify the gradient condition $\alpha_i = \partial_i\psiL$.}
Combining \eqref{eq:sie_euler} and \eqref{eq:sie_alpha} and
differentiating gives
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial\psiL}{\partial\theta_i}
    \;=\; \alpha_i
        + \theta_j\,\frac{\partial\alpha_j}{\partial\theta_i} \,,
\end{equation*}
so the gradient condition is equivalent to
$\theta_j(\partial\alpha_j/\partial\theta_i) = 0$.  Direct
differentiation of \eqref{eq:sie_alpha} shows that all cross terms
cancel after using the identity
$\partial_i(1/F) = -q^{2(1-\delta_{i2})}\theta_i/F^3$ and the
derivative of $\arctan$/$\operatorname{arctanh}$.  The detailed
cancellation is carried out symbolically in
\texttt{sie\_deflection.wl}; the result is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial\psiL}{\partial\theta_1} = \alpha_1,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial\psiL}{\partial\theta_2} = \alpha_2.
    \label{eq:sie_gradient_check}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[The cancellation in Step 3, written out]
Let $k \equiv \sqrt{1-q^2}$, $F = \sqrt{q^2\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2}$,
$\theta^2 = \theta_1^2+\theta_2^2$, and call the two arguments
$x = k\theta_1/F$, $y = k\theta_2/F$.  The prefactor is
$C = \thetaE\sqrt{q}/k$.

\textit{(1) Inner derivatives} (quotient rule, $\partial_1F = q^2\theta_1/F$,
$\partial_2F = \theta_2/F$):
\begin{align*}
  \partial_1x &= \frac{k(F^2-q^2\theta_1^2)}{F^3} = \frac{k\theta_2^2}{F^3}, &
  \partial_2x &= -\frac{k\theta_1\theta_2}{F^3},\\
  \partial_1y &= -\frac{kq^2\theta_1\theta_2}{F^3}, &
  \partial_2y &= \frac{k(F^2-\theta_2^2)}{F^3} = \frac{kq^2\theta_1^2}{F^3}.
\end{align*}
\textit{(2) Outer derivatives.}  Because
$F^2 + k^2\theta_1^2 = q^2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2 + (1-q^2)\theta_1^2 = \theta^2$
and $F^2 - k^2\theta_2^2 = q^2\theta^2$,
\[
  \frac{d\arctan x}{dx} = \frac{1}{1+x^2} = \frac{F^2}{\theta^2},
  \qquad
  \frac{d\operatorname{arctanh} y}{dy} = \frac{1}{1-y^2} = \frac{F^2}{q^2\theta^2}.
\]
\textit{(3) Chain rule.}
\[
  \partial_1\alpha_1 = C\frac{F^2}{\theta^2}\frac{k\theta_2^2}{F^3}
  = \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}\,\theta_2^2}{\theta^2F},\qquad
  \partial_2\alpha_2 = C\frac{F^2}{q^2\theta^2}\frac{kq^2\theta_1^2}{F^3}
  = \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}\,\theta_1^2}{\theta^2F},
\]
\[
  \partial_2\alpha_1 = \partial_1\alpha_2
  = -\frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}\,\theta_1\theta_2}{\theta^2F}.
\]
\textit{(4) Consequences.}  $\partial_2\alpha_1 = \partial_1\alpha_2$: the field
is curl-free, so a potential exists.  And
\[
  \theta_j\partial_1\alpha_j
  = \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{\theta^2F}\big(\theta_1\theta_2^2 - \theta_2\theta_1\theta_2\big) = 0,
  \qquad
  \theta_j\partial_2\alpha_j
  = \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{\theta^2F}\big(-\theta_1^2\theta_2 + \theta_2\theta_1^2\big) = 0,
\]
which is the condition of Step~3.  (With a symmetric Hessian,
$\theta_j\partial_i\alpha_j = \theta_j\partial_j\alpha_i$, which vanishes by
Euler's theorem because each $\alpha_i$ is homogeneous of degree~0.)
\end{derivation}


\paragraph{Step 4: verify $\nabla^2\psiL = 2\kappab$.}
Computing
$\nabla^2\psiL = \partial_1\alpha_1 + \partial_2\alpha_2$ from
\eqref{eq:sie_alpha}:
\begin{align*}
    \partial_1\alpha_1
    &= \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{1-q^2}}\;
       \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_1}\!
            \arctan\!\!\left(\frac{\theta_1\sqrt{1-q^2}}{F}\right)
     = \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{1-q^2}}
       \cdot \frac{F^2}{\theta^2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1-q^2}\,\theta_2^2}{F^{3}} \\
    &= \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}\,\theta_2^2}{\theta^2 F},
    \\[2pt]
    \partial_2\alpha_2
    &= \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}\,\theta_1^2}{\theta^2 F},
\end{align*}
after applying the chain rule to $\partial_i F = q^{2(1-\delta_{i2})}\theta_i/F$
and simplifying, with $\theta^2 = \theta_1^2+\theta_2^2$.  Adding:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2\psiL \;=\;
        \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}\,(\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2)}{\theta^2 F}
    \;=\; \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{F}
    \;=\; 2\,\kappab(\vectheta),
    \label{eq:sie_poisson_check}
\end{equation}
matching \eqref{eq:sie_kappa}.  This completes the verification.

\begin{derivation}[Every isothermal lens has $\det\Amat = 1 - 2\kappab$]
From the Step-3 box (and Step~4), the SIE Hessian is
\[
  \partial_i\partial_j\psiL = \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{\theta^2F}
  \begin{pmatrix}\theta_2^2 & -\theta_1\theta_2\\ -\theta_1\theta_2 & \theta_1^2\end{pmatrix}
  = 2\kappab\;\hat{t}\,\hat{t}^{\mathsf T},
  \qquad \hat{t} = \frac{(-\theta_2,\theta_1)}{\theta},
\]
since $\thetaE\sqrt{q}/F = 2\kappab$.  Hence
$\Amat = \mathbb{I} - 2\kappab\,\hat{t}\hat{t}^{\mathsf T}$.  The radial
direction has eigenvalue $1$ and the tangential direction $1-2\kappab$, so
\[
  \det\Amat = 1 - 2\kappab, \qquad |\gammaL| = \kappab, \qquad
  \mu = \frac{1}{1-2\kappab}.
\]
This holds for \emph{any} isothermal potential $\psiL = \theta\,G(\phi)$.  In
polar components $\partial_\theta^2\psiL = 0$, the mixed term
$\partial_\theta(\theta^{-1}\partial_\phi\psiL) = 0$, and the tangential entry
is $(G+G'')/\theta = 2\kappab$; compare eq.~\eqref{eq:ring_crit}.
Consequences: (i)~the radial eigenvalue is identically 1, so there is no
radial critical curve; (ii)~the tangential critical curve is the contour
$2\kappab = 1$; (iii)~an image's magnification follows from the local
$\kappab$ alone, a fast hand check (\S\ref{sec:image_configurations}).
\end{derivation}


\paragraph{Step 5: the SIS limit $q \to 1$.}
As $q\to 1^-$, $\sqrt{1-q^2}\to 0$ and the arguments of the inverse
functions vanish; using $\arctan x / x \to 1$ and $\operatorname{arctanh} x/x \to 1$,
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_i \;\to\; \thetaE\,\sqrt{q}\,\frac{\theta_i}{\sqrt{q^2\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2}}
          \;\to\; \thetaE\,\frac{\theta_i}{|\vectheta|}\,,
\end{equation*}
which is the SIS deflection.  The limit is taken symbolically in
\texttt{sie\_deflection.wl}.

\subsection{Critical Curves and Caustics of the SIE}

The critical curves of the SIE are found by solving
$\det\Amat(\vectheta) = 0$.  Unlike the SIS (where the critical
curve is a circle), the SIE tangential critical curve is an
ellipse, $q^2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2 = q\thetaE^2$, with the same
axis ratio $q$ as the mass (it is the contour $2\kappab = 1$ of
eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_kappa}).  Its shape depends on $q$.

For axis ratios $q \lesssim 0.9$ the SIE has a tangential critical
curve that is an ellipse close to the Einstein radius, elongated
\emph{along} the major axis of the mass distribution: with $q < 1$
and mass elongated along $\theta_1$, the critical curve crosses the
$\theta_1$-axis at $\pm\thetaE/\sqrt{q}$ and the $\theta_2$-axis at
$\pm\thetaE\sqrt{q}$, so it stretches farther along $\theta_1$ than
along $\theta_2$.  Its tangential caustic is a diamond (astroid)
similar to that of the SIS + shear case, with four cusps; for
$q < 1$ the diamond is also wider along the mass major axis
$\beta_1$.

The SIE, being singular at the origin, does not have a radial
critical curve (just as the SIS does not).  A non-singular
isothermal ellipsoid would also have a radial critical curve mapping
to a radial caustic.

\begin{derivation}[The SIE critical curve is an exact ellipse of area $\pi\thetaE^2$; its cusps]
\textit{Critical curve.}  $2\kappab = 1 \Leftrightarrow F = \thetaE\sqrt{q}
\Leftrightarrow q^2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2 = q\thetaE^2$.  This is an ellipse
with semi-axes $\thetaE/\sqrt{q}$ (along $\theta_1$) and $\thetaE\sqrt{q}$
(along $\theta_2$), the same axis ratio $q$ and orientation as the mass.  Its
area is $\pi(\thetaE/\sqrt{q})(\thetaE\sqrt{q}) = \pi\thetaE^2$.

\textit{Mass inside it.}  Use $\theta_1 = r\cos t/q$, $\theta_2 = r\sin t$, so
$F = r$ and $d^2\theta = (r/q)\,dr\,dt$:
\[
  \int\kappab\,d^2\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}
  \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{2r}\,\frac{r}{q}\,dr\,dt = \pi\thetaE^2 .
\]
The mean convergence inside the critical curve is exactly 1 for every $q$.
This is the elliptical analogue of $\bar\kappab(\thetaE) = 1$, and it is why
$M_{\mathrm{E}} = \pi\thetaE^2\Dd^2\Sigmacr$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing},
eq.~\eqref{eq:mass_einstein}) holds for the SIE without any $q$ correction.

\textit{Cusps.}  At a critical point let $\vec{e}$ be the zero-eigenvalue
eigenvector of $\Amat$.  The caustic tangent is
$d\vecbeta/ds = \Amat\,d\vectheta/ds$, which vanishes (a cusp) exactly where the
critical-curve tangent $d\vectheta/ds$ is parallel to $\vec{e}$.  For the SIE,
$\vec{e} = \hat{t} \propto (-\theta_2,\theta_1)$ and the curve tangent is
perpendicular to $\nabla F$.  Since
$(-\theta_2,\theta_1)\cdot\nabla F = \theta_1\theta_2(1-q^2)/F$, the cusps sit
where $\theta_1\theta_2 = 0$: the four points on the axes.  All other points
are folds.

\textit{Cusp positions.}  Map $\vectheta = (\thetaE/\sqrt{q},0)$ and
$(0,\thetaE\sqrt{q})$ through eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_alpha}.  The arguments become
$k/q$ and $k$, giving
\[
  \beta_1^{\mathrm{cusp}} = \frac{\thetaE}{\sqrt{q}} - \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{k}\arctan\frac{k}{q},
  \qquad
  \beta_2^{\mathrm{cusp}} = \thetaE\sqrt{q} - \frac{\thetaE\sqrt{q}}{k}\operatorname{arctanh}k .
\]
For $q = 0.7$ these are $0.263\,\thetaE$ and $-0.213\,\thetaE$; for $q = 0.8$
they are $0.159\,\thetaE$ and $-0.139\,\thetaE$.  The negative sign means the
$\theta_2$-axis cusp lands on the opposite side.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[The cut: what replaces the radial caustic of a singular lens]
For a singular isothermal lens $\vecalpha$ does not vanish at the centre.  As
$\vectheta \to 0$ along direction $\phi$, $\vecalpha$ tends to a finite,
direction-dependent limit, so an infinitesimal circle about the centre maps to
a closed curve in the source plane.  This curve is often called the
\emph{cut} or pseudo-caustic.  For $q = 0.7$ it crosses the axes at
$|\beta_1| = 0.932\,\thetaE$ and $|\beta_2| = 1.049\,\thetaE$.  It splits the
source plane by image count: one image outside the cut, two between the cut
and the diamond, four inside the diamond.  The SIS limit is the familiar
statement that $\beta > \thetaE$ gives one image.

As $q$ decreases the diamond grows faster than the cut.  For $q < 0.394$ the
$\beta_1$ cusps poke outside it (\emph{naked cusps}).  A source in the
protruding tip then has three images: for $q = 0.3$, a source halfway between
the cut and the cusp tip on the $\beta_1$ axis gives 3 images in
\texttt{problems\_08.wl}.
\end{annotation}

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}: the SIE Einstein-radius ratio]
In a double-source-plane lens, ignoring the mass of the near source, number
the planes as in Part~V: plane~1 is the near source, plane~2 the far one.  The
same deflector then has $\kappab_1 = \Brat\,\kappab_2$, where
$\Brat = D_{l,s1}D_{s2}/(D_{s1}D_{l,s2}) < 1$ is the ratio of $\Dds/\Ds$ for
the two sources, a ratio of four angular-diameter distances.
The SIE enters only through $\thetaE \propto \Dds/\Ds$, so plane~1 sees the
same SIE with $\thetaE^{(1)} = \Brat\,\thetaE^{(2)}$.  Its critical ellipse is
the plane-2 ellipse scaled by $\Brat$ along \emph{both} axes, so the measured
Einstein-radius ratio $\etamea = \thetaE^{(1)}/\thetaE^{(2)}$ equals $\Brat$
independently of $q$.  Any convention that
multiplies $\thetaE$ by a $q$-dependent factor, the $\sqrt{q}$ here or another,
cancels in the ratio.  Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl} uses exactly this to
read $\Brat$, and hence cosmology, off a DSPL with an elliptical main
deflector.
\end{bridge}


The cross-section for quad lensing (the area enclosed by the
tangential caustic) increases with ellipticity.  For $q = 0.7$, the
caustic area is roughly $0.066\,\thetaE^2$, while for $q = 0.5$ it
can be several times larger.  More elliptical galaxies are
therefore more likely to produce quad lens systems.

\section{The Angular Structure of Lenses: Multipole Expansion}
\label{sec:multipoles}

The SIE and the SIS${}+{}$shear model are two \emph{specific} ways of
breaking circular symmetry.  A more systematic view treats any lens
potential as a circularly symmetric \emph{monopole} plus a series of
\emph{angular multipoles}.  This is the natural language for asking
\emph{which} deviations from circular symmetry a set of images can
constrain, a question that, as \citet{schneider_kochanek_wambsganss_2006}
emphasize (Part~2, \S4.4--4.5, Kochanek), gravitational lensing answers
better than any other probe of galaxy potentials.

\subsection{The Multipole Series}
\label{sec:multipole_series}

Expand the lensing potential in azimuthal harmonics of the polar angle
$\varphi$ (with $\vectheta = \theta(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$):
\begin{equation}
    \psiL(\theta,\varphi)
    = \psiL_0(\theta)
    + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \psiL_m(\theta)\,
      \cos\!\big[m(\varphi - \varphi_m)\big],
    \label{eq:multipole_series}
\end{equation}
where $\psiL_0$ is the monopole (the axisymmetric part of
Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}) and each $m \ge 1$ term has its own
orientation $\varphi_m$.  The convergence of a single multipole follows
from the two-dimensional Poisson equation $\nabla^2\psiL = 2\kappab$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}), with the Laplacian in polar coordinates
$\nabla^2 = \partial_\theta^2 + \theta^{-1}\partial_\theta
+ \theta^{-2}\partial_\varphi^2$.

For the \emph{isothermal-scaling} multipole
$\psiL_m = a_m\,\theta\,\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]$ (radial dependence
$\propto\theta$, matching the flat rotation curve of the SIS), a direct
computation gives
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \kappab_m(\theta,\varphi)
    = \frac{a_m\,(1-m^2)}{2\,\theta}\,
      \cos\!\big[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)\big] \; }
    \label{eq:kappa_multipole}
\end{equation}
(verified symbolically in the companion script; cf.\ the equivalent
relation in \citet{schneider_kochanek_wambsganss_2006}, Part~2, eq.~77).
Two cases anchor this result.  For $m=0$,
$\kappab_0 = a_0/(2\theta)$ is the SIS profile
$\kappab = \thetaE/(2\theta)$ with $a_0 = \thetaE$.  For $m=2$,
$\kappab_2 = -(3a_2/2\theta)\cos[2(\varphi-\varphi_2)]$ is the leading
\emph{quadrupole}, the elliptical distortion of an isothermal galaxy.

\begin{derivation}[The ``direct computation'' of eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_multipole}]
For $\psiL_m = a\,\theta\cos m(\varphi-\varphi_m)$ the three terms of the
polar Laplacian are
$\partial_\theta^2\psiL_m = 0$,
$\theta^{-1}\partial_\theta\psiL_m = a\cos m(\varphi-\varphi_m)/\theta$, and
$\theta^{-2}\partial_\varphi^2\psiL_m = -a\,m^2\cos m(\varphi-\varphi_m)/\theta$.
Their sum is $a(1-m^2)\cos m(\varphi-\varphi_m)/\theta = 2\kappab_m$.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[The isothermal dipole is massless; signs of the quadrupole]
For $m = 1$ the factor $(1-m^2)$ vanishes: $\kappab_1 = 0$.  Indeed
$\psiL_1 = a_1\theta\cos(\varphi-\varphi_1) = a_1\,\hat{n}\cdot\vectheta$ with
$\hat n = (\cos\varphi_1,\sin\varphi_1)$.  Its deflection is the constant
vector $a_1\hat{n}$, a rigid shift of the source plane.  That is why the
dipole is ``nearly degenerate with the source position'': in pure isothermal
form it is \emph{exactly} degenerate, and a physical lopsidedness needs a
radial profile other than $\propto\theta$.

The sign needs care for $m \ge 2$: $\kappab_m$ has the opposite sign to $\psiL_m$.  A
potential quadrupole $\propto +\cos 2\varphi$ means a mass \emph{deficit} along
$\varphi = 0$ and an excess along $\varphi = \pi/2$.  Consistently, the SIE of
\S\ref{sec:sie}, with mass elongated along $\theta_1$, has a \emph{negative}
$\cos 2\varphi$ coefficient in its potential.  For $q = 0.7$ the coefficient of
$\psiL(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$ is $-0.059\,\thetaE$.
\end{annotation}


\subsection{Internal vs.\ External Multipoles}
\label{sec:int_ext_multipoles}

Each angular harmonic $m$ admits two radial ``flavours'' that are
solutions of Laplace's equation away from the mass: an \emph{internal}
part sourced by mass \emph{interior} to $\theta$ (decaying outward) and
an \emph{external} part sourced by mass \emph{exterior} to $\theta$
(growing outward).  The quadrupole makes this concrete.  The external
shear of \S\ref{sec:sis_shear} is the massless external quadrupole,
$\psiL = \tfrac12\gammaL\,\theta^2
\cos[2(\varphi-\varphi_\gamma)]$, with $\kappab = 0$ but nonzero
$\gammaL$: a pure tidal field from the environment.  The isothermal
quadrupole $\psiL_2 \propto \theta$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_multipole}
is the internal quadrupole carried by the lens's own elliptical mass.
For a singular isothermal ellipsoid the internal and external quadrupole
amplitudes are locked in the ratio
$\psiL_{2,\mathrm{int}}/\psiL_{2,\mathrm{ext}} = (m-1)/(m+1) = 1/3$, so
the internal fraction of the total quadrupole is
$f_{\mathrm{int}} = 1/4$: \emph{three-quarters of an SIE's quadrupole is
generated by mass outside any given radius}
\citep[][Part~2, eq.~74]{schneider_kochanek_wambsganss_2006}.  For lenses
embedded in flat-rotation-curve dark haloes, most of the quadrupole
therefore comes from the halo beyond the Einstein ring, which is
why an \emph{external} shear term is an adequate stand-in for
the environment in most models.

\subsection{Which Multipoles Matter}
\label{sec:which_multipoles}

The amplitude of successive poles falls steeply with ellipticity,
$\psiL_m \propto \epsilon^{m/2}$ (with $q = 1-\epsilon$).  For a
typical lens with $\thetaE \sim 1''$ the quadrupole ($m=2$) produces
angular deflections $\sim 0.46''\,\epsilon$, which matter for any
ellipticity given milliarcsecond astrometry; the $m=4$ pole
(boxy/discy isophotes) contributes $\sim 0.09''\,\epsilon^2$ and
matters only for $q \lesssim 0.75$; and the dipole ($m=1$,
lopsidedness) is nearly degenerate with the source position and
rarely matters.  The practical conclusion, a recurring theme of the
applications in Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}, is that a monopole
plus an external shear (a single quadrupole) accounts for most of the
constrainable angular structure, with the $m=4$ term becoming
relevant only for markedly flattened lenses.

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:multipoles}: angular structure versus a central mass]
Chapter~\ref{ch:multipoles} asks whether angular structure in the main
deflector ($m = 1$ lopsidedness, $m = 3$, the $m = 4$ boxy/discy term) can be
confused with, or hide, a compact central mass in a compound lens.  Both kinds
of perturbation alter the deflection field near the Einstein ring where the
images form.  It builds on this section: the convergence of an isothermal
multipole, eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_multipole}; the fact that an isothermal $m = 1$
term is a pure source shift (previous box); and the internal/external split,
which decides whether a multipole fitted at the ring must also be present at
the radius of an inner source.
\end{bridge}

\section{Critical Curves}
\label{sec:critical_curves}

We now develop the general theory of critical curves, building on
the preview in Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}
(Section~\ref{sec:critical_curves_preview}).

\begin{definition}
The \emph{critical curves} are the loci in the image plane where
the Jacobian determinant vanishes:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \det\Amat(\vectheta) = 0
    \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad
    (1 - \kappab)^2 - |\gammaL|^2 = 0
    }
    \label{eq:critical_curve_def}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\mu(\vectheta) \to \infty$ on critical curves.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Two Families of Critical Curves}

Using the eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm = 1 - \kappab \pm |\gammaL|$ of
the Jacobian (Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}, eq.~\ref{eq:eigenvalues}),
the condition $\det\Amat = \lambda_+\,\lambda_- = 0$ is satisfied
when either eigenvalue vanishes, and there are accordingly two
families.  The \emph{tangential critical curve} is where
$\lambda_- = 0$,
\begin{equation}
    1 - \kappab(\vectheta) = |\gammaL(\vectheta)|
    \label{eq:tangential_crit}
\end{equation}
Images near this curve are stretched tangentially (along the curve),
because the eigenvalue corresponding to the tangential direction
vanishes; giant arcs in clusters trace the tangential critical
curve.  The \emph{radial critical curve} is where $\lambda_+ = 0$,
\begin{equation}
    1 - \kappab(\vectheta) = -|\gammaL(\vectheta)|
    \label{eq:radial_crit}
\end{equation}
Images near this curve are stretched radially (perpendicular to the
curve).  Radial arcs, pointing toward the lens center, form near
radial critical curves, though they are rarer and fainter than
tangential arcs.

\begin{annotation}[Which eigenvalue is ``tangential''?]
Always $\lambda_- = 1-\kappab-|\gammaL| \le \lambda_+$.  For an axisymmetric
lens $|\gammaL| = \bar\kappab - \kappab$, where $\bar\kappab = \alpha/\theta$
is the mean convergence inside $\theta$.  Then
\[
  \lambda_- = \lambda_t = 1 - \frac{\alpha}{\theta} = 1 - \bar\kappab,
  \qquad
  \lambda_+ = \lambda_r = 1 - \frac{d\alpha}{d\theta} = 1 - 2\kappab + \bar\kappab .
\]
$\lambda_t = 0$ is the Einstein-ring condition $\bar\kappab = 1$, and
$\lambda_r = 0$ requires $d\alpha/d\theta = 1$.  The latter is impossible for
the SIS ($\alpha$ constant) and for any isothermal lens, whose radial
eigenvalue is identically 1 (box in \S\ref{sec:sie_deflection}).  A common
error is to take ``radial critical curve'' to mean $\kappab = 1$; the condition
involves both $\kappab$ and $\bar\kappab$.  For non-axisymmetric lenses the
eigenvectors are only approximately radial and tangential; the names describe
the direction in which images are stretched.
\end{annotation}


\subsection{Critical Curves for Axisymmetric vs.\ Elliptical Lenses}

For axisymmetric lenses the critical curves are circles centered on
the lens: for the SIS there is only a tangential critical curve at
$\theta = \thetaE$, and for the point mass the critical curve is the
Einstein ring.  For elliptical lenses the critical curves become
ellipses or ovals.  For the SIE (mass elongated along $\theta_1$,
$q < 1$) the tangential critical curve is elongated \emph{along} the
mass major axis, crossing $\theta_1$ at $\pm\thetaE/\sqrt{q}$ and
$\theta_2$ at $\pm\thetaE\sqrt{q}$.  In general the shapes are
computed numerically by solving $\det\Amat = 0$ on a grid or
parametrically.

\subsection{Physical Meaning}

Critical curves are the sites of maximum magnification.  Images that
form near a critical curve are highly magnified, $|\mu| \gg 1$,
because $\det\Amat \approx 0$.  They are also highly stretched: along
one direction (tangential or radial) the magnification diverges while
it remains finite in the orthogonal direction, which produces the
elongated arc morphology.  Finally, critical curves are parity
boundaries: as we cross one, $\det\Amat$ changes sign, so the image
parity flips, with positive parity (Type~I) on one side and negative
parity (Type~II) on the other.  The practical significance is
considerable, since critical curves are where the most highly
magnified background sources are to be found; cluster lensing surveys
map out critical curves to find magnified galaxies at very high
redshift.

\section{Caustics}
\label{sec:caustics}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{caustics} are the images of the critical curves under the
lens mapping.  That is, the caustics are the curves in the
source plane obtained by applying the lens equation to each
point on a critical curve:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \vecbeta_{\mathrm{caustic}}
    = \vectheta_{\mathrm{crit}}
    - \vecalpha(\vectheta_{\mathrm{crit}})
    }
    \label{eq:caustic_def_mod8}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Types of Caustics}

Corresponding to the two families of critical curves there are two
types of caustics.  The \emph{tangential caustic} is the mapping of
the tangential critical curve; for elliptical lenses it is typically
a diamond (astroid) with four cusps, and sources inside it are
quadruply imaged.  The \emph{radial caustic} is the mapping of the
radial critical curve; it is typically a smooth, roughly elliptical
curve that encloses the tangential caustic.  Sources inside the
radial caustic but outside the tangential caustic are doubly imaged,
and sources outside both caustics have a single image.

\subsection{Caustics for Specific Models}

For the point mass, the tangential critical curve (the Einstein ring
$\theta = \thetaE$) maps to a single point ($\beta = 0$), a
degenerate point caustic; a source at $\beta = 0$ produces the
Einstein ring, and any source at $\beta > 0$ produces exactly two
images.  The SIS behaves in the same way: the critical curve
$\theta = \thetaE$ maps to $\beta = 0$, and there is no radial
caustic, so sources with $\beta < \thetaE$ produce two images and
sources with $\beta > \thetaE$ produce one image.  For the SIS +
shear the tangential caustic opens up into a diamond (astroid) with
four cusps whose size is proportional to $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$,
and the SIE has the same diamond structure, with the size controlled
by the ellipticity $(1 - q)$.  Non-singular models (NIS, NFW) have
both tangential and radial caustics; the radial caustic encloses the
tangential one, creating regions with 1, 3, and 5 images.

\section{Image Topology}
\label{sec:image_topology}

This section explains why images appear and disappear as a source
moves, why they always come in pairs, and how the full image
configuration (positions, parities, magnifications) is determined
by the location of the source relative to the caustic structure.
Caustics are the boundaries between regions of different image
multiplicity, and critical curves are the boundaries between regions
of different image parity; together they provide a complete
topological description of the lens mapping.


\subsection{Critical Curves as Parity Boundaries}
\label{sec:parity_boundaries}

Recall from Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification} that the magnification is
$\mu = 1/\det\Amat$ and that the sign of $\mu$ determines the image
parity: $\det\Amat > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mu > 0$ gives positive
parity (a Type~I image, with the same orientation as the source),
and $\det\Amat < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mu < 0$ gives negative parity (a
Type~II image, mirror-reflected relative to the source).  Since
$\det\Amat$ is a continuous function of position that passes through
zero on critical curves, the sign of $\det\Amat$ must change across
a critical curve.  Therefore:

\begin{proposition}
\textbf{Critical curves are parity boundaries.}  Images on opposite
sides of a critical curve have opposite parities.  If a critical
curve separates region $R_+$ ($\det\Amat > 0$) from region $R_-$
($\det\Amat < 0$), then images in $R_+$ have positive parity
(Type~I, minima of the arrival time surface) and images in $R_-$
have negative parity (Type~II, saddle points of the arrival time
surface).
\end{proposition}

For a typical galaxy lens with an elliptical mass distribution, the
region outside the tangential critical curve has $\det\Amat > 0$, so
images there are Type~I (positive parity, time-delay minima); the
region between the tangential and radial critical curves has
$\det\Amat < 0$, so images there are Type~II (negative parity,
saddle points); and the region inside the radial critical curve has
$\det\Amat > 0$ again, so images there are Type~I or Type~III
(positive parity, but corresponding to time-delay maxima for
Type~III, as discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:fermat_time_delays}).

\begin{remark}
The connection to Morse theory (Chapter~\ref{ch:fermat_time_delays})
is direct.  The three image types correspond to the three types of
stationary points of the Fermat potential: Type~I is a minimum,
Type~II a saddle, and Type~III a maximum.  The magnification parity
determines the Morse index.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Caustic Crossings and Image Creation/Destruction}
\label{sec:caustic_crossings}

The most important property of caustics is the following.

\begin{proposition}
\textbf{Image creation/destruction at caustics.}  When a source
crosses a caustic, the number of images changes by exactly two.  A
pair of images either appears (as the source enters a
higher-multiplicity region) or disappears (as it exits).
\end{proposition}

This happens because, as a source approaches a caustic from the side
with fewer images, two of the ``potential'' image positions on
opposite sides of the corresponding critical curve converge toward
the critical curve.  At the moment the source is exactly on the
caustic, the two images merge \textit{on} the critical curve (where
$\det\Amat = 0$ and the magnification diverges).  As the source
crosses the caustic, the two images separate and become distinct.

The two images that appear or disappear are located on opposite
sides of the critical curve, have opposite parities (one Type~I, one
Type~II), are both highly magnified near the caustic (their
magnifications diverge as they approach the critical curve), and
have nearly equal magnifications in absolute value.

\begin{remark}
The fact that images always appear or disappear in pairs
with opposite parities is a topological necessity.  It preserves
Burke's odd-number theorem
(Chapter~\ref{ch:fermat_time_delays}): the total number of images is
always odd (for a non-singular, transparent lens with a source
inside the outermost caustic).  Since images are created and
destroyed in pairs, the parity of the count (odd or even) never
changes.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Walking a Source Across Caustics: A Worked Example}
\label{sec:source_walk}

To make the image creation/destruction process concrete, we walk
through a complete example using an SIE lens with axis ratio
$q = 0.7$ and Einstein radius $\thetaE = 1''$.  The SIE has a
tangential caustic (diamond shape) in the source plane.  For a
non-singular model there would also be a radial caustic enclosing
the diamond, but for the SIE (which is singular at the center) we
focus on the tangential caustic.

Consider a source that starts far from the lens and moves inward,
crossing the caustics:

\paragraph{Stage 1: Source outside the tangential caustic.}
For the singular SIE the outermost boundary in the source plane is
not a radial caustic but the \emph{cut} (pseudo-caustic): the closed
curve onto which an infinitesimal circle about the singular centre
is mapped.  If the source is beyond the cut (for $q = 0.7$,
$|\beta_1| > 0.932\,\thetaE$ on the $\beta_1$ axis and
$|\beta_2| > 1.049\,\thetaE$ on the $\beta_2$ axis) the SIE forms a
single Type~I image, the elliptical version of the SIS rule
that $\beta > \thetaE$ gives one image.  Once the source is inside
the cut but still outside the diamond, there are two images
(one Type~I outside the critical curve, near the true source
position, and one Type~II inside the critical curve on the opposite
side of the lens).  In a non-singular model a third, Type~III image
appears near the lens center once the source is inside the radial
caustic, but for the SIE that central image is suppressed because
$\Sigma$ is singular at the origin.  The two observable images are a
Type~I image (positive parity, a minimum of the arrival time) outside
the tangential critical curve near the true source position, weakly
magnified ($|\mu| \approx 1$), and a Type~II image (negative parity,
a saddle of the arrival time) inside the critical curve on the far
side of the lens, also weakly magnified.

\begin{annotation}[How far is ``far''?]
In a cored model the radial caustic replaces the cut: one image outside it,
three inside (the table at the end of this subsection).
\end{annotation}


\paragraph{Stage 2: Source crosses the tangential caustic (outside
$\to$ inside).}
As the source reaches the tangential (diamond) caustic, two new
images appear simultaneously, straddling the tangential critical
curve.  The total count goes from 2 to 4 images.  The original
Type~I image (positive parity, outside the tangential critical curve)
persists, moving slightly.  A new Type~II image (negative parity)
appears just inside the tangential critical curve; it is a saddle
point of the arrival time surface, highly magnified and tangentially
stretched.  A new Type~I image (positive parity) appears just outside
the tangential critical curve, close to the Type~II image; it is a
minimum of the arrival time surface and is also highly magnified.

The two new images are born as a highly magnified, closely spaced
pair straddling the critical curve.  They have opposite parities
(one positive, one negative) and nearly equal $|\mu|$.  As the
source moves further inside the caustic, they separate and their
magnifications decrease.

\paragraph{Stage 3: Source inside the tangential caustic (quad
configuration).}
With the source well inside the diamond caustic, four bright images
surround the lens (plus a faint, highly demagnified central image in
non-singular models).  The typical configuration is an Einstein
cross: two Type~I images (positive parity) outside the tangential
critical curve and two Type~II images (negative parity) inside it.
The four images form a roughly cross-shaped pattern around the lens.
The magnification depends on the source's proximity to the caustic
folds and cusps.

\paragraph{Stage 4: Source crosses the tangential caustic again
(inside $\to$ outside).}
As the source exits the diamond caustic, two images converge on the
critical curve, merge, and disappear.  The count drops from
4 back to 2.  The
two images that disappear are a Type~I/Type~II pair, just as in the
creation event.

For a non-singular lens model (e.g., a softened isothermal
ellipsoid), there is also a radial caustic enclosing the
tangential one.  As the source crosses the radial caustic inward,
two additional images appear (a Type~II near the radial critical
curve and the Type~III central image), bringing the total from 1
to 3; crossing the tangential caustic then adds two more, giving 5.
This gives the complete sequence:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Region} & \textbf{Image count} & \textbf{Types} \\
\midrule
Outside radial caustic & 1 & I \\
Between radial \& tangential & 3 & I, II, III \\
Inside tangential caustic & 5 & I, I, II, II, III \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here Type~III is a maximum of the arrival time (the highly
demagnified central image).

\begin{bridge}[Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images}: the third image]
The bookkeeping of this subsection is all one needs to count images in a
compound lens.  A caustic crossing changes the number by two; parity flips
across every critical curve; $N_+ - N_- = 1$ for a smooth, transparent lens.
The singular SIE parks the Type~III image on the singularity with zero flux.
A finite core brings it back, demagnified, inside the radial critical curve.
Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images} asks what a central point mass (a supermassive
black hole) does to that central image and to the radial caustic.  The odd
image probes the innermost mass profile.
\end{bridge}



\subsection{The Fold and Cusp Catastrophes}
\label{sec:fold_cusp}

The caustic curves of a generic smooth lens consist of two types of
points: \emph{folds} (smooth segments) and \emph{cusps} (pointed
corners).  These are the only two generic singularities of smooth
two-dimensional mappings, a result from catastrophe theory
(or singularity theory; see Petters, Levine \& Wambsganss 2001 for
a thorough treatment).

\subsubsection{Fold Caustics}

Near a fold (a smooth segment of the caustic) two images merge: as a
source crosses a fold caustic, two images appear or disappear,
straddling the critical curve with one on each side.  The
magnification of each of the two merging images scales as
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    |\mu| \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\perp}}}
    }
    \label{eq:fold_magnification}
\end{equation}
where $d_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance of the source from the
caustic.  The magnification diverges as $d_\perp \to 0$ (source on
the caustic), but only as an inverse square root, a relatively mild
divergence.  The two merging images have magnifications that
approach $+|\mu_0|$ and $-|\mu_0|$ (equal absolute values, opposite
signs), so their total signed magnification approaches zero, while
the unsigned total $2|\mu_0| \propto 1/\sqrt{d_\perp}$ diverges.
The merging images are highly elongated tangentially (along the
critical curve) and compressed radially, which is the origin of the
``arc'' morphology.

\begin{derivation}[Why $|\mu| \propto d_\perp^{-1/2}$ at a fold]
Centre local coordinates on a critical point, with axes along the
eigenvectors of $\Amat$ there.  The eigenvalues are $c \ne 0$ (along
$\theta_\parallel$) and $0$ (along $\theta_\perp$).  Expand the lens map.
Along $\theta_\parallel$ the linear term survives: $\beta_\parallel \simeq
c\,\theta_\parallel$.  Along $\theta_\perp$ the linear term vanishes because
$\det\Amat = 0$, so the leading term is quadratic: $\beta_\perp \simeq
a\,\theta_\perp^2$ (to leading order, after a smooth change of local
coordinates; this is the fold normal form).
Then:
\begin{align*}
  \theta_\perp &= \pm\sqrt{\beta_\perp/a}
  && \text{(real only on the side } \beta_\perp/a > 0\text{)},\\
  \Amat &= \mathrm{diag}(c,\ 2a\theta_\perp),\quad
  \det\Amat = 2ac\,\theta_\perp
  && \text{(opposite signs: opposite parities)},\\
  \mu_\pm &= \pm\frac{1}{2c\sqrt{a\beta_\perp}}
  && \Rightarrow\ |\mu| \propto \beta_\perp^{-1/2} = d_\perp^{-1/2},
  \quad \mu_+ + \mu_- = 0 .
\end{align*}
Exercise~\ref{ex:fold_magnification} is the one-dimensional version of the
same calculation.
\end{derivation}


\subsubsection{Cusp Caustics}

Near a cusp (a pointed corner of the caustic) the behavior is
richer.  Three images merge: as a source approaches a cusp from
inside the caustic, three images converge toward a single point on
the critical curve, forming the characteristic \emph{cusp triplet}.
The magnification divergence is stronger than at a fold, since the
sum of the (unsigned) image magnifications diverges as
\begin{equation}
    |\mu_A| + |\mu_B| + |\mu_C| \propto d_{\perp}^{-1}
    \label{eq:cusp_magnification}
\end{equation}
(compared to $d_\perp^{-1/2}$ for a fold).  The \emph{signed}
magnifications of the three cusp images satisfy a different relation,
\begin{equation}
    \mu_A + \mu_B + \mu_C \;\to\; 0 \quad
    \text{(in the limit $d_\perp \to 0$)} \,,
    \label{eq:cusp_relation}
\end{equation}
where $A$, $B$, $C$ are the three images of the cusp triplet (with
signed magnifications).  That is, the sum of the signed
magnifications of the three brightest images in a cusp configuration
should vanish; violations of this relation indicate substructure
(e.g., dark matter subhalos) perturbing the images.  The three cusp
images are closely spaced along the critical curve, and one is on the
opposite side of the critical curve from the other two, ensuring
opposite parities.

\begin{derivation}[The cusp relation from the cusp normal form]
Near a cusp the lens map can be written, in local coordinates with constants
$a, b, c$, as
\[
  \beta_1 = c\,\theta_1 + \tfrac{b}{2}\theta_2^2, \qquad
  \beta_2 = b\,\theta_1\theta_2 + a\,\theta_2^3 .
\]
Its Jacobian $\begin{pmatrix} c & b\theta_2\\ b\theta_2 & b\theta_1 + 3a\theta_2^2\end{pmatrix}$
is symmetric, so this is a legitimate (gradient) lens map.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Eliminate $\theta_1 = (\beta_1 - b\theta_2^2/2)/c$.  Then $\theta_2$
        solves the cubic
        $g(t) = \big(a - \tfrac{b^2}{2c}\big)t^3 + \tfrac{b\beta_1}{c}t - \beta_2 = 0$.
  \item Substituting the same $\theta_1$ into the determinant gives
        $\det\Amat = c\,g'(t)$, so each image has $\mu_i = 1/[c\,g'(t_i)]$.
  \item For any cubic with distinct roots, $\sum_i 1/g'(t_i) = 0$.  Write
        $1/g(t) = \sum_i [g'(t_i)(t-t_i)]^{-1}$ (partial fractions); the left
        side falls as $t^{-3}$ at large $t$, while the right side falls as
        $t^{-1}\sum_i 1/g'(t_i)$, so the sum must vanish.
\end{enumerate}
Hence $\mu_A + \mu_B + \mu_C = 0$ exactly within the normal form, with
corrections of higher order in the distance from the cusp.  On the cusp axis
(take $a = b = c = 1$, $\beta_2 = 0$, $\beta_1 < 0$) the three magnifications
are $1/\beta_1$, $-1/(2\beta_1)$ and $-1/(2\beta_1)$.  They sum to zero, while
$\sum|\mu| = 2/|\beta_1| \propto d_\perp^{-1}$, compared with $d_\perp^{-1/2}$
at a fold.
\end{derivation}


\begin{remark}
The fold and cusp are the only stable (generic)
singularities of smooth two-dimensional maps.  Higher-order
singularities (swallowtails, lips, etc.)\ require special symmetries
or fine-tuning and are not generically present in gravitational lens
models.  This follows from Whitney's theorem in singularity
theory (Petters et al.\ 2001, Ch.~7, Sec.~7.5).
\end{remark}

\begin{annotation}[Fold or cusp? A practical test]
At a critical point let $\vec{e}$ be the eigenvector of $\Amat$ with zero
eigenvalue.  The caustic tangent is $d\vecbeta/ds = \Amat\,d\vectheta/ds$.  If
$\vec{e}$ is transverse to the critical curve, $d\vecbeta/ds \neq 0$: a fold.
If $\vec{e}$ is tangent to the critical curve, $d\vecbeta/ds = 0$: the caustic
stops and reverses, a cusp.  Both models of this chapter put their cusps on
the symmetry axes.  For the SIS$+$shear, $d\vecbeta/d\phi = 0$ only at
$\sin\phi\cos\phi = 0$; for the SIE, the tangential eigenvector is parallel to
the critical ellipse only on the axes (derivation boxes in \S\ref{sec:sis_shear}
and \S\ref{sec:sie}).
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Image Configuration Taxonomy}
\label{sec:image_taxonomy}

The caustic structure of a lens determines all possible image
configurations.  For an elliptical galaxy lens (SIE or similar model),
the following taxonomy applies:

\subsubsection{Doubles}

When the source is outside the tangential caustic, two images form
(or effectively one bright image plus one highly demagnified image
near the lens center for singular models): one Type~I image
(positive parity, outside the critical curve), the dominant, brighter
image, and one Type~II image (negative parity, inside the critical
curve), typically fainter and on the opposite side of the lens from
the source.  Doubles account for $\sim$70--80\% of known
galaxy-scale lens systems.

\subsubsection{Quads / Einstein Crosses}

When the source is inside the tangential caustic, four bright images
form (plus a faint central image): two Type~I images (positive
parity), roughly on the side of the lens closer to the source, and
two Type~II images (negative parity) on the opposite side.  The four
images form a roughly cross-shaped (Einstein cross) or diamond-shaped
pattern around the lens.  Quads account for $\sim$20--30\% of
galaxy-scale systems and are especially valuable for cosmography
because they provide three independent time delays.

\subsubsection{Cusp Configurations}

When the source is near a cusp of the tangential caustic, three of
the four images in a quad merge into a closely spaced triplet near
one point on the critical curve.  This produces the \emph{naked cusp}
or \emph{cusp triplet} configuration: three closely spaced images
near the cusp of the critical curve, with the cusp relation
$\mu_A + \mu_B + \mu_C \approx 0$, and one isolated image on the
opposite side of the lens.

\subsubsection{Why Most Observed Quads Have a Diamond Caustic}

The tangential caustic of an elliptical galaxy lens is generically a
diamond (astroid).  The dominant perturbation from circular symmetry
is quadrupolar (ellipticity or shear), which produces exactly four
cusps, and higher-order multipoles (hexapole, octupole) are typically
subdominant and do not change the topology of the caustic.  The
diamond caustic produces Einstein crosses (quads with roughly equal
magnifications) when the source is near the center, and fold or cusp
configurations when the source is near a fold or cusp of the
diamond.


\subsection{Why Giant Arcs Form}
\label{sec:giant_arcs_preview}

The formation of giant luminous arcs in galaxy clusters follows
from the theory of critical curves and caustics.

\paragraph{Step 1: Sources near the tangential caustic.}
Giant arcs form when a background galaxy lies very close to (but
just inside or outside) the tangential caustic.  At this location,
one or two of its images lie very close to the tangential critical
curve.

\paragraph{Step 2: Tangential stretching near the critical curve.}
The tangential critical curve is where $\lambda_- = 0$ (the
eigenvalue corresponding to the tangential direction vanishes).
Near this curve, images are stretched strongly in the
tangential direction (along the critical curve) while
remaining relatively unstretched in the radial direction.

Quantitatively, if the source is at perpendicular distance
$d_\perp$ from the tangential caustic, the tangential magnification
scales as:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\mathrm{tan}} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_\perp}}
    \label{eq:tangential_magnification}
\end{equation}
while the radial magnification remains of order unity.  The result
is an image that is stretched by a factor of $\mu_{\mathrm{tan}}$
along the critical curve but not radially: an arc.

\paragraph{Step 3: Arc length and curvature.}
Since the arc follows the tangential critical curve, its curvature
matches that of the critical curve.  For a galaxy cluster, the
tangential critical curve has a radius of $\sim$10--30 arcsec, so
giant arcs appear as curved features with large opening angles.
The length-to-width ratio of the arc is approximately
$\mu_{\mathrm{tan}} \sim 1/\sqrt{d_\perp}$, which can reach
10:1 or more for sources very close to the caustic.

\paragraph{Step 4: Multiple arcs.}
A source just inside the tangential caustic produces two highly
magnified images on opposite sides of the tangential critical curve
(a fold pair).  Both images are arcs.  If the source is also near a
cusp, three images merge into a very long arc (a ``cusp arc'').

\begin{remark}
The largest known arcs in galaxy clusters have length-to-width ratios
exceeding 10:1 and total magnifications of $\mu \sim 20$--50.  These
extreme magnifications are used to study intrinsically faint galaxies
at high redshift (``cosmic telescopes'').
\end{remark}

\section{Image Configurations: Doubles, Quads, and Einstein Crosses}
\label{sec:image_configurations}

We now illustrate the different image configurations with explicit
examples for an SIE lens.

\subsection{Double Configuration}

For a source at $\vecbeta = (0.5, 0.0)''$ lensed by an SIE with
$\thetaE = 1''$, $q = 0.7$, the source is outside the tangential
caustic and two images form: image A at
$\vectheta_A \approx (1.43, 0.0)''$, Type~I with positive parity and
$\mu_A \approx 6.1$, and image B at
$\vectheta_B \approx (-0.43, 0.0)''$, Type~II with negative parity
and $\mu_B \approx -0.6$.  The brighter image (A) is on the same side as the source; the
fainter image (B) is on the opposite side.

\begin{annotation}[Checking the double by hand]
On the $\theta_1$ axis, $F = q|\theta_1|$ and
$\alpha_1 = \mathrm{sgn}(\theta_1)\,(\thetaE\sqrt{q}/k)\arctan(k/q) =
\pm 0.932\,\thetaE$ for $q = 0.7$.  The deflection is constant along the axis,
as for the SIS.  So $\theta_A = 0.5 + 0.932 = 1.432''$ and
$\theta_B = 0.5 - 0.932 = -0.432''$.  With $\mu = 1/(1-2\kappab)$ and
$2\kappab = 1/(\sqrt{q}\,|\theta_1|)$ on this axis, $\mu_A = +6.05$ and
$\mu_B = -0.566$, matching the values above.  A common error is to use the
SIS formula $\mu = \theta/(\theta-\thetaE)$ with $\thetaE$.  On this axis the SIE
behaves like an SIS with Einstein radius $\thetaE/\sqrt{q} = 1.195\,\thetaE$.
\end{annotation}


\subsection{Quad / Einstein Cross Configuration}

For a source at $\vecbeta = (0.05, 0.05)''$ (inside the tangential
caustic):  Four bright images form, arranged in a cross pattern.
The two Type~I images tend to be on the side closer to the source;
the two Type~II images are on the opposite side.  The magnifications
of the four images are comparable, with the images nearer the
critical curve being brighter.

\subsection{Cusp Configuration}

For a source near a cusp of the tangential caustic (e.g., near
$\vecbeta \approx (0.25, 0.0)''$ for an SIE with $q = 0.7$, where the
cusp on the $\beta_1$-axis sits at $|\beta_1| \approx 0.263\,\thetaE$):
Three images cluster together near the corresponding point on the
critical curve, forming a cusp triplet.  The fourth image is
isolated on the opposite side.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:elliptical_summary}

The SIS with external shear is the simplest non-axisymmetric model.
The external shear $\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}$ deforms the critical curve
into an oval and opens the point caustic into a diamond (astroid)
with four cusps.  The singular isothermal ellipsoid (SIE) builds
ellipticity directly into the mass distribution; its deflection angle
involves $\arctan$ and $\mathrm{arctanh}$ functions (Kormann et al.\
1994), and it is the standard model for galaxy-scale lens modeling.
Critical curves ($\det\Amat = 0$) are parity boundaries in the image
plane: the tangential critical curve ($1 - \kappab = |\gammaL|$) is
the site of giant arcs, and the radial critical curve
($1 - \kappab = -|\gammaL|$) is the site of radial arcs.  Caustics
are the mappings of critical curves to the source plane and divide
it into regions of different image multiplicity.

As a source crosses a caustic, two images appear or disappear on
opposite sides of the critical curve, with opposite parities, which
preserves Burke's odd-number theorem.  At a fold two images merge,
with magnification $|\mu| \propto 1/\sqrt{d_\perp}$; this produces
giant arcs.  At a cusp three images merge and the cusp relation
$\mu_A + \mu_B + \mu_C \approx 0$ holds.  Doubles (source outside
the caustic), quads or Einstein crosses (source inside the caustic),
and cusp triplets (source near a cusp) account for the observed
morphologies of strong lens systems.  Giant arcs form when extended
sources lie near the tangential caustic: images are tangentially
stretched by factors of $\mu_{\mathrm{tan}} \propto 1/\sqrt{d_\perp}$
along the tangential critical curve.

\section{Problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:sis_shear_crit}
\textbf{(SIS + shear: critical curves and caustics.)}
Consider an SIS + external shear model with $\thetaE = 1''$ and
$\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}} = 0.1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Starting from the lensing potential
          (eq.~\ref{eq:psi_sis_shear}), compute the Jacobian matrix
          $\Amat$ and the determinant $\det\Amat$ as functions of
          $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$.
    \item Solve $\det\Amat = 0$ to find the critical curve.  Express
          the result in polar coordinates $\theta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\phi)$.
          Plot the critical curve.
    \item Map the critical curve through the lens equation to obtain
          the caustic.  Plot the caustic in the source plane.  Verify
          that it has four cusps and the shape of an astroid.
    \item Estimate the area enclosed by the caustic (the quad
          cross-section).
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\solnlink{08_Elliptical_Models_Caustics/problems\_08.wl}{problems\_08.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}

\textbf{(SIE tangential caustic.)}
For an SIE with $\thetaE = 1''$ and axis ratio $q = 0.7$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Write down the deflection angle components $\alpha_1$ and
          $\alpha_2$ from eq.~\eqref{eq:sie_alpha}.
    \item Compute the tangential caustic numerically by: (i)~finding
          the critical curve from $\det\Amat = 0$, and (ii)~mapping
          it through the lens equation.  Plot the caustic.
    \item Compute the area of the tangential caustic (the quad
          cross-section) numerically.  How does it compare to the

\end{enumerate}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:fold_magnification}
\textbf{(Fold magnification scaling.)}
Show that images appear or disappear in pairs at caustic crossings
by analyzing $\det\Amat$ near a fold:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Near a fold caustic, the lens mapping in the direction
          perpendicular to the fold can be approximated as
          $\beta_\perp \approx a\,\theta_\perp^2$, where
          $\theta_\perp$ is the signed distance from the critical
          curve and $a$ is a constant.  Show that for
          $\beta_\perp > 0$, there are two solutions
          $\theta_\perp = \pm\sqrt{\beta_\perp/a}$ (one on each
          side of the critical curve), while for $\beta_\perp < 0$
          there are no solutions.
    \item Compute the Jacobian of the simplified mapping and show
          that the magnification scales as
          $|\mu| \propto 1/\sqrt{\beta_\perp}$ for each image.
    \item Show that the two images have opposite parities
          (opposite signs of $\det\Amat$).
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\solnlink{08_Elliptical_Models_Caustics/problems\_08.wl}{problems\_08.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:sie_images}
\textbf{(SIE image positions.)}
For a source at $\vecbeta = (0.1, 0.2)''$ lensed by an SIE with
$\thetaE = 1''$ and $q = 0.8$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Determine whether the source is inside or outside the
          tangential caustic.  How many images do you expect?
    \item Find all image positions $\vectheta_i$ numerically by
          solving the lens equation
          $\vecbeta = \vectheta - \vecalpha(\vectheta)$.
    \item For each image, compute the magnification $\mu_i$ and
          determine the parity (Type~I or Type~II).
    \item Compute the signed magnification sum $\sum_i \mu_i$ and
          discuss whether it is consistent with the expected value
          (considering the missing central image for the singular SIE).
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\solnlink{08_Elliptical_Models_Caustics/problems\_08.wl}{problems\_08.wl}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:burke_verification}
\textbf{(Burke's odd-number theorem for the SIE.)}
Verify Burke's odd-number theorem for an SIE with $\thetaE = 1''$
and $q = 0.7$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item For a source outside the tangential caustic (e.g.,
          $\vecbeta = (0.5, 0.0)''$), find all image positions.
          Count the images (including any faint central image) and
          verify the count is odd.
    \item For a source inside the tangential caustic (e.g.,
          $\vecbeta = (0.05, 0.05)''$), find all image positions.
          Verify the count is odd.
    \item For each case, classify each image by parity and verify
          that the number of positive-parity images exceeds the
          number of negative-parity images by one.
    \item Discuss why the SIE (being singular at the origin)
          requires care in applying Burke's theorem.  What role does
          the central image play?
\end{enumerate}
Verify with
\solnlink{08_Elliptical_Models_Caustics/problems\_08.wl}{problems\_08.wl}.
\end{exercise}
