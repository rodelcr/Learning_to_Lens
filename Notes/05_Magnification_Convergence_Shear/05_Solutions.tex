\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:derive_jacobian}: Derivation of the Jacobian Matrix}

(a) The lens equation is $\beta_i = \theta_i - \alpha_i(\vectheta)$
where $\alpha_i = \partial\psiL/\partial\theta_i$.  Taking partial
derivatives:
\begin{equation*}
    \Amat_{ij} = \frac{\partial\beta_i}{\partial\theta_j}
    = \delta_{ij} - \frac{\partial\alpha_i}{\partial\theta_j}
    = \delta_{ij} - \frac{\partial^2\psiL}{\partial\theta_i\,\partial\theta_j} \,.
\end{equation*}

(b) The Hessian matrix $H_{ij} = \partial^2\psiL/\partial\theta_i\,\partial\theta_j$
is a $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix.  Any such matrix can be decomposed
into a trace part and a traceless part:
\begin{equation*}
    H_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{tr}\,H)\,\delta_{ij}
    + \left(H_{ij} - \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{tr}\,H)\,\delta_{ij}\right) .
\end{equation*}
The trace is $\mathrm{tr}\,H = \psiL_{,11} + \psiL_{,22} = \nabla^2\psiL = 2\kappab$,
so the trace part gives $\kappab\,\delta_{ij}$.

The traceless part is:
\begin{equation*}
    H_{ij} - \kappab\,\delta_{ij}
    = \begin{pmatrix}
    \psiL_{,11} - \kappab & \psiL_{,12} \\
    \psiL_{,12} & \psiL_{,22} - \kappab
    \end{pmatrix}
    = \begin{pmatrix}
    \gamma_1 & \gamma_2 \\
    \gamma_2 & -\gamma_1
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
where $\gamma_1 = \frac{1}{2}(\psiL_{,11} - \psiL_{,22})$ and
$\gamma_2 = \psiL_{,12}$.
\begin{derivation}[The diagonal entries of the traceless part]
With $\kappab = \tfrac12(\psiL_{,11} + \psiL_{,22})$:
$\psiL_{,11} - \kappab = \psiL_{,11} - \tfrac12\psiL_{,11} - \tfrac12\psiL_{,22}
= \tfrac12(\psiL_{,11} - \psiL_{,22}) = \gamma_1$, and
$\psiL_{,22} - \kappab = \tfrac12(\psiL_{,22} - \psiL_{,11}) = -\gamma_1$.
The two diagonal entries are equal and opposite, which is what ``traceless''
means. The off-diagonal entries are untouched by subtracting
$\kappab\,\delta_{ij}$, so they remain $\psiL_{,12} = \gamma_2$.
\end{derivation}

(c) Substituting into $\Amat_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - H_{ij}$:
\begin{equation*}
    \Amat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
    - \begin{pmatrix}
    \kappab + \gamma_1 & \gamma_2 \\
    \gamma_2 & \kappab - \gamma_1
    \end{pmatrix}
    = \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \kappab - \gamma_1 & -\gamma_2 \\
    -\gamma_2 & 1 - \kappab + \gamma_1
    \end{pmatrix} . \quad \square
\end{equation*}
\begin{annotation}[Counting degrees of freedom]
A symmetric $2\times2$ matrix has three independent entries. The trace takes
one ($\kappab$) and leaves two for the traceless part ($\gamma_1, \gamma_2$).
This matches the two components of a spin-2 quantity, and the split is unique.
The decomposition is also the reason the lens mapping has three local
degrees of freedom. Without the $\gamma$'s, $\Amat$ would only rescale, and without
$\kappab$ it would mainly distort shapes (area changes only at second order in
$\gamma$, $\mu = 1/(1-|\gammaL|^2)$).
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:poisson_2d}: The 2D Poisson Equation}

(a) In two dimensions, $\ln|\vectheta|$ is harmonic for
$\vectheta \neq 0$.  To find $\nabla^2\ln|\vectheta|$ as a distribution,
integrate over a disk $D_\epsilon$ of radius $\epsilon$ centered at the
origin using Gauss's theorem:
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{D_\epsilon} \nabla^2\ln|\vectheta|\,d^2\theta
    = \oint_{\partial D_\epsilon}
    \nabla\ln|\vectheta| \cdot \hat{n}\, dl
    = \oint_{\partial D_\epsilon}
    \frac{\vectheta}{|\vectheta|^2} \cdot \hat{n}\, dl
    = \oint_{\partial D_\epsilon}
    \frac{1}{\epsilon}\, dl = 2\pi \,.
\end{equation*}
Since $\nabla^2\ln|\vectheta| = 0$ for $\vectheta \neq 0$, the
entire contribution comes from the origin.  Therefore
$\nabla^2\ln|\vectheta| = 2\pi\,\delta^{(2)}(\vectheta)$. \checkmark
\begin{derivation}[The boundary integral in detail]
On the circle $|\vectheta| = \epsilon$ the outward unit normal is
$\hat n = \vectheta/\epsilon$, and $\nabla\ln|\vectheta| = \vectheta/|\vectheta|^2$.
Their dot product is $|\vectheta|^2/(\epsilon\,|\vectheta|^2) = 1/\epsilon$, a constant on the circle. The
circumference is $2\pi\epsilon$, so the flux is $(1/\epsilon)(2\pi\epsilon) = 2\pi$,
independent of $\epsilon$. Since the flux does not depend on $\epsilon$, shrinking the
disk does not change the integral, so all of it must come from the origin.
\emph{Alternative route.} Smooth the logarithm to
$\tfrac12\ln(|\vectheta|^2 + \epsilon^2)$. Its Laplacian,
$2\epsilon^2/(|\vectheta|^2 + \epsilon^2)^2$, is an ordinary function whose
integral over the plane is $2\pi$ for every $\epsilon > 0$; it tends to
$2\pi\delta^{(2)}$ as $\epsilon \to 0$. Both are checked in
\texttt{problems\_05.wl}.
\end{derivation}

(b) Applying $\nabla^2_\theta$ to the definition of $\psiL$:
\begin{align*}
    \nabla^2_\theta\, \psiL(\vectheta)
    &= \frac{1}{\pi} \int \kappab(\vectheta')\,
    \nabla^2_\theta\, \ln|\vectheta - \vectheta'|\, d^2\theta' \\
    &= \frac{1}{\pi} \int \kappab(\vectheta')\,
    2\pi\,\delta^{(2)}(\vectheta - \vectheta')\, d^2\theta' \\
    &= 2\,\kappab(\vectheta) \,. \quad \square
\end{align*}

(c) For the point mass: $\psiL = \thetaE^2\ln|\vectheta|$
and $\kappab = \pi\thetaE^2\,\delta^{(2)}(\vectheta)$.
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla^2\psiL = \thetaE^2\,\nabla^2\ln|\vectheta|
    = \thetaE^2 \cdot 2\pi\,\delta^{(2)}(\vectheta)
    = 2\pi\thetaE^2\,\delta^{(2)}(\vectheta)
    = 2\kappab \,. \quad \checkmark
\end{equation*}
\begin{annotation}[Where the $\pi$ went]
The normalization $\kappab = \pi\thetaE^2\delta^{(2)}$ is exactly what makes
the total convergence $\int\kappab\,d^2\theta = \pi\thetaE^2$ equal to the
area of the Einstein disk. Hence the mean convergence inside
$\thetaE$ is 1, as it must be for any axisymmetric lens.
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:point_mass_shear}: Shear of a Point Mass Lens}

(a) With $\psiL = \frac{\thetaE^2}{2}\ln(\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2)$:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial^2\psiL}{\partial\theta_1^2}
    &= \thetaE^2\,\frac{\theta^2 - 2\theta_1^2}{\theta^4} \,, \qquad
    \frac{\partial^2\psiL}{\partial\theta_2^2}
    = \thetaE^2\,\frac{\theta^2 - 2\theta_2^2}{\theta^4} \,, \\
    \frac{\partial^2\psiL}{\partial\theta_1\,\partial\theta_2}
    &= -\frac{2\thetaE^2\,\theta_1\theta_2}{\theta^4} \,.
\end{align*}
\begin{derivation}[The derivatives, by the quotient rule]
$\partial_1\psiL = \frac{\thetaE^2}{2}\cdot\frac{2\theta_1}{\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2}
= \thetaE^2\theta_1/\theta^2$. Differentiating again with
$\partial_1(\theta^2) = 2\theta_1$:
$\partial_1^2\psiL = \thetaE^2\,\frac{\theta^2 - \theta_1\cdot2\theta_1}{\theta^4}
= \thetaE^2(\theta^2 - 2\theta_1^2)/\theta^4$, and
$\partial_2\partial_1\psiL = \thetaE^2\theta_1\cdot(-2\theta_2)/\theta^4$.
Since $\theta^2 - 2\theta_1^2 = \theta_2^2 - \theta_1^2$, this agrees with the
form in eq.~\eqref{eq:psi_second_derivs_point}.
\end{derivation}
Therefore:
\begin{align*}
    \gamma_1 &= \frac{1}{2}\left(
    \thetaE^2\,\frac{\theta^2 - 2\theta_1^2}{\theta^4}
    - \thetaE^2\,\frac{\theta^2 - 2\theta_2^2}{\theta^4}\right)
    = \frac{\thetaE^2(\theta_2^2 - \theta_1^2)}{\theta^4} \,, \\
    \gamma_2 &= -\frac{2\thetaE^2\,\theta_1\theta_2}{\theta^4} \,.
\end{align*}

(b) Computing the magnitude:
\begin{align*}
    |\gammaL|^2 &= \gamma_1^2 + \gamma_2^2
    = \frac{\thetaE^4}{\theta^8}\left[
    (\theta_2^2 - \theta_1^2)^2 + 4\theta_1^2\theta_2^2\right] \\
    &= \frac{\thetaE^4}{\theta^8}\left[
    \theta_1^4 + \theta_2^4 - 2\theta_1^2\theta_2^2
    + 4\theta_1^2\theta_2^2\right]
    = \frac{\thetaE^4}{\theta^8}(\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2)^2
    = \frac{\thetaE^4}{\theta^4} \,.
\end{align*}
Therefore $|\gammaL| = \thetaE^2/\theta^2$. \checkmark

(c) In polar coordinates, $\theta_1 = \theta\cos\phi$,
$\theta_2 = \theta\sin\phi$:
\begin{align*}
    \gamma_1 &= \frac{\thetaE^2(\sin^2\phi - \cos^2\phi)}{\theta^2}
    = -\frac{\thetaE^2\cos 2\phi}{\theta^2} \,, \\
    \gamma_2 &= -\frac{2\thetaE^2\cos\phi\sin\phi}{\theta^2}
    = -\frac{\thetaE^2\sin 2\phi}{\theta^2} \,.
\end{align*}
The complex shear is:
\begin{equation*}
    \gammaL = \gamma_1 + i\gamma_2
    = -\frac{\thetaE^2}{\theta^2}(\cos 2\phi + i\sin 2\phi)
    = -\frac{\thetaE^2}{\theta^2}\,e^{2i\phi} \,.
\end{equation*}
The factor $e^{2i\phi}$ with the minus sign indicates tangential
shear (aligned perpendicular to the radius vector). \checkmark
\begin{annotation}[Checking ``perpendicular'' directly]
$-e^{2i\phi} = e^{2i(\phi + \pi/2)}$, so the shear angle is
$\phi_\gamma = \phi + 90^\circ$. Equivalently, at the point
$(\theta\cos\phi, \theta\sin\phi)$ the Jacobian has eigenvector
$(-\sin\phi, \cos\phi)$ (tangential) with the smaller eigenvalue
$1 - \thetaE^2/\theta^2$, and $(\cos\phi, \sin\phi)$ (radial) with
$1 + \thetaE^2/\theta^2$. The image is therefore stretched tangentially.
\end{annotation}

(d) Since $\kappab = 0$ everywhere except the origin for the
point mass, the ratio $|\gammaL|/\kappab$ is undefined (infinity) at
any $\theta \neq 0$.  This is characteristic of the point mass: all
image distortion comes from shear alone, with no contribution from
convergence.
\begin{annotation}[Comparing profiles, and the second half of the question]
For any axisymmetric lens $|\gammaL|/\kappab = \bar\kappab/\kappab - 1$. A
power law $\kappab \propto \theta^{1-\gp}$ gives $(\gp - 1)/(3 - \gp)$, i.e.\
$1$ for the SIS ($\gp = 2$), and the point mass is the limit $\kappab \to 0$
where the ratio diverges. \emph{Far from the Einstein radius} (the second
half of part~(d)), the more useful number is $|\gammaL|$ itself at the two
images. In units of $\thetaE$, $|\gammaL(\theta_\pm)| = 1/x_\pm^2$, and since
$x_+x_- = -1$, $|\gammaL(\theta_+)|\,|\gammaL(\theta_-)| = 1$. As $u \to \infty$
the outer image sees vanishing shear (it is nearly undistorted), and the
inner image sees diverging shear. That inner image is strongly distorted and
demagnified.
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:magnification_consistency}: Magnification Consistency Check}

(a) With $\kappab = 0$ and $|\gammaL| = \thetaE^2/\theta^2$:
\begin{equation*}
    \mu = \frac{1}{(1 - 0)^2 - (\thetaE^2/\theta^2)^2}
    = \frac{1}{1 - \thetaE^4/\theta^4}
    = \frac{\theta^4}{\theta^4 - \thetaE^4} \,. \quad \checkmark
\end{equation*}

(b) At $\theta_\pm = \frac{1}{2}(\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\thetaE^2})$,
with $u = \beta/\thetaE$ and working in units of $\thetaE$:
$\theta_\pm/\thetaE = \frac{1}{2}(u \pm \sqrt{u^2 + 4})$.
After substitution and simplification (verified in \texttt{problems\_05.wl}):
\begin{equation*}
    \mu_+ = \frac{u^2 + 2}{2u\sqrt{u^2+4}} + \frac{1}{2} \,, \qquad
    \mu_- = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{u^2 + 2}{2u\sqrt{u^2+4}} \,,
\end{equation*}
i.e.\ $|\mu_\pm| = (u^2+2)/(2u\sqrt{u^2+4}) \pm 1/2$, the form quoted in
eq.~\eqref{eq:magnification_u} (which, like Narayan \& Bartelmann
eq.~27, gives the absolute value for the lower sign). \checkmark
\begin{derivation}[The ``substitution and simplification'', spelled out]
Let $x_\pm = \theta_\pm/\thetaE$, so that $\mu_\pm = x_\pm^4/(x_\pm^4 - 1)$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $x_\pm$ are the roots of $x^2 - ux - 1 = 0$, so
          $x_+ + x_- = u$, $x_+x_- = -1$, $x_+ - x_- = \sqrt{u^2+4}$.
    \item $x_-^4 = (x_+x_-)^4/x_+^4 = 1/x_+^4$, hence
          $\mu_- = 1/(1 - x_+^4)$ and $\mu_+ + \mu_- = 1$.
    \item $\mu_+ - \mu_- = (x_+^4 + 1)/(x_+^4 - 1)$; dividing top and
          bottom by $x_+^2$ and using $x_+^{-2} = x_-^2$ gives
          $(x_+^2 + x_-^2)/(x_+^2 - x_-^2)$.
    \item $x_+^2 + x_-^2 = (x_+ + x_-)^2 - 2x_+x_- = u^2 + 2$ and
          $x_+^2 - x_-^2 = (x_+ + x_-)(x_+ - x_-) = u\sqrt{u^2+4}$.
    \item $\mu_\pm = \tfrac12[1 \pm (u^2+2)/(u\sqrt{u^2+4})]$, which is the
          displayed result.
\end{enumerate}
\end{derivation}

(c) Write $A(u) \equiv (u^2+2)/(2u\sqrt{u^2+4}) > 0$.  Then
$\mu_+ = A + 1/2 > 0$ at once.  For $\mu_- = 1/2 - A < 0$ we need
$A > 1/2$, i.e.\ $u^2 + 2 > u\sqrt{u^2+4}$.  Both sides are positive,
so we may square: $u^4 + 4u^2 + 4 > u^4 + 4u^2$, i.e.\ $4 > 0$, which
holds for every $u$.  Hence $\mu_- < 0$ for all $u > 0$.

The same conclusion follows directly from
$\mu = \theta^4/(\theta^4 - \thetaE^4)$: for $u > 0$,
$|\theta_-|/\thetaE = (\sqrt{u^2+4} - u)/2 < 1$, so
$\theta_-^4 < \thetaE^4$ and the denominator is negative.  (That
$\theta_- < 0$, i.e.\ the image lies on the far side of the lens, plays
no role here, since $\theta_-$ enters only through $\theta_-^4$.)
The negative sign means the image is mirror-reflected. \checkmark

(d) The total magnification is:
\begin{equation*}
    |\mu_+| + |\mu_-| = \frac{u^2+2}{2u\sqrt{u^2+4}} + \frac{1}{2}
    + \frac{u^2+2}{2u\sqrt{u^2+4}} - \frac{1}{2}
    = \frac{u^2+2}{u\sqrt{u^2+4}} \,.
\end{equation*}
We need $(u^2+2)^2 > u^2(u^2+4)$, i.e.,
$u^4 + 4u^2 + 4 > u^4 + 4u^2$, i.e., $4 > 0$, which holds. \checkmark

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:elliptical_image}: Elliptical Image from Constant $\kappab$ and $\gammaL$}

(a) For constant $\kappab$, $\gamma_1$, $\gamma_2$:
\begin{equation*}
    \Amat = \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \kappab - \gamma_1 & -\gamma_2 \\
    -\gamma_2 & 1 - \kappab + \gamma_1
    \end{pmatrix} .
\end{equation*}

(b) The eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = 1 - \kappab \pm |\gammaL|$
where $|\gammaL| = \sqrt{\gamma_1^2 + \gamma_2^2}$.

The Jacobian maps a circle of radius $\delta$ in the source plane to
an ellipse in the image plane.  Since $\Amat$ maps image $\to$ source,
the inverse $\Amat^{-1}$ maps source $\to$ image.  The
eigenvalues of $\Amat^{-1}$ are $1/\lambda_\pm$.  Therefore a circle
of radius $\delta$ is mapped to an ellipse with semi-axes:
\begin{equation*}
    a = \frac{\delta}{|\lambda_-|}
    = \frac{\delta}{|1 - \kappab - |\gammaL||} \,, \qquad
    b = \frac{\delta}{|\lambda_+|}
    = \frac{\delta}{|1 - \kappab + |\gammaL||} \,.
\end{equation*}
(Here $a > b$ when $|\lambda_-| < |\lambda_+|$.)
\begin{derivation}[Why the semi-axes are $\delta/|\lambda_\pm|$]
$\Amat$ is real and symmetric, so it has orthonormal eigenvectors
$\mathbf{e}_\pm$. Expand an image-plane displacement as
$\delta\vectheta = y_+\mathbf{e}_+ + y_-\mathbf{e}_-$. Then
$\delta\vecbeta = \Amat\,\delta\vectheta = \lambda_+y_+\mathbf{e}_+ + \lambda_-y_-\mathbf{e}_-$.
The source boundary $|\delta\vecbeta| = \delta$ becomes
$\lambda_+^2y_+^2 + \lambda_-^2y_-^2 = \delta^2$, i.e.\
$y_+^2/(\delta/|\lambda_+|)^2 + y_-^2/(\delta/|\lambda_-|)^2 = 1$: an ellipse
with semi-axes $\delta/|\lambda_\pm|$ along $\mathbf{e}_\pm$. The
absolute values appear because only $\lambda^2$ enters. The signs of the
$\lambda$'s decide the parity, not the shape.
\end{derivation}

(c) The axis ratio is:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{b}{a} = \frac{|\lambda_-|}{|\lambda_+|}
    = \frac{|1 - \kappab - |\gammaL||}{|1 - \kappab + |\gammaL||} \,.
\end{equation*}
For $\kappab < 1$ and $|\gammaL| < 1 - \kappab$ (subcritical):
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{b}{a} = \frac{1 - \kappab - |\gammaL|}{1 - \kappab + |\gammaL|} \,.
\end{equation*}
The magnification is:
\begin{equation*}
    \mu = \frac{1}{\lambda_+\,\lambda_-}
    = \frac{1}{(1-\kappab)^2 - |\gammaL|^2} \,.
\end{equation*}

(d) For $\gamma_2 = 0$, the shear matrix is diagonal:
$\gamma_1 > 0$ means $\lambda_- = 1 - \kappab - \gamma_1$ corresponds
to stretching along the $\theta_1$ axis (the major axis is horizontal).
$\gamma_1 < 0$ means the major axis is vertical (along $\theta_2$).
\begin{derivation}[Part (d) from the eigenvectors]
With $\gamma_2 = 0$, $\Amat = \operatorname{diag}(1 - \kappab - \gamma_1,\, 1 - \kappab + \gamma_1)$
is already diagonal. Its eigenvectors are the coordinate axes. For
$\gamma_1 > 0$ the smaller eigenvalue (assuming $1 - \kappab > \gamma_1$) sits
on $\theta_1$, so the major axis $\delta/|\lambda_-|$ is along $\theta_1$. In
general the major axis points along $\phi_\gamma = \tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}(\gamma_2, \gamma_1)$;
$\gamma_1 < 0$, $\gamma_2 = 0$ gives $\phi_\gamma = 90^\circ$, along $\theta_2$.
\end{derivation}

(e) For $\kappab = 0.3$, $|\gammaL| = 0.2$:
\begin{align*}
    \lambda_+ &= 1 - 0.3 + 0.2 = 0.9 \,, \\
    \lambda_- &= 1 - 0.3 - 0.2 = 0.5 \,, \\
    b/a &= 0.5/0.9 \approx 0.556 \,, \\
    \mu &= 1/(0.9 \times 0.5) = 1/0.45 \approx 2.22 \,.
\end{align*}
The image is approximately $2.2\times$ magnified and has an axis ratio
of about $0.56$ (moderately elliptical). \checkmark
\begin{annotation}[The reduced shear organizes the answer]
The shape depends only on $g = |\gammaL|/(1 - \kappab) = 0.2/0.7 = 0.286$:
$b/a = (1 - g)/(1 + g) = 5/9 = 0.556$, as found. The size depends on
$\kappab$ as well. A mass-sheet transformation
($\kappab \to \lambda\kappab + 1 - \lambda$, $\gammaL \to \lambda\gammaL$) would
leave $b/a$ unchanged and multiply $\mu = 2.22$ by $1/\lambda^2$. This is
why image shapes alone cannot measure absolute magnification
(Chapter~\ref{ch:msd_kinematics}).
\end{annotation}
