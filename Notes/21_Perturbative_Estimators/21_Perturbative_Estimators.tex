\chapter{First-Order Estimators and Multi-Plane Coherence}
\label{ch:perturbative_estimators}

\noindent
Every lens model is wrong in some small way. A power law has no black
hole at its centre, no core, no transition from stellar to dark matter;
a single-plane model has no line-of-sight structure. When such a model
is fitted to real image positions it does not fail loudly. It returns
parameters that are \emph{biased}: the fit absorbs the omitted component
by moving whatever parameters it has. The usual way to find out by how
much is numerical --- simulate data with the extra component, refit,
compare. That answers ``how much'' for one configuration at a time, but
it does not say \emph{why}, and it cannot tell you in advance which
images, which parameters or which configurations are the dangerous ones.

This chapter develops the analytic counterpart: a \textbf{first-order
estimator} that predicts, in closed form, the bias of a least-squares
fit when the omitted component is small. The idea is general --- any
omitted nuisance component, any parametric model --- and the recipe fits
in one box (\S\ref{sec:pe_idea}). We work it out in full for one
physically important case: a pure power law fitted to a lens that also
contains a small central point mass, such as a supermassive black hole
(Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass} develops that lens;
Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias} catalogues the bias channels it
opens). The example shows every feature of the method: a nuisance
parameter that must not be fixed, a single image --- the radial image
--- that in a source-plane fit carries almost all the leverage (and in
an image-plane fit much less), an exact closed form for a
canonical configuration, a sign change, and, once several source planes
look through the same deflector, a \emph{coherent} error that does not
average down.

\section{The General Idea: Bias as Linear Response}
\label{sec:pe_idea}

Suppose a model with parameters $\vb{p}$ predicts data $d_j(\vb{p})$,
and the truth is the model plus a small extra piece,
\begin{equation}
    d_j^{\rm true} = d_j(\vb{p}_0) + m\,g_j ,
    \label{eq:pe_truth}
\end{equation}
where $m\ll1$ is a dimensionless amplitude and $g_j$ is the
\emph{shape} of the omitted component evaluated at datum $j$. A fit
minimises $\chi^2(\vb{p})=\sum_j w_j\,[d_j^{\rm true}-d_j(\vb{p})]^2$
with weights $w_j$. Expanding the model about the truth,
$d_j(\vb{p}_0+\delta\vb{p})\simeq d_j(\vb{p}_0)+\sum_k X_{jk}\,\delta p_k$
with $X_{jk}=\partial d_j/\partial p_k$, the residuals become
$m\,g_j-\sum_k X_{jk}\delta p_k$: a \emph{linear} least-squares problem
in $\delta\vb{p}$. Its solution is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \delta\vb{p} \;=\; m\,\bigl(X^{\mathsf T}WX\bigr)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}W\,\vb{g}
    \;+\;\order{m^2},
    \qquad W=\operatorname{diag}(w_j).\;}
    \label{eq:pe_general}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:pe_general} is the estimator. The bias is linear
in the amplitude $m$, and its coefficient is the regression of the
omitted shape $\vb{g}$ onto the columns of $X$ --- the directions in
which the model is free to move. Three consequences are immediate.
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $\vb{g}$ lies in the span of the columns of $X$, the model
          absorbs the omitted component exactly: the fit is perfect, the
          component is \emph{invisible}, and yet the parameters are
          biased.
    \item If $\vb{g}$ is orthogonal (in the metric $W$) to every column,
          the parameters are unbiased and the component shows up only in
          the residuals.
    \item Everything in between is decided by the \emph{data geometry}:
          which data exist, where they sit, and how they are weighted.
          That is why the same omitted mass produces very different
          biases in lenses with different image configurations.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[The first-order recipe]
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Write the truth as \emph{model + small component}, with an
          explicit amplitude $m$ and shape $g_j$.
    \item Linearise the residuals \emph{at the observed data} in all
          small quantities: $m$ and the parameter shifts $\delta p_k$.
    \item Include \emph{every} unobservable nuisance parameter (source
          position, overall offsets) as a free column of $X$. Fixing a
          nuisance that the data do not determine changes the answer.
    \item Solve the normal equations, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_general}. The
          bias is $m$ times a response coefficient that depends only on
          the data geometry and the weights.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keybox}

\begin{annotation}[Why ``response coefficient'']
When only one parameter of interest remains (plus nuisances), the
matrix expression collapses to a single number, $\delta p=m\,c$. We
call $c$ the \emph{response coefficient}: the fractional change of the
fitted parameter per unit fraction of omitted mass. It plays the role
of a susceptibility in linear-response theory --- a property of the
probe (the image positions, the model, the weights), not of the
perturbation's amplitude.
\end{annotation}

\section{Worked Example: A Power Law with a Hidden Central Mass}
\label{sec:pe_setup}

\subsection{The lens}

Take an axisymmetric lens and work along the line through the lens
centre and the source, so that image and source positions are signed
one-dimensional angles $\theta$ and $\ysrc$. The true deflection is a
power law plus a central point mass,
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\theta) \;=\;
    \underbrace{\thetaE^{\gp-1}\,|\theta|^{2-\gp}\operatorname{sgn}\theta}_{\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta)}
    \;+\;
    \underbrace{\mBH\,\frac{\thetaE^{2}}{\theta}}_{\text{point mass}} ,
    \qquad 1<\gp<2,
    \label{eq:pe_deflection}
\end{equation}
where $\gp$ is the slope of the three-dimensional density
$\rho\propto r^{-\gp}$ and $\thetaE$ is the Einstein radius of the
power-law component. The first term is the deflection produced by the
power-law convergence of Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_power_law}; the second is
the point-mass deflection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pm_alpha}, with the point
mass's own Einstein radius written as $\thetaBH^2=\mBH\,\thetaE^2$. The
amplitude
\begin{equation}
    \mBH \;=\; \frac{\thetaBH^2}{\thetaE^2}
    \;=\; \frac{M_\bullet}{M_{\rm PL}(<\thetaE)}
    \label{eq:pe_m_def}
\end{equation}
is the central mass as a fraction of the power-law mass inside the
Einstein radius. The second equality holds because the mass inside any
Einstein radius is $\pi\thetaE^2\Dd^2\Sigmacr$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:mass_einstein}), for the power law and for the point
mass alike.

\begin{bridge}[Back to the axisymmetric formalism]
Everything in this chapter is one-dimensional because of the
axisymmetric reduction of Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}: the
deflection is radial, Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_radial}, and the lens
equation is $\beta=\theta-\alpha(\theta)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:lens_eq_1d}.
Letting $\theta$ be negative describes images on both sides of the
centre with one equation. The relation $\alpha(\thetaE)=\thetaE$, i.e.\
a mean convergence $\bar\kappa(\thetaE)=1$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_mean_kappa}, is what makes $\mBH$ a mass ratio.
\end{bridge}

The observer, unaware of the point mass, fits a \emph{pure} power law
with the slope held fixed (we free it in \S\ref{sec:pe_free_slope}).
The free parameters are the Einstein radius and the source position,
which is never observed.

\subsection{Image configuration}

For the pure power law ($\mBH=0$) and a source at $0<\ysrc$ inside the
radial caustic, the lens equation $\ysrc=\theta-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta)$
has three solutions: an image $\theta_A>\thetaE$ on the source side, a
counter-image $\theta_B$ with $\theta_r<|\theta_B|<\thetaE$, and a faint
\emph{radial image} $\theta_C$ with $|\theta_C|<\theta_r$. The divide
between the last two is the radial critical radius, where the radial
eigenvalue of the Jacobian,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_r(\theta) \;=\; 1-\deriv{\alpha_{\rm PL}}{\theta}
    \;=\; 1-(2-\gp)\Bigl|\frac{\theta}{\thetaE}\Bigr|^{1-\gp}
    \;=\; 1-\Bigl|\frac{\theta}{\theta_r}\Bigr|^{1-\gp},
    \qquad
    \theta_r=(2-\gp)^{1/(\gp-1)}\,\thetaE ,
    \label{eq:pe_lambda_r}
\end{equation}
vanishes. Since $1-\gp<0$, $\lambda_r<0$ inside $\theta_r$ (the radial
image has negative radial parity) and $\lambda_r>0$ outside; at the
Einstein radius $\lambda_r(\thetaE)=\gp-1$. With $\mBH>0$ a fourth,
extremely faint image appears very close to the point mass; it plays no
role at first order and is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:odd_images}.

\paragraph{A running example.} Throughout we use $\gp=1.6$ and a source
at $\ysrc=0.2\,\thetaE$. Then $\theta_r=0.217\,\thetaE$ and the three
power-law images sit at
\[
    \theta_A=+1.316\,\thetaE,\qquad
    \theta_B=-0.633\,\thetaE,\qquad
    \theta_C=-0.0237\,\thetaE ,
\]
with radial eigenvalues $\lambda_r=0.66$, $0.47$ and $-2.78$.

\subsection{First-order image positions}
\label{sec:pe_image_shift}

Switch on the point mass at fixed source position. Differentiating the
lens equation $\ysrc=\theta-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta)-\mBH\thetaE^2/\theta$
implicitly with respect to $\mBH$ at $\mBH=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \delta\theta_j \;=\; \mBH\,\frac{a_j}{\lambda_r(\theta_j)}
    +\order{\mBH^2},
    \qquad a_j\equiv\frac{\thetaE^2}{\theta_j}.\;}
    \label{eq:pe_image_shift}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[Implicit differentiation of the lens equation]
Write $F(\theta,\mBH)=\theta-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta)-\mBH\thetaE^2/\theta-\ysrc=0$.
Along a branch of solutions $\theta_j(\mBH)$,
\[
  0=\deriv{F}{\mBH}=\pderiv{F}{\theta}\deriv{\theta_j}{\mBH}+\pderiv{F}{\mBH}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \deriv{\theta_j}{\mBH}=-\frac{\partial F/\partial\mBH}{\partial F/\partial\theta}
  =\frac{\thetaE^2/\theta_j}{1-\alpha_{\rm PL}'(\theta_j)+\mBH\thetaE^2/\theta_j^2}.
\]
At $\mBH=0$ the denominator is $\lambda_r(\theta_j)$. The derivative of
$\operatorname{sgn}\theta\,|\theta|^{2-\gp}$ is $(2-\gp)|\theta|^{1-\gp}$
on both sides of the origin, which gives the middle form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_lambda_r}; substituting
$(2-\gp)=(\theta_r/\thetaE)^{\gp-1}$ gives the last form.
\end{derivation}

\begin{bridge}[The Jacobian sets the response of an image]
Equation~\eqref{eq:pe_image_shift} is a one-dimensional instance of a
general fact from Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}: a small extra
deflection $\delta\vecalpha$ at fixed source moves an image by
$\delta\vectheta=\Amat^{-1}\delta\vecalpha$, with $\Amat$ the Jacobian of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:jacobian_def}. Only the radial eigenvalue $\lambda_r$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalues_circular} enters here, because both the
perturbation and the displacement are radial. Images near a critical
curve ($\lambda\to0$) are hypersensitive to any perturbation --- for the
same reason that they are highly magnified.
\end{bridge}

In the running example the shifts per unit $\mBH$ are $1.15$, $-3.34$
and $15.2$ (in units of $\thetaE$) for $A$, $B$ and $C$, and an exact
numerical solve of the perturbed lens equation reproduces them to
better than $10^{-4}$. The radial image moves by far the most, because
the point-mass deflection $a_j\propto1/\theta_j$ is largest there.

\section{Residuals and the Least-Squares Fit}
\label{sec:pe_residuals}

\subsection{The first-order residual}

A position-only fit asks: is there one source position
$\ysrc_{\rm fit}$ and one Einstein radius $\thetaE e^{\epsilon}$ such
that the model maps every observed image back to that source? Its
residual at image $j$ is
$r_j=\theta_j-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta_j;\thetaE e^{\epsilon})-\ysrc_{\rm fit}$.
The true lens maps the same image to the true source,
$\ysrc=\theta_j-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta_j;\thetaE)-\mBH a_j$. Subtracting
and writing $\ysrc_{\rm fit}=\ysrc+\delta\ysrc$,
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    r_j \;=\; \mBH\,a_j \;-\;\epsilon\,b_j\;-\;\delta\ysrc ,
    \qquad
    a_j=\frac{\thetaE^2}{\theta_j},\qquad
    b_j=\pderiv{\alpha_{\rm PL}}{\ln\thetaE}\bigg|_{\theta_j}
       =(\gp-1)\,\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta_j)\;}
    \label{eq:pe_residual}
\end{equation}
to first order in the small quantities $(\mBH,\epsilon,\delta\ysrc)$.
Here $a_j$ is the shape of the omitted component and $b_j$ is the one
direction in which the fit can move its deflection. This is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_truth} with $\vb{g}=\vb{a}$ and two columns,
$X=[\,\vb{b},\;\vb{1}\,]$; the column of ones is the source shift.

\begin{derivation}[Expanding the residual]
Since $\alpha_{\rm PL}\propto\thetaE^{\gp-1}$,
$\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta;\thetaE e^{\epsilon})=e^{(\gp-1)\epsilon}\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta;\thetaE)
=\alpha_{\rm PL}+(\gp-1)\epsilon\,\alpha_{\rm PL}+\order{\epsilon^2}$.
Then
\[
r_j=\bigl[\theta_j-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta_j)-(\gp-1)\epsilon\,\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta_j)\bigr]
-\bigl[\theta_j-\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta_j)-\mBH a_j\bigr]-\delta\ysrc
=\mBH a_j-\epsilon b_j-\delta\ysrc .
\]
The residual is evaluated at the \emph{observed} image positions.
Whether one uses those or the unperturbed ones of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_image_shift} changes $a_j$ and $b_j$ only at
$\order{\mBH}$, and hence the bias only at $\order{\mBH^2}$.
\end{derivation}

\subsection{Which $\chi^2$? Source plane versus image plane}

Minimising $\sum_j r_j^2$ is a \emph{source-plane} fit. An
\emph{image-plane} fit compares predicted and observed image positions
instead. To first order the model's predicted image $j$ is displaced
from the observed one by $-r_j/\lambda_r(\theta_j)$ (the same
implicit-differentiation argument as for
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_image_shift}), so an image-plane $\chi^2$ with equal
astrometric errors $\sigma$ is again a sum over the same residuals:
\begin{equation}
    \chi^2 \;=\; \sum_j w_j\,r_j^2,
    \qquad
    w_j=
    \begin{cases}
      1 & \text{source plane},\\[2pt]
      \dfrac{1}{\sigma^2\lambda_r(\theta_j)^2} & \text{image plane}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:pe_chi2}
\end{equation}
Both are weighted linear regressions; they differ only in the weights.
We keep $w_j$ general.

\subsection{The normal equations and the response coefficient}

Setting $\partial\chi^2/\partial\epsilon=\partial\chi^2/\partial\delta\ysrc=0$
gives two linear equations,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_j w_j\,b_j\,(\mBH a_j-\epsilon b_j-\delta\ysrc)=0,
    \qquad
    \sum_j w_j\,(\mBH a_j-\epsilon b_j-\delta\ysrc)=0 ,
    \label{eq:pe_normal}
\end{equation}
whose solution is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \epsilon=\mBH\,c,\qquad
    c=\frac{\sum_j w_j\,(a_j-\bar a)(b_j-\bar b)}{\sum_j w_j\,(b_j-\bar b)^2}
     \equiv\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_w(a,b)}{\operatorname{Var}_w(b)},
    \qquad
    \delta\ysrc=\mBH\,(\bar a-c\,\bar b),\;}
    \label{eq:pe_c}
\end{equation}
with weighted means $\bar a=\sum_j w_ja_j/\sum_j w_j$ and
$\bar b=\sum_j w_jb_j/\sum_j w_j$. The response coefficient is the slope
of a weighted linear regression of the omitted shape $a$ on the model's
direction $b$ \emph{with an intercept}, and the intercept is the source
shift.

\begin{derivation}[Solving the normal equations]
Divide the second of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:pe_normal} by $\sum_jw_j$:
$\delta\ysrc=\mBH\bar a-\epsilon\bar b$. Substitute into the first, which
becomes $\sum_jw_jb_j[\mBH(a_j-\bar a)-\epsilon(b_j-\bar b)]=0$. Because
$\sum_jw_j(a_j-\bar a)=\sum_jw_j(b_j-\bar b)=0$, the factor $b_j$ may be
replaced by $b_j-\bar b$:
\[
\mBH\sum_jw_j(a_j-\bar a)(b_j-\bar b)=\epsilon\sum_jw_j(b_j-\bar b)^2 ,
\]
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_c}. It is also the special case
$X=[\vb{b},\vb{1}]$, $\vb{g}=\vb{a}$ of the matrix formula
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_general}; both forms are checked symbolically for
general weights in \texttt{response\_coefficient.wl}.
\end{derivation}

Three properties follow directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_c}.

\textbf{Two images give an exact fit.} With two images there are two
equations and two unknowns, both residuals can be made zero, and
$c=(a_1-a_2)/(b_1-b_2)$ for any weights. A source plane with only two
images cannot \emph{detect} the central mass; it can only be biased by
it.

\textbf{The source shift absorbs any constant.} Replacing
$a_j\to a_j+\kappa$ shifts $\bar a$ by $\kappa$ and leaves every centred
difference, and hence $c$, unchanged; only $\delta\ysrc$ moves.

\textbf{$c$ is scale free.} Under $\theta\to\lambda\theta$,
$\thetaE\to\lambda\thetaE$ both $a_j$ and $b_j$ scale by $\lambda$, so
$c$ depends only on the dimensionless positions $\theta_j/\thetaE$. We
set $\thetaE=1$ from now on.

\begin{keybox}[The free intercept is not a technicality]
The source position is not observed, so $\delta\ysrc$ must be fitted.
Dropping it --- regressing $a$ on $b$ through the origin --- is
equivalent to claiming the source position is known, and gives a
different, wrong, $c$. The invariance of $c$ under $a_j\to a_j+\kappa$
is exactly the statement that a uniform shift of the source is
unobservable. In the general recipe this is step~3: every unobservable
nuisance parameter is a free column.
\end{keybox}

\section{The Radial Image's Leverage}
\label{sec:pe_leverage}

\subsection{The tangential pair alone measures enclosed mass}

Start with only the two bright images, idealised as a symmetric pair at
$\theta=\pm1$. Then $a=(1,-1)$ and $b=(\gp-1)(1,-1)$, so $a=b/(\gp-1)$
exactly: on these two images the point-mass shape is
\emph{indistinguishable} from a rescaling of the Einstein radius, and
\begin{equation}
    c_{\rm pair}=\frac{1}{\gp-1}.
    \label{eq:pe_floor}
\end{equation}
This number has a clean meaning. Solving the lens equation of the
\emph{total} lens for its Einstein radius to first order in $\mBH$
gives
\begin{equation}
    \thetaE^{\rm tot}=\thetaE\Bigl(1+\frac{\mBH}{\gp-1}\Bigr)+\order{\mBH^2},
    \label{eq:pe_total_mass}
\end{equation}
so a fit to a symmetric tangential pair simply returns the Einstein
radius of the total enclosed mass, as it should.

\begin{bridge}[The Einstein radius measures enclosed mass]
This is the first-order version of the statement of
Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mass_einstein}, that the
Einstein radius measures the mass it encloses in projection, whatever its
radial distribution. To the tangential images a point mass inside
$\thetaE$ is just more enclosed mass; they cannot tell it from a
slightly heavier power law.
\end{bridge}

\subsection{Why the radial image dominates}

Now add a radial image at $\theta=\rho$, $0<\rho<1$. Its two shapes
behave oppositely:
\[
    a_C=\frac{1}{\rho}\;\to\;\infty,
    \qquad
    b_C=(\gp-1)\rho^{2-\gp}\;\to\;0
    \qquad(\rho\to0,\ 1<\gp<2).
\]
Near the centre the point-mass deflection diverges while the power
law's response to a change of Einstein radius vanishes. This is where
the two shapes are \emph{most} different, so this is the datum that
breaks the degeneracy of the pair --- and, by the same token, the datum
that drags the fitted Einstein radius furthest when the point mass is
ignored, provided it carries the same weight as the other images (a
source-plane fit). How strongly it pulls in an image-plane fit is a
different matter (\S\ref{sec:pe_beyond_canonical}).

\subsection{The exact closed form for the canonical triple}
\label{sec:pe_closed_form}

For the \textbf{canonical triple} --- a symmetric pair at $\pm1$ and a
radial image at $\rho$ --- the regression can be done exactly. With unit
weights, i.e.\ for the source-plane $\chi^2$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_chi2},
\begin{equation}
    a=\Bigl(1,\,-1,\,\frac1\rho\Bigr),
    \qquad
    b=(\gp-1)\bigl(1,\,-1,\,\rho^{2-\gp}\bigr),
    \label{eq:pe_triple}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}(a,b)=2(\gp-1)\Bigl[1+\tfrac13\rho^{1-\gp}\Bigr],
    \qquad
    \operatorname{Var}(b)=2(\gp-1)^2\Bigl[1+\tfrac13\rho^{4-2\gp}\Bigr],
    \label{eq:pe_covvar}
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    c(\rho,\gp)=\frac{3+\rho^{1-\gp}}{(\gp-1)\bigl(3+\rho^{4-2\gp}\bigr)}\;}
    \label{eq:pe_cclosed}
\end{equation}
This is exact, not an expansion in $\rho$: three data and two free
parameters leave one residual degree of freedom, and the normal
equations close.

\begin{derivation}[Centring the canonical triple]
The pair cancels in both means, so exactly
$\bar a=\tfrac13(1-1+\rho^{-1})=1/(3\rho)$ and
$\bar b=\tfrac13(\gp-1)\rho^{2-\gp}$. Using
$\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)=\sum_ja_jb_j-3\bar a\bar b$ and
$\sum_ja_jb_j=(\gp-1)\bigl[1+1+\rho^{-1}\rho^{2-\gp}\bigr]=(\gp-1)\bigl[2+\rho^{1-\gp}\bigr]$,
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)=(\gp-1)\Bigl[2+\rho^{1-\gp}-\tfrac13\rho^{1-\gp}\Bigr]
=2(\gp-1)\Bigl[1+\tfrac13\rho^{1-\gp}\Bigr].
\]
Similarly $\sum_jb_j^2=(\gp-1)^2[2+\rho^{4-2\gp}]$ and
$3\bar b^2=\tfrac13(\gp-1)^2\rho^{4-2\gp}$ give $\operatorname{Var}(b)$.
The factor $2(\gp-1)$ cancels in the ratio, and multiplying numerator
and denominator by $3$ gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_cclosed}. If the radial
image sits on the counter-image side ($\theta_C=-\rho$), both $a_C$ and
$b_C$ change sign and $c$ is unchanged: $c$ is even in the radial
image's side.
\end{derivation}

\paragraph{Limits.} Figure~\ref{fig:pe_response} shows
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_cclosed}; its limits carry the physics of the
source-plane fit.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\rho\to1$: $c\to4/[4(\gp-1)]=1/(\gp-1)$, the floor of
          Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_floor}. The ``radial'' image has merged with
          a tangential one and the configuration has lost its extra
          datum. For the canonical triple, masked and unmasked cases
          are one function evaluated at the two ends of $\rho$.
    \item $\rho\to0$: $\rho^{1-\gp}\to\infty$ dominates the numerator
          while $\rho^{4-2\gp}\to0$ in the denominator, so
          \begin{equation}
              c\;\simeq\;c_{\rm asym}\equiv\frac{\rho^{1-\gp}}{3(\gp-1)}
              \;\longrightarrow\;\infty .
              \label{eq:pe_asym}
          \end{equation}
          In a source-plane fit, the deeper the radial image, the larger
          the leverage of an unmodelled central mass. The divergence is
          a warning, not a prediction: the linear estimate fails well
          before $\mBH c$ becomes large (\S\ref{sec:pe_validity}). It is
          also specific to unit weights: under image-plane weights $c$
          tends to the floor instead (\S\ref{sec:pe_beyond_canonical}).
    \item In between, $c$ decreases strictly with $\rho$ for every
          $1<\gp<2$: the numerator of $\dd c/\dd\rho$ is a sum of three
          terms with negative coefficients
          \annot{\texttt{response\_coefficient.wl}, check~10}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How good is the asymptote?}

Dividing Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_cclosed} by Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_asym} gives an
exact correction factor (Fig.~\ref{fig:pe_corrfactor}),
\begin{equation}
    \frac{c}{c_{\rm asym}}=\frac{1+3u}{1+v/3},
    \qquad u\equiv\rho^{\gp-1},\qquad v\equiv\rho^{4-2\gp}.
    \label{eq:pe_corrfactor}
\end{equation}
Here $u$ is the tangential pair's contribution to the covariance and $v$
is the radial image's contribution to the variance --- precisely the
two terms the $\rho\to0$ limit discards. Both exponents are positive for
$1<\gp<2$, so both corrections vanish as $\rho\to0$, but at different
rates. Expanding to first order in each,
\begin{equation}
    c\simeq\frac{\rho^{1-\gp}+3-\tfrac13\rho^{5-3\gp}}{3(\gp-1)} ,
    \label{eq:pe_next_order}
\end{equation}
a form that displays the structure; Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_cclosed} is the
form to use.

\begin{keybox}[$\gp=5/3$ is a structural boundary]
Which correction in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_corrfactor} leads is decided by the
slope alone: $\gp-1<4-2\gp$ if and only if $\gp<5/3$. Below $\gp=5/3$ the
$+3u$ term rules and $c$ approaches the asymptote from \emph{above};
above $5/3$ the $v/3$ term rules at small $\rho$ and $c$ dips
\emph{below} it. The two cross where $\rho^{5-3\gp}=9$, i.e.\ at
\[
    \rho_\times=9^{1/(5-3\gp)},
\]
which lies inside $0<\rho<1$ only for $\gp>5/3$ (for example
$\rho_\times=1/243\approx4.1\times10^{-3}$ at $\gp=1.8$ and
$4.3\times10^{-2}$ at $\gp=1.9$). A table of $c/c_{\rm asym}$ that dips
slightly below one at steep slopes is therefore not a numerical glitch;
it is this crossing.
\end{keybox}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{../Figures/21_Perturbative_Estimators/correction_factor.pdf}
    \caption{The exact correction factor $c/c_{\rm asym}$ of
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_corrfactor} against the radial-image position
    $\rho$. For $\gp<5/3$ the asymptote is approached from above and can
    be badly low at moderate $\rho$; for $\gp>5/3$ the curve dips below
    unity before converging. All curves reach $3$ at $\rho=1$, where $c$
    has returned to the floor while $c_{\rm asym}$ has not. From
    \texttt{response\_coefficient.wl}.}
    \label{fig:pe_corrfactor}
\end{figure}

\begin{annotation}[Reading the correction factor]
The asymptote is the natural first answer and is correct as a limit,
but at realistic radial-image depths it can be off by a large factor:
for shallow profiles the dropped tangential-pair term $3u$ decays only
like $\rho^{\gp-1}$, which is very slow.
Exercise~\ref{ex:pe_radial_leverage}(e) puts a number on it. In
general, the accuracy of a leading-order asymptote of a regression
coefficient is set by the slowest-decaying term it drops.
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Beyond the canonical triple}
\label{sec:pe_beyond_canonical}

Real tangential pairs are \emph{not} symmetric. In the running example
($\gp=1.6$, $\ysrc=0.2$) the source-plane regression of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_c}, with $w_j=1$ on the three images listed in
\S\ref{sec:pe_setup}, gives
$c=10.46$, whereas the canonical formula evaluated at
$\rho=|\theta_C|=0.0237$ gives $6.81$: the asymmetric pair raises $c$ by
about half. With the radial image masked, the same regression gives
$c=2.00$ rather than the canonical floor $1/(\gp-1)=1.67$. The closed
form is the tool for understanding structure and limits; for a specific
lens one evaluates Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_c} on the actual images.

The estimator itself is confirmed by brute force in
\texttt{response\_coefficient.wl}: generating the images of the
power-law-plus-point-mass lens for $\mBH=10^{-6}$ and fitting a pure
power law with \texttt{FindMinimum} returns $\epsilon/\mBH$ equal to the
linear $c$ evaluated on the same perturbed images to better than
$10^{-4}$, both with and without the radial image. (On those images
$c=10.466$. Evaluating $a_j$ and $b_j$ on the perturbed rather than the
unperturbed images changes $c$ only at $\order{\mBH}$, here by a
relative $4\times10^{-4}$ at $\mBH=10^{-6}$, large because the radial image is so
sensitive; see \S\ref{sec:pe_validity}.)

\paragraph{The weights matter.} An image-plane fit of the same three
images --- Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_chi2} with $w_j\propto1/\lambda_r^2$ ---
gives $c=2.08$, again confirmed by a brute-force image-plane fit. The
radial image is \emph{demagnified}, $|\lambda_r(\theta_C)|=2.78>1$, so a
given source-plane residual corresponds to a small image-plane
displacement and the image-plane $\chi^2$ down-weights it. The radial
image's leverage is therefore a property of the \emph{estimator} as well
as of the lens: which $\chi^2$ is minimised, and with which astrometric
errors, is part of the data geometry in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_general}.

The weights change the dependence on $\rho$ as well as the value of
$c$. Apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_chi2} with equal $\sigma$ to the canonical
triple: the pair has weight $1/(\gp-1)^2$, and the radial image has weight
$1/\lambda_r(\rho)^2$. Since $|\lambda_r(\rho)|$ grows like
$\rho^{1-\gp}$, that weight falls like $\rho^{2(\gp-1)}$, and the deep
radial image is switched off rather than given unlimited leverage.
At $\gp=1.6$ the image-plane $c$ does not diverge but tends to the floor
$1/(\gp-1)=5/3$ as $\rho\to0$, and it is not monotonic: $1.70$, $1.83$,
$2.48$, $3.72$, $4.76$ and $4.70$ at $\rho=0.001$, $0.01$, $0.05$,
$0.1$, $0.15$ and $0.2$, where the source-plane values are $36.7$,
$10.4$, $4.87$, $3.68$, $3.17$ and $2.86$. The $\rho\to0$ divergence
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_asym}, Fig.~\ref{fig:pe_response} and the sign-change
example of \S\ref{sec:pe_sign_change} are therefore source-plane
(unit-weight) results.

\begin{annotation}[Common error: quoting $c$ without the likelihood]
A response coefficient is meaningless without the weights it was
computed with. Source-plane and image-plane fits of identical data can
differ by a large factor, as the running example shows. When comparing
an analytic estimate with a numerical experiment, check first that both
minimise the same $\chi^2$.
\end{annotation}

\section{Freeing the Slope: Where the Leverage Goes}
\label{sec:pe_free_slope}

So far the slope was fixed. Freeing it adds a third column to $X$,
\begin{equation}
    h_j=\pderiv{\alpha_{\rm PL}}{\gp}\bigg|_{\theta_j}
    =\alpha_{\rm PL}(\theta_j)\,\ln\frac{\thetaE}{|\theta_j|} ,
    \label{eq:pe_hcolumn}
\end{equation}
which vanishes at $|\theta|=\thetaE$: the power-law deflection at the
Einstein radius equals $\thetaE$ for \emph{every} slope, so the slope
pivots there. On the canonical triple there are now three equations for
three unknowns $(\epsilon,\delta\gp,\delta\ysrc)$, and the exact solution
is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \epsilon=\frac{\mBH}{\gp-1},\qquad
    \delta\gp=\mBH\,\frac{\rho^{\gp-3}-1}{\ln(1/\rho)}>0,\qquad
    \delta\ysrc=0,\;}
    \label{eq:pe_free_slope}
\end{equation}
with all three residuals exactly zero.

\begin{derivation}[The three-parameter solve]
The pair equations are $\pm\mBH=\pm\epsilon(\gp-1)+\delta\ysrc$ (their
$h_j$ vanish), so $\delta\ysrc=0$ and $\epsilon=\mBH/(\gp-1)$. The
radial-image equation is then
$\mBH/\rho=\epsilon(\gp-1)\rho^{2-\gp}+\delta\gp\,\rho^{2-\gp}\ln(1/\rho)$;
subtracting the first term on the right and dividing by
$\rho^{2-\gp}\ln(1/\rho)$ gives $\delta\gp$. Writing $\rho=e^{-L}$ with
$L>0$, $\delta\gp/\mBH=(e^{L(3-\gp)}-1)/L>0$.
\end{derivation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:pe_free_slope} makes the role of the radial image
explicit. For the canonical triple with the slope free, the Einstein
radius falls back to the
total-mass value of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_total_mass}, and \emph{all} of the
radial image's information goes into the slope: the fitted profile
steepens to mimic the central mass, the fit is perfect, and the central
mass is undetectable from these three positions. Without the radial
image the free-slope normal matrix is singular (rank~2 for three
parameters): two tangential images at the pivot cannot constrain the
slope at all, so the radial image is what fixes the slope, and an
unmodelled central mass biases the slope through it. For the asymmetric
running example the same calculation gives $\delta\gp/\mBH=86.4$ and
$\epsilon/\mBH=-3.51$, again confirmed by a three-parameter
\texttt{FindMinimum} fit: the fit is still exact and the slope still
steepens, but the Einstein radius no longer returns to the total-mass
value $1/(\gp-1)=1.67$. How this slope bias propagates into other
observables is one of the channels of
Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}.

\section{Two Source Planes: Bias of the Distance Ratio}
\label{sec:pe_dspl}

\subsection{Setup}

In a double-source-plane lens (Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}) the
same deflector images two sources at different redshifts. Let
$d_i=D_{{\rm d}s_i}/D_{s_i}$ be the distance factor of plane $i$, with
plane~1 the nearer source, and
\begin{equation}
    \Brat=\frac{d_1}{d_2}<1
    \label{eq:pe_B}
\end{equation}
their ratio --- the cosmological observable of a double-source-plane
lens \citep{collett_2012}. We treat each plane as lensed by the main
deflector only: any extra deflection of the far source by the nearer
source's own mass is assumed to be modelled correctly
(Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl} gives the full multi-plane
treatment). Because every reduced deflection scales with $d_i$,
\begin{equation}
    \thetaE^{(i)\,\gp-1}\propto d_i,\qquad
    \thetaBH^{(i)\,2}\propto d_i
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1},\quad
    \mBH_2=\mBH_1\Brat^{\,k},\quad
    k=\frac{3-\gp}{\gp-1}>0 ,
    \label{eq:pe_mscaling}
\end{equation}
where $\etamea=\thetaE^{(1)}/\thetaE^{(2)}$.

\begin{derivation}[Why the central-mass fraction falls with source distance]
The normalisation $\thetaE^{\gp-1}$ of $\alpha_{\rm PL}$ carries all the
distance dependence of the power law, so
$\thetaE^{(i)}\propto d_i^{1/(\gp-1)}$ and
$\etamea^{\gp-1}=d_1/d_2=\Brat$. A point mass has $\thetaBH^2\propto d_i$.
Hence
$\mBH_i=\thetaBH^{(i)2}/\thetaE^{(i)2}\propto d_i^{\,1-2/(\gp-1)}=d_i^{-k}$,
and $\mBH_2/\mBH_1=(d_1/d_2)^{k}=\Brat^{k}$. Since $k>0$ for $1<\gp<3$,
the farther plane sees a \emph{smaller} central-mass fraction: its
Einstein radius grows faster with distance than the point mass's.
\end{derivation}

\begin{bridge}[Distances]
The distance factors $d_i$ are ratios of the angular-diameter distances
of Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}; $\Brat$ depends on cosmology
only through them, which is what makes it a cosmological probe. Nothing
in this chapter needs their explicit form.
\end{bridge}

At fixed $\gp$ the two planes share no fitted parameter --- each has its
own Einstein radius and its own source position --- so \textbf{the
planes decouple}: each is a separate regression with its own response
coefficient $c_i$, and $\epsilon_i=\mBH_i c_i$.

\subsection{The bias}

Linearising $\ln\Brat=(\gp-1)\ln(\thetaE^{(1)}/\thetaE^{(2)})$ gives
$\delta\ln\Brat=(\gp-1)(\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2)$, and with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_mscaling},
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \frac{\Delta\Brat}{\Brat}=(\gp-1)\,\mBH_1\bigl[c_1-\Brat^{\,k}c_2\bigr]
    +\order{\mBH^2}.\;}
    \label{eq:pe_bias}
\end{equation}
The bias is a \emph{difference}: it would vanish if both planes
responded alike, and it survives because $\Brat^{k}<1$ suppresses
plane~2's response. Each $c_i$ depends on where that plane's images
sit, so the bias depends on the source positions --- above all through
the depth of each plane's radial image.

\paragraph{The total-mass shortcut.} If both planes are put at the
floor, $c_1=c_2=1/(\gp-1)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_bias} reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Delta\Brat}{\Brat}\bigg|_{\rm floor}=\mBH_1\bigl(1-\Brat^{k}\bigr),
    \label{eq:pe_shortcut}
\end{equation}
which is just the difference between the total-mass Einstein radii of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_total_mass} on the two planes. It is a correct answer
to a different question --- how two \emph{definitions} of the Einstein
radius differ --- and contains no image positions, no source
marginalisation and no fit. In particular it is positive for every
$0<\Brat<1$.

\subsection{The sign change}
\label{sec:pe_sign_change}

Equation~\eqref{eq:pe_bias} changes sign where the bracket vanishes:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;\frac{c_2}{c_1}=\Brat^{-k}>1.\;}
    \label{eq:pe_sign}
\end{equation}
The bias is positive while plane~2 responds less than $\Brat^{-k}$ times
as strongly as plane~1, and negative beyond. In a source-plane fit a
deep radial image raises $c$ without bound, so a plane-2 radial image
close enough to the centre always flips the sign. For example, with
$\gp=1.6$, $\Brat=0.8$, plane~1 at its floor and plane~2 a canonical
triple, the source-plane bias is negative for $\rho_2<0.212$ and
positive above. (A root always exists in $0<\rho_2<1$: the source-plane
$c_2(\gp-1)$ runs from $1$ at $\rho_2=1$ to $\infty$ as $\rho_2\to0$,
while $\Brat^{-k}>1$.) An image-plane fit of the same configuration
reverses the pattern: since its $c_2$ returns to the floor as
$\rho_2\to0$ (\S\ref{sec:pe_beyond_canonical}), the bias is positive for
a very deep radial image and negative only for $0.065<\rho_2<\theta_r$.
The shortcut Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_shortcut} has a fixed sign and cannot
reproduce either pattern. The sign of the cosmological bias is set by
the image geometry together with the choice of $\chi^2$.

\section{Multi-Plane Coherence}
\label{sec:pe_coherence}

With $N$ source planes, form the ratios $\Brat_i=d_1/d_i$ against the
nearest plane ($i=2,\dots,N$). The derivation is unchanged, so the
response of each ratio to the central mass is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    r_i\equiv\pderiv{\ln\Brat_i}{\mBH_1}=(\gp-1)\bigl[c_1-\Brat_i^{\,k}c_i\bigr].\;}
    \label{eq:pe_ri}
\end{equation}
Three structural facts follow (checked symbolically for $N=5$ planes).
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Every $r_i$ contains the same $c_1$}, with the same
          coefficient $\gp-1$. All ratios share the reference plane and
          look through one deflector, so their errors share a cause: the
          $r_i$ form a deterministic family, not independent draws.
    \item \textbf{A common non-zero limit.} As $\Brat_i^{k}\to0$,
          $r_i\to(\gp-1)c_1$ whatever $c_i$ is. In that limit plane $i$
          sees a negligible central-mass fraction
          ($\mBH_i=\mBH_1\Brat_i^{k}\to0$) and acts as a clean reference
          against which plane~1's contamination is measured in full. The
          response tends to a constant, never to zero.
    \item \textbf{No cancellation.} For equal $c_i=c>0$,
          $r_i=(\gp-1)c\,(1-\Brat_i^{k})$ lies strictly between $0$ and
          $(\gp-1)c$ and grows monotonically as $\Brat_i$ decreases: all
          ratios are biased in the same direction.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{annotation}[How far can the limit be pushed?]
Every $d_i$ is less than one, so $\Brat_i=d_1/d_i>d_1$: even a source at
infinite redshift does \emph{not} send $\Brat_i\to0$. The limit is
approached, not reached, and is approached fastest for shallow slopes,
where $k$ is large. Its content is structural: the responses are bounded
by, and pulled towards, the shared plane-1 term.
\end{annotation}

\section{The Consequence: Significance, Not Amplitude}
\label{sec:pe_significance}

``Does the bias stack across planes?'' is best asked not of the raw size
of the bias but of its size \emph{in units of the statistical error},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}=\frac{|\text{systematic shift of a parameter}|}{\text{statistical error of that parameter}} ,
    \label{eq:pe_S}
\end{equation}
because that is what decides whether a published result is wrong. Two
generic facts about linear least squares settle how $\mathcal{S}$
scales.

\textbf{The bias does not know the noise level.} For data with
covariance $C=\sigma^2I$ and Jacobian $J$, the bias of a linear fit to a
data shift $\boldsymbol\delta$ is
$(J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J)^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\boldsymbol\delta$, in
which $\sigma^2$ cancels, while the parameter covariance
$(J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J)^{-1}$ scales as $\sigma^2$. Better data shrink
the error bar but not the bias, so $\mathcal{S}\propto1/\sigma$.

\textbf{Coherent biases do not average down.} In the simplest toy ---
$N$ ratios with equal sensitivity to one parameter, each shifted by the
same $\mBH r$ --- the averaged parameter carries the full bias $\mBH r$
while its error falls as $\sigma/\sqrt N$, so
$\mathcal{S}_N=\sqrt N\,\mathcal{S}_1$. Had the shifts random signs, the
root-mean-square bias would fall as $1/\sqrt N$ along with the error and
$\mathcal{S}$ would not grow. Equation~\eqref{eq:pe_ri} says the
central-mass bias is of the first, coherent, kind.

\begin{keybox}[The design consequence]
A compound lens with more source planes is more \emph{exposed} to a
coherent modelling systematic, not less. Extra planes buy statistical
precision, which the systematic does not share. Intuition of the form
``$N$ planes, so errors average down like $\sqrt N$'' presumes
independent draws, and here there is one physical cause; when the
planes' response coefficients are comparable it also pushes every ratio
the same way (unequal $c_i$ can give opposite signs,
\S\ref{sec:pe_sign_change}). The only
lever that lowers $\mathcal{S}$ is the modelling one: give the
deflector's inner profile the freedom to represent the central mass, or
measure the radial image well enough to constrain it. How per-ratio
biases map into cosmological parameters through a full Fisher matrix is
the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_sensitivity}.
\end{keybox}

\section{When the Linear Estimate Fails}
\label{sec:pe_validity}

Equation~\eqref{eq:pe_general} is a first-order expansion. It fails, and
the numerics must be used, in the following situations.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The fitted shift is not small.} The step
          $e^{(\gp-1)\epsilon}\simeq1+(\gp-1)\epsilon$ requires
          $|\epsilon|=\mBH|c|\ll1$. That is necessary but not
          sufficient. The image positions of
          Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_image_shift} were also linearised, which
          requires each image to move by a small fraction of its
          distance from the centre,
          $|\mBH a_j/(\lambda_r(\theta_j)\,\theta_j)|\ll1$. For a deep
          radial image this is the stricter condition: in the running
          example it reads $640\,\mBH\ll1$, against
          $\mBH c\simeq10\,\mBH$. The brute-force $\epsilon/\mBH$ there
          departs from $c$ by $0.4\%$ at $\mBH=10^{-5}$ and by $4.5\%$ at
          $\mBH=10^{-4}$, and by $\mBH=10^{-3}$ the radial image has
          disappeared altogether (item~3). In a source-plane fit $c$ also
          grows as $\rho^{1-\gp}$, so a deep radial image breaks the
          expansion at a smaller $\mBH$ on both counts.
    \item \textbf{An image is near a critical curve.} The image shift
          $\mBH a_j/\lambda_r(\theta_j)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_image_shift}
          diverges as $\lambda_r\to0$, i.e.\ as the source approaches
          the radial caustic.
    \item \textbf{The image count changes.} A finite central mass can
          make the radial image merge with the extra central image and
          disappear. That is a change of topology, not a small
          perturbation, and no linear theory captures it
          (Chapters~\ref{ch:odd_images} and~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}).
\end{enumerate}
A fourth limitation is not a failure of linearity but of scope: the
closed form Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_cclosed} applies only to the symmetric
triple, and for any other configuration Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_c} must be
evaluated on the actual images (\S\ref{sec:pe_beyond_canonical}).

Inside its domain, the error of the linear estimate is second order.
In the running example, the departure of the brute-force
$\epsilon/\mBH$ from $c$ doubles (to within $1\%$) when $\mBH$ is
doubled from $10^{-5}$ to $2\times10^{-5}$, as an $\order{\mBH^2}$ error
in $\epsilon$ must.

\begin{annotation}[Using the estimator in practice]
The estimator does not replace a numerical bias study; it explains it.
Its job is to say which images carry the leverage, which sign to
expect, how the bias scales with the omitted amplitude, and whether
several measurements will share the error. A numerical experiment that
disagrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_general} in the small-$\mBH$ limit is
minimising a different $\chi^2$, has fixed a nuisance parameter that
should be free, or contains a bug.
\end{annotation}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:pe_summary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item To first order, the bias of a least-squares fit caused by a
          small omitted component of amplitude $m$ and shape $\vb{g}$ is
          $\delta\vb{p}=m\,(X^{\mathsf T}WX)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}W\vb{g}$:
          the regression of the omitted shape on the model's free
          directions, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_general}.
    \item Every unobservable nuisance parameter (here the source
          position) must be a free column; it enters as an intercept,
          and fixing it changes the answer.
    \item For a fixed-slope power law fitted to a lens with a central
          point mass, $\epsilon=\mBH c$ with
          $c=\operatorname{Cov}_w(a,b)/\operatorname{Var}_w(b)$,
          $a=\thetaE^2/\theta$, $b=(\gp-1)\alpha_{\rm PL}$
          (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_c}); the weights distinguish source-plane
          from image-plane fits.
    \item A symmetric tangential pair alone gives $c=1/(\gp-1)$: it
          measures the total enclosed mass. The radial image, where the
          point-mass and power-law shapes differ most, supplies the
          leverage in a source-plane fit. For the canonical triple with
          unit (source-plane) weights
          $c=(3+\rho^{1-\gp})/[(\gp-1)(3+\rho^{4-2\gp})]$ exactly, with
          asymptote $\rho^{1-\gp}/[3(\gp-1)]$, a crossing at
          $\rho_\times=9^{1/(5-3\gp)}$ for $\gp>5/3$, and the floor at
          $\rho=1$. Under image-plane weights the deep radial image is
          down-weighted and $c$ returns to the floor as $\rho\to0$.
    \item With the slope free, the radial image's leverage goes into the
          slope: the fit steepens and absorbs the central mass exactly,
          and for the canonical triple the Einstein radius returns to its
          total-mass value.
    \item For two source planes,
          $\Delta\Brat/\Brat=(\gp-1)\mBH_1[c_1-\Brat^{k}c_2]$ with
          $k=(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)$, which changes sign at
          $c_2/c_1=\Brat^{-k}$; the total-mass shortcut
          $\mBH_1(1-\Brat^{k})$ cannot. Where the sign changes depends
          on the weights: in a source-plane fit a deep enough plane-2
          radial image always makes the bias negative, in an image-plane
          fit it does not.
    \item For $N$ planes all responses share the plane-1 term and tend
          to $(\gp-1)c_1$: the error is coherent, so adding planes makes
          the systematic more \emph{significant}.
    \item The estimate fails when $\mBH c$ is not small, when a deep
          radial image's fractional shift $\mBH a_j/(\lambda_r\theta_j)$
          is not small (about $640\,\mBH$ in the running example), near
          critical curves, and when images are created or destroyed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pe_two_images}
\textbf{(Two images.)}
Derive $c=(a_1-a_2)/(b_1-b_2)$ directly by solving $r_1=r_2=0$, with
$r_j$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_residual}, and explain why the residual is

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pe_intercept}
\textbf{(Why the intercept matters.)}
Show that replacing $a_j\to a_j+\kappa$ for a constant $\kappa$ leaves
the weighted $c$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_c} unchanged, and find how
$\delta\ysrc$ changes. State the physical content of this invariance,
and show with the canonical triple that fixing $\delta\ysrc=0$ changes
$c$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pe_radial_leverage}
\textbf{(The radial image's leverage.)}
Consider the canonical triple of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_triple} with unit
(source-plane) weights.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Show that the radial image dominates $\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)$
          as $\rho\to0$ while $\operatorname{Var}(b)\to2(\gp-1)^2$, and
          deduce $c\simeq\rho^{1-\gp}/[3(\gp-1)]$.
    \item Evaluate $\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(b)$
          \emph{without} expanding and obtain Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_cclosed}.
    \item In the correction factor $(1+3u)/(1+v/3)$, identify which term
          belongs to the tangential pair and which to the radial image,
          and show that they exchange dominance at $\gp=5/3$.
    \item Check that $\rho\to1$ returns the two-image floor.
    \item For $\rho=0.03$, $\gp=1.4$, evaluate $c$ from the asymptote,
          from the one-term form Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_next_order}, and from
          Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_cclosed}. Which would you carry into a
          systematic error budget?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pe_design}
\textbf{(Design.)}
A survey can either add a fourth source plane to an existing three-plane
lens or halve the per-ratio statistical error on the existing three.
Using \S\ref{sec:pe_significance} and its equal-sensitivity toy model,
argue which does more for the \emph{accuracy} (as opposed to the
precision) of a cosmological parameter, and identify the only effective
lever.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:pe_mass_sheet}
\textbf{(A different omitted component.)}
Apply the recipe of \S\ref{sec:pe_idea} to an omitted uniform mass
sheet of convergence $\kappa$ instead of a point mass, still fitting a
fixed-slope power law with free source position.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Show that the omitted shape is $a_j=\theta_j$ (with
          $\thetaE=1$), so that the residual keeps the form of
          Eq.~\eqref{eq:pe_residual} with $\mBH\to\kappa$.
    \item For the canonical triple, show that
          $c_{\rm sheet}=(3+\rho^{3-\gp})/[(\gp-1)(3+\rho^{4-2\gp})]$.
    \item Show that $c_{\rm sheet}\to1/(\gp-1)$ both as $\rho\to0$ and
          for the tangential pair alone, and that this equals the
          first-order shift of the total-mass Einstein radius. Compare
          $c_{\rm sheet}$ with the point-mass $c$ at $\rho=0.05$,
          $\gp=1.6$, and explain why the radial image, so powerful
          against a point mass, gives almost no leverage against a
          sheet.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
