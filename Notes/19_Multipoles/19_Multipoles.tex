\chapter{Azimuthal Multipoles and the Central-Mass Confusion}
\label{ch:multipoles}

\noindent
Every lens model we have built so far has had either circular
(Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}) or elliptical
(Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}) isodensity contours, which is a
modelling convenience.  Lens galaxies carry angular structure beyond
the ellipse at the per-cent level in their mass:
\citet{oriordan_vegetti_2024} model mass multipoles of 1 and 3 per
cent, consistent with the departures seen in galaxy isophotes, and
\citet{stacey_2024} measure $m=3$ and $m=4$ multipoles of about 1\% of
the convergence in three lenses observed at high resolution with ALMA.
The classic shape is the $m=4$ Fourier mode (``boxy'' versus
``discy''); odd modes ($m=1$ lopsidedness, $m=3$) are the other
low orders.

This chapter treats such structure quantitatively, as a single
azimuthal multipole on a power-law radial base.  We derive its lensing
potential and deflection in closed form, pin down the bookkeeping that
connects the amplitude conventions in use, and then prove the result
the rest of the chapter uses: \emph{a multipole of
order $m\ge1$ encloses zero mass at every radius}.  It is therefore
exactly orthogonal to a central point mass in the space of convergence
fields, and yet the two can be confused in a fit, because a
likelihood is not an integral over a full circle.  We close with the
residual diagnostic that tells them apart: an omitted central mass
misfits the inner image region, an omitted multipole misfits the
outer arcs.

The chapter builds on the multipole series of
Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models} (\S\ref{sec:multipoles}), on the
Poisson equation and Jacobian of Chapter~\ref{ch:magnification}, and on
the power-law convergence of Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}.  Its
diagnostic feeds forward into the discussion of central-mass
inferences in Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}.

\section{Notation and Set-up}
\label{sec:mu_notation}

We use polar image-plane coordinates $(\theta,\varphi)$ centred on the
deflector, exactly as in \S\ref{sec:multipoles}.  Two notational
collisions must be settled at the outset.  First, the integer $m$ is
the azimuthal multipole order, whereas elsewhere in Part~V the symbol
$\mBH$ denotes the central point-mass fraction.  To avoid a clash, in
this chapter we write the central point-mass fraction as
\begin{equation}
    f_\bullet \;\equiv\; \frac{\thetaBH^2}{\thetaE^2},
    \label{eq:mu_fbullet}
\end{equation}
where $\thetaBH$ is the Einstein radius the point mass would have on
its own (so its deflection is $\thetaBH^2/\theta$,
Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}).  Second, the symbol $a_m$ needs
care: in \S\ref{sec:multipole_series} $a_m$ was the amplitude of an isothermal
potential multipole, $\psiL_m = a_m\theta\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]$.
Here $a_m$ is the \emph{fractional convergence} amplitude defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_am} below.  The two are related in
\S\ref{sec:mu_isothermal_limit}.
The total three-dimensional density slope is $\gp$ ($\rho\propto
r^{-\gp}$, with $\gp=2$ isothermal), as in
\S\ref{sec:power_law_profile}.

\begin{bridge}[Back to Chapters~\ref{ch:magnification} and~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}]
Everything in this chapter is a solution of the two-dimensional Poisson
equation $\nabla^2\psiL = 2\kappab$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson_2d}, with
$\vecalpha = \nabla\psiL$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_grad_psi}, and Jacobian
$\Amat = \mathbb{I} - \nabla\nabla\psiL$.  In polar coordinates the
Laplacian is $\nabla^2 = \partial_\theta^2 + \theta^{-1}\partial_\theta
+ \theta^{-2}\partial_\varphi^2$.  Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}
used it to compute the convergence of an isothermal potential
multipole, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_multipole}.  Here we run the logic in
the opposite direction and for a general slope: we specify the
convergence multipole (the quantity photometry and dynamics talk about)
and solve for its potential.
\end{bridge}

\section{The Multipole Expansion of the Convergence}
\label{sec:mu_formalism}

Any convergence field can be expanded in a Fourier series in the
azimuth,
\begin{equation}
    \kappab(\theta,\varphi) \;=\; \kappab_0(\theta)
    \;+\; \sum_{m\ge1}\kappab_m(\theta)\cos\!\big[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)\big],
    \label{eq:mu_kappa_expansion}
\end{equation}
where $\kappab_0$ is the circularly averaged (monopole) profile and
each $m\ge1$ term is a pure angular redistribution of mass with its own
orientation $\varphi_m$.  This is the convergence-space counterpart of
the potential-space series Eq.~\eqref{eq:multipole_series}.

\subsection{The power-law base}
\label{sec:mu_base}

We put the multipoles on the circular power law of
Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_power_law}:
\begin{equation}
    \kappab_0(\theta) \;=\; \frac{3-\gp}{2}
    \left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{\gp-1},
    \qquad
    \psiL_0(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\thetaE^{\,\gp-1}\,\theta^{\,3-\gp}}{3-\gp},
    \qquad
    \alpha_0(\theta) \;=\; \thetaE^{\,\gp-1}\,\theta^{\,2-\gp},
    \label{eq:mu_monopole}
\end{equation}
valid for $1<\gp<3$.  One checks directly that $\nabla^2\psiL_0 =
2\kappab_0$, that $\alpha_0 = \psiL_0'$, and that $\alpha_0(\thetaE) =
\thetaE$, so $\thetaE$ is the Einstein radius.  The deflection also
follows from the enclosed-mass formula of
Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}, $\alpha_0 = (2/\theta)\int_0^\theta
\kappab_0\,\theta'\,\dd\theta'$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_enclosed_mass} in
dimensionless form).

\subsection{A single multipole on the power-law base}
\label{sec:mu_single}

The simplest non-circular extension multiplies the monopole by a small
angular modulation.  A single order $m$ with the same radial
run as the base is
\begin{equation}
    \kappab_m(\theta,\varphi)
    \;=\; \frac{k_m}{2}\left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{\gp-1}
    \cos\!\big[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)\big],
    \label{eq:mu_kappam}
\end{equation}
where the multipole shares the centre, $\thetaE$ and $\gp$ of the
monopole.  The physically meaningful amplitude is the \emph{peak
fractional convergence perturbation},
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    a_m \;\equiv\; \frac{\max_\varphi|\kappab_m|}{\kappab_0}
    \;=\; \frac{k_m}{3-\gp}
    \;}
    \label{eq:mu_am}
\end{equation}
which is independent of radius, because $\kappab_m$ and
$\kappab_0$ both scale as $\theta^{-(\gp-1)}$.  The total convergence
is therefore
$\kappab = \kappab_0(\theta)\,\{1 + a_m\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]\}$:
$a_m = 0.01$ means a 1\% azimuthal modulation of the surface density at
every radius.  This is an intrinsic departure from the base
shape, not the $m=4$ content that an ellipse itself carries; the latter
is what \S\ref{sec:which_multipoles} estimated to matter only for
markedly flattened lenses ($q\lesssim0.75$).

\begin{annotation}[Why two amplitudes?]
$k_m$ is the coefficient that makes the potential and deflection
formulae of the next section tidy; $a_m$ is the coefficient with a
direct physical meaning.  They differ by the factor $(3-\gp)$ that sits
in front of the monopole in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_monopole}.  At isothermal
$\gp=2$ the factor is unity and the two coincide; at the shallow slopes
$\gp=1.4$--$1.5$ used as examples in this chapter (shallow enough,
$\gp<2$, for a radial critical curve and central images to exist,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_theta_r} and Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass}) it
is $1.6$--$1.5$.  Section~\ref{sec:mu_convention} explains what goes
wrong when they are conflated.
\end{annotation}

\section{Potential and Deflection in Closed Form}
\label{sec:mu_potential}

\subsection{Solving the Poisson equation mode by mode}

Because the polar Laplacian does not mix different azimuthal orders,
we can solve $\nabla^2\psiL_m = 2\kappab_m$ one order at a time with
the separable ansatz $\psiL_m = A\,\theta^{p}\cos m\Delta\varphi$,
$\Delta\varphi\equiv\varphi-\varphi_m$.  The identity needed is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2\!\left(\theta^{p}\cos m\Delta\varphi\right)
    \;=\; (p^2 - m^2)\,\theta^{p-2}\cos m\Delta\varphi .
    \label{eq:mu_lap_identity}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[The separable Laplacian and the matching conditions]
Apply the three pieces of the polar Laplacian to $\theta^p\cos m\Delta\varphi$:
$\partial_\theta^2$ gives $p(p-1)\theta^{p-2}\cos m\Delta\varphi$;
$\theta^{-1}\partial_\theta$ gives $p\,\theta^{p-2}\cos m\Delta\varphi$;
$\theta^{-2}\partial_\varphi^2$ gives $-m^2\theta^{p-2}\cos m\Delta\varphi$.
The sum is $[p(p-1)+p-m^2]\,\theta^{p-2}\cos m\Delta\varphi
=(p^2-m^2)\,\theta^{p-2}\cos m\Delta\varphi$, which is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_lap_identity}.

Now demand $\nabla^2\psiL_m = 2\kappab_m = k_m\,\thetaE^{\gp-1}\,
\theta^{1-\gp}\cos m\Delta\varphi$.
The powers of $\theta$ must match, $\theta^{p-2} = \theta^{1-\gp}$,
which forces $p = 3-\gp$; both sides carry $\cos m\Delta\varphi$, so no
other harmonic is generated; and the amplitudes must match,
$A\,(p^2-m^2) = k_m\thetaE^{\gp-1}$, i.e.\
$A = -k_m\thetaE^{\gp-1}/[m^2-(3-\gp)^2]$.
Any solution of the homogeneous equation $\nabla^2\psiL=0$ could be
added; with the same angular dependence these are $\theta^{\pm m}\cos
m\Delta\varphi$, the internal and external multipoles of
\S\ref{sec:int_ext_multipoles}.  They carry no convergence and we set
them to zero: the growing ones belong to the environment (for $m=2$,
external shear), the decaying ones to mass not described by our
$\kappab_m$.
\end{derivation}

The result is the closed-form multipole potential on a power-law base:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \psiL_m(\theta,\varphi) \;=\;
    -\,\frac{k_m\,\thetaE^{\,\gp-1}}{m^{2}-(3-\gp)^{2}}\;
    \theta^{\,3-\gp}\cos\!\big[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)\big] .
    \;}
    \label{eq:mu_psim}
\end{equation}
Its gradient gives the deflection in the polar frame,
$\vecalpha_m = (\partial_\theta\psiL_m,\ \theta^{-1}\partial_\varphi\psiL_m)$:
\begin{align}
    \alpha_{m,\theta} &=
    -\,\frac{(3-\gp)\,k_m\,\thetaE^{\,\gp-1}\,\theta^{\,2-\gp}}{m^{2}-(3-\gp)^{2}}\,
    \cos\!\big[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)\big],
    \label{eq:mu_alphar}\\[3pt]
    \alpha_{m,\varphi} &=
    +\,\frac{m\,k_m\,\thetaE^{\,\gp-1}\,\theta^{\,2-\gp}}{m^{2}-(3-\gp)^{2}}\,
    \sin\!\big[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)\big].
    \label{eq:mu_alphaphi}
\end{align}
Only the radial component carries the factor $(3-\gp)$, only the
tangential one the factor $m$; at high $m$ the perturbation is
predominantly tangential.  As a sanity check, setting $m=0$ and
$k_0 = 3-\gp$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_psim} returns the monopole
potential $\psiL_0$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_monopole}.

\begin{annotation}[Sign of the potential]
For $m^2>(3-\gp)^2$ (every $m\ge2$, and $m=1$ only when $\gp>2$) the
prefactor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_psim} is negative: the potential is
\emph{lowest} where the convergence is \emph{highest}.  That looks
backwards if one thinks of $\psiL$ as a projected gravitational
potential, but it is just the statement that $\nabla^2\psiL_m =
2\kappab_m$ for a rapidly oscillating function: the angular part of
the Laplacian returns $-m^2$ times the function, and for large $m$ it
dominates the radial terms.  The case $m^2<(3-\gp)^2$ is the subject
of \S\ref{sec:mu_dipole}.
\end{annotation}

\subsection{The isothermal limit and Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}}
\label{sec:mu_isothermal_limit}

At $\gp=2$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_psim} becomes
$\psiL_m = A\,\theta\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]$ with
$A = -k_m\thetaE/(m^2-1)$.  This is precisely the isothermal-scaling
potential multipole of \S\ref{sec:multipole_series}; inserting this $A$
into Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_multipole} gives
$\kappab = A(1-m^2)/(2\theta)\cos[\ldots] = \tfrac12 k_m(\thetaE/\theta)
\cos[\ldots]$, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_kappam} at $\gp=2$.  Since
$k_m = a_m$ at $\gp=2$, the isothermal potential amplitude of
Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models} (call it $A_m$ to avoid the clash)
and the fractional convergence amplitude of this chapter are related by
\begin{equation}
    A_m \;=\; -\,\frac{\thetaE\,a_m}{m^2-1}
    \qquad(\gp=2).
    \label{eq:mu_iso_dictionary}
\end{equation}

\subsection{A radius-independent perturbation}
\label{sec:mu_ratio}

Dividing the magnitude $|\vecalpha_m| = (\alpha_{m,\theta}^2 +
\alpha_{m,\varphi}^2)^{1/2}$ by the monopole deflection, the common
factor $\thetaE^{\gp-1}\theta^{2-\gp}$ cancels:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{|\vecalpha_m|}{\alpha_0}
    = \frac{k_m\sqrt{(3-\gp)^{2}\cos^{2}m\Delta\varphi + m^{2}\sin^{2}m\Delta\varphi}}
           {|m^{2}-(3-\gp)^{2}|},
    \qquad
    \max_\varphi\frac{|\vecalpha_m|}{\alpha_0} = \frac{m\,k_m}{m^{2}-(3-\gp)^{2}}
    \quad (m > 3-\gp).
    \label{eq:mu_alpharatio}
\end{equation}
The \emph{fractional} deflection perturbation is the same at every
radius; the \emph{absolute} perturbation grows outward as
$\theta^{2-\gp}$ for the shallow slopes $\gp<2$.  This will matter in
\S\ref{sec:mu_residuals}.  For $a_m = 0.01$ on a $\gp=1.5$ base,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_alpharatio} gives a peak of $0.67\%$ ($m=3$) and
$0.44\%$ ($m=4$) of the monopole deflection --- about $6.7$ and $4.4$
milliarcseconds for $\thetaE=1''$.

\begin{annotation}[Why higher orders bend light less]
At fixed $k_m$ and large $m$ the peak ratio tends to $k_m/m$: the
deflection response to a \emph{fixed} convergence amplitude falls as
$1/m$.  The Poisson equation suppresses the potential of an angular
wavenumber-$m$ mode by $1/(m^2-p^2)$ relative to its convergence, and
taking the gradient restores only one power of $m$.  Physically, rapid
azimuthal oscillations of $\kappab$ largely cancel in the integral that
generates the potential.  Hence at the same $a_m$, an $m=3$ term
perturbs the deflection more than $m=4$ --- by a factor $1.565$ at
$\gp=1.4$ and $1.528$ at $\gp=1.5$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:mu_potential}).
\end{annotation}

\section{Amplitude Bookkeeping}
\label{sec:mu_convention}

\subsection{The convention trap: \texorpdfstring{$k_m$}{k\_m} is not \texorpdfstring{$a_m$}{a\_m}}

Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_am},
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\; k_m \;=\; a_m\,(3-\gp) \;}
    \label{eq:mu_km_am}
\end{equation}
A multipole can be stored as two Cartesian-like
components $(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2)$ with
\begin{equation}
    (\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2) \;=\; k_m\,\big(\cos m\varphi_m,\ \sin m\varphi_m\big),
    \qquad
    k_m = \sqrt{\epsilon_1^2+\epsilon_2^2},
    \qquad
    \varphi_m = \tfrac1m\operatorname{atan2}(\epsilon_2,\epsilon_1),
    \label{eq:mu_components}
\end{equation}
so that $\epsilon_1\cos m\varphi + \epsilon_2\sin m\varphi =
k_m\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]$ is linear in the sampled parameters.
Two factors hide here: the magnitude is $k_m$, not $a_m$; and the
angle carries a factor $1/m$, so $\varphi_m$ is defined only modulo
$2\pi/m$ (and a shift by $\pi/m$ is equivalent to flipping the sign of
$k_m$).

\begin{keybox}[Amplitude conventions are not interchangeable]
\textbf{Always derive the fractional density amplitude from the
code's own definition} before comparing to a published value or
injecting a ``1\% multipole''; Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mu_kappam},
\eqref{eq:mu_am} and~\eqref{eq:mu_components} are one convention among
several.  Check the angle too: if a code orders the components the
other way, $(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2)=k_m(\sin m\varphi_m,\cos
m\varphi_m)$, the same two numbers put the mode at $90^\circ/m -
\varphi_m$ instead of $\varphi_m$.  Evaluate the code's own convergence
and locate its peak.  For the circular
power-law form used here, $k_m = a_m(3-\gp)$; taking a component
magnitude of $0.01$ to mean $a_m=0.01$ actually injects
$a_m = 0.01/(3-\gp)$, which is $33\%$ low at $\gp=1.5$ and $37.5\%$ low
at $\gp=1.4$.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{From fractional amplitude to isophotal deviation}
\label{sec:mu_isophote}

What does $a_m$ mean geometrically?  Suppose the isodensity contours
are perturbed circles,
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\rm iso}(\varphi) \;=\; \theta_0\big[1 + \varepsilon_m\cos m\Delta\varphi\big],
    \label{eq:mu_iso_contour}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon_m$ the fractional \emph{contour} deviation (for
isophotes, the quantity a photometric fit of Fourier modes to the
contour radius returns).  A convergence
that is constant along these contours and has the power-law radial run
is a function of the ``shape radius''
$\theta/[1+\varepsilon_m\cos m\Delta\varphi]$:
\begin{equation}
    \kappab(\theta,\varphi)
    = C\left[\frac{\theta}{1+\varepsilon_m\cos m\Delta\varphi}\right]^{-(\gp-1)}
    = \kappab_0(\theta)\big[1+\varepsilon_m\cos m\Delta\varphi\big]^{\gp-1}
    \simeq \kappab_0(\theta)\big[1 + (\gp-1)\,\varepsilon_m\cos m\Delta\varphi\big],
    \label{eq:mu_iso_expand}
\end{equation}
to first order in $\varepsilon_m$ (binomial expansion of $(1+x)^{\gp-1}$;
the first neglected term is $\tfrac12(\gp-1)(\gp-2)\varepsilon_m^2\cos^2
m\Delta\varphi$).  Comparing with Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_am}:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\; a_m \;=\; (\gp-1)\,\varepsilon_m \;}
    \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
    \varepsilon_m = \frac{a_m}{\gp-1}.
    \label{eq:mu_am_eps}
\end{equation}
At isothermal $\gp=2$ the two coincide.  At shallow slopes a given
convergence multipole demands a \emph{larger} contour distortion:
$a_m = 0.01$ needs $\varepsilon_m = 2.5\%$ at $\gp=1.4$ and $2.0\%$ at
$\gp=1.5$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:mu_am_eps} is the ellipticity-equivalent
translator: it tells you how ``boxy'' a lens must look for a given
lensing amplitude.  It is first order, for circular base contours and
a radially constant amplitude; real multipole amplitudes vary with
radius and sit on elliptical contours, so use it as an
order-of-magnitude translator, not a calibration.

\begin{annotation}[Two different factors --- do not confuse them]
$k_m/a_m = 3-\gp$ is a \emph{normalisation} factor (it comes from the
monopole's prefactor); $\varepsilon_m/a_m = 1/(\gp-1)$ is a
\emph{geometric} factor (it comes from the logarithmic slope of
$\kappab$).  They agree only at $\gp=2$, where both are unity.  At
$\gp=1.4$: $k_m = 1.6\,a_m$ but $\varepsilon_m = 2.5\,a_m$.
\end{annotation}

\section{The Structural Fact: a Multipole Encloses No Mass}
\label{sec:mu_zero_mass}

\begin{theorem}[Zero enclosed mass]
\label{thm:mu_zero_mass}
For every integer $m\ge1$, every orientation $\varphi_m$ and every
radius $\theta$, the multipole Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_kappam} contributes
nothing to the projected mass enclosed within $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
    \delta M_m(<\theta) \;\propto\;
    \int_0^{\theta}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\kappab_m(\theta',\varphi)\,
    \dd\varphi\;\theta'\,\dd\theta' \;=\; 0 .
    \label{eq:mu_zero_mass}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the azimuthal average of its radial deflection vanishes
on every circle, $\oint\alpha_{m,\theta}\,\theta\,\dd\varphi = 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Do the $\varphi$-integral first:
$\int_0^{2\pi}\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]\,\dd\varphi =
\tfrac1m\big[\sin m(\varphi-\varphi_m)\big]_0^{2\pi} = 0$, because $2\pi$
is an integer multiple of the period $2\pi/m$.  The radial integral that
remains, $\int_0^\theta\theta'^{\,2-\gp}\,\dd\theta'$, is finite for
$\gp<3$, so the product is zero.  For the second form, apply the
two-dimensional divergence theorem to $\vecalpha=\nabla\psiL$ on the
disc of radius $\theta$:
$\oint\vecalpha\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol n}\,\theta\,\dd\varphi =
\int\nabla^2\psiL\,\dd A = 2\int\kappab\,\dd A$; the right-hand side is
proportional to $\delta M_m$.
\end{proof}

A central point mass is the opposite extreme.  Its deflection
$\thetaBH^2/\theta$ is independent of $\varphi$, so all its Fourier
content is in $m=0$; it changes $M(<\theta)$ by the same amount at
every radius and changes nothing else.  Distinct multipole orders are
also mutually orthogonal, for any orientations:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{2\pi}\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]\cos[n(\varphi-\varphi_n)]\,\dd\varphi
    \;=\; \pi\,\delta_{mn}\cos[m(\varphi_m-\varphi_n)]
    \qquad(m,n\ge1).
    \label{eq:mu_orthogonality}
\end{equation}

\begin{keybox}[Orthogonal sectors of the convergence]
A multipole of order $m\ge1$ carries \textbf{zero enclosed mass at
every radius} (Theorem~\ref{thm:mu_zero_mass}); a central point mass is
a \textbf{pure monopole}.  The two are exactly orthogonal in the space
of convergence fields, and so are different multipole orders.  A
multipole leaves the mass inside the Einstein ring unchanged, and a
central point mass leaves the contour shapes unchanged.
\end{keybox}

\begin{bridge}[Back to Chapter~\ref{ch:axisymmetric_models}: the deflection knows only the enclosed mass]
For an axisymmetric lens, Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_enclosed_mass} says that
the deflection at $\theta$ is set entirely by $M(<\theta)$ --- the
two-dimensional shell theorem.  Theorem~\ref{thm:mu_zero_mass} is the
non-axisymmetric generalisation: the \emph{azimuthally averaged} radial
deflection is still set by $M(<\theta)$ alone, and a multipole leaves
that average untouched.  Everything a multipole does to the images
lives in the angular variation of $\vecalpha$ about its mean.
\end{bridge}

\section{Degenerate in the Likelihood, not in \texorpdfstring{$\kappab$}{kappa}}
\label{sec:mu_likelihood}

Orthogonality holds under an integral over the \emph{full} circle.  A
likelihood is a sum over the pixels that actually contain lensed
light, and those occupy a few localised arcs.  Over a partial arc, two
orthogonal functions generally have a nonzero overlap.

The simplest model of the effect: suppose the data contain a residual
$A\cos m\varphi$ (from an omitted multipole) over an arc
$|\varphi|<\Delta$, and the model has only a $\varphi$-independent
template available (as any monopole perturbation is).  The
least-squares amplitude the fit assigns to the template is
\begin{equation}
    \hat A_0 \;=\; \frac{\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}A\cos m\varphi\,\dd\varphi}
                       {\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\dd\varphi}
    \;=\; A\,\frac{\sin m\Delta}{m\Delta},
    \label{eq:mu_arc_overlap}
\end{equation}
which vanishes for the full circle ($\Delta=\pi$) but is of order unity
for short arcs: $2/\pi\simeq0.64$ for an $m=4$ residual seen over a
$45^\circ$ arc ($\Delta=\pi/8$), and $0.41$ over $60^\circ$.  A model
with \emph{no} angular freedom will therefore buy whatever $\chi^2$
reduction its available parameters can deliver, and a central point
mass is one such parameter.  That is a \textbf{specification error},
not a physical degeneracy.  Conversely, because the two perturbations
are orthogonal in $\kappab$, once the multipole orders actually
present are \emph{in} the model
the angular signal has a legitimate home: one expects free multipoles
neither to absorb a genuine central mass nor to leave room for a
spurious one.  Before quoting an evidence ratio for a central mass as a
detection, check that the winning model fits the data to the noise
level.

\begin{annotation}[How to read an evidence ratio in this situation]
When the angular structure is omitted, a model with an extra central
mass will typically win a Bayesian evidence comparison against one
without --- but both models are wrong, and the evidence ratio only says
which is \emph{less} wrong.  Before quoting such a ratio as a
detection, check that the winning model actually fits the data to the
noise level.  If adding the multipoles produces a good fit and the
central mass then \emph{costs} evidence by about the size of an Occam
penalty (the log of the ratio of its prior width to its posterior
width), the original ``detection'' was the angular structure in
disguise.
\end{annotation}

\section{Response of the Tangential Critical Curve}
\label{sec:mu_critical}

The tangential critical curve is the most directly observed feature of
a lens --- the Einstein ring traces it.  How does a small multipole
deform it?

For the circular base the tangential eigenvalue of the Jacobian
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalues}) is $\lambda_t = 1-\alpha_0/\theta$, which
vanishes at $\thetaE$; the radial eigenvalue there is
$\lambda_r = 1-\alpha_0' = \gp-1 > 0$.  Write the critical curve as
$\theta_c(\varphi) = \thetaE[1+\delta(\varphi)]$ and expand
$\det\Amat=0$ to first order in $a_m$.

\begin{derivation}[First-order critical-curve shift]
In the orthonormal polar frame the Hessian of $\psiL$ has components
\[
    \psiL_{\theta\theta} = \partial_\theta^2\psiL,\qquad
    \psiL_{\varphi\varphi} = \frac{\partial_\theta\psiL}{\theta}
                          + \frac{\partial_\varphi^2\psiL}{\theta^2},\qquad
    \psiL_{\theta\varphi} = \frac{\partial_\theta\partial_\varphi\psiL}{\theta}
                          - \frac{\partial_\varphi\psiL}{\theta^2},
\]
and $\det\Amat = (1-\psiL_{\theta\theta})(1-\psiL_{\varphi\varphi})
- \psiL_{\theta\varphi}^2$ (the determinant is rotation invariant; the
companion script checks the polar formula against the Cartesian
Hessian).  For the monopole alone, $1-\psiL_{\varphi\varphi} =
\lambda_t$ and $\psiL_{\theta\varphi}=0$.  Now add $\psiL_m$.  The term
$\psiL_{\theta\varphi}^2$ is second order in $a_m$ and is dropped.  The
factor $1-\psiL_{\theta\theta}$ is $\gp-1+\order{a_m}$, and it
multiplies $1-\psiL_{\varphi\varphi}$, which is itself first order on
the perturbed curve, so to first order only $\gp-1$ survives.
Expanding about $\thetaE$,
$1-\psiL_{\varphi\varphi} \simeq (\gp-1)\,\delta
- \big[\partial_\theta\psiL_m/\theta + \partial_\varphi^2\psiL_m/\theta^2\big]_{\thetaE}$,
using $\partial_\theta(1-\alpha_0/\theta)|_{\thetaE} = (\gp-1)/\thetaE$.
With Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_psim} the bracket is
$k_m\,[m^2-(3-\gp)]\cos m\Delta\varphi/[m^2-(3-\gp)^2]$.
Setting $1-\psiL_{\varphi\varphi}=0$ gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_crit_shift}.
\end{derivation}

The result is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta\theta_c}{\thetaE} \;=\;
    \frac{k_m\,[m^2-(3-\gp)]}{(\gp-1)\,[m^2-(3-\gp)^2]}\,\cos m\Delta\varphi
    \;=\;
    a_m\,\frac{(3-\gp)\,(m^2-3+\gp)}{(\gp-1)\,[m^2-(3-\gp)^2]}\,\cos m\Delta\varphi .
    \label{eq:mu_crit_shift}
\end{equation}
Three readings follow.  At $\gp=2$ the coefficient is exactly $1$ for
every $m$: $\delta\theta_c = a_m\thetaE\cos m\Delta\varphi =
\varepsilon_m\thetaE\cos m\Delta\varphi$.  For an isothermal lens the
tangential critical curve is the $\kappab=\tfrac12$ isodensity contour,
so it inherits the contour shape exactly (compare
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_am_eps} at $\gp=2$).  Shallow slopes amplify the
response, since the factor $1/(\gp-1)$ is the inverse of the radial
eigenvalue: a shallow profile has a slowly varying $\lambda_t$ near the
ring, so a given perturbation moves its zero further.  For $m=4$ at
$\gp=1.5$ the coefficient is $3.16$; a 1\% multipole moves the critical
curve by $\pm3.2\%$ of $\thetaE$.  Finally, the shift averages to zero
over $\varphi$, so the area inside the critical curve is unchanged at
first order, by the same integral $\oint\cos m\Delta\varphi\,\dd\varphi=0$
that proves Theorem~\ref{thm:mu_zero_mass}.
A central point mass added to a fixed base does the opposite:
it expands the critical curve uniformly, $\delta\theta_c/\thetaE =
f_\bullet/(\gp-1)$, with no angular dependence.  If instead the base
normalisation is reduced so that the Einstein ring stays where the data
put it, the tangential critical curve does not move at all.
Figure~\ref{fig:mu_kappa4} shows the $m=4$ convergence pattern and the
corresponding first-order critical curve.

\begin{bridge}[Back to Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptical_models}: critical curves]
\S\ref{sec:critical_curves} defined critical curves as the zeros of
$\det\Amat$ and showed how ellipticity turns the SIS ring into an
oval.  Equation~\eqref{eq:mu_crit_shift} is the perturbative version
of the same calculation for an arbitrary harmonic and slope, and it
reduces to a simple picture at $\gp=2$: the tangential critical curve
of an isothermal lens is a contour of constant convergence.
\end{bridge}

\section{The Residual Diagnostic: Inner versus Outer}
\label{sec:mu_residuals}

We can now say where in the image plane each perturbation shows
up.  The data fix the Einstein ring very precisely, so any viable model
must reproduce it; a fair comparison holds $\thetaE$ fixed.

\subsection{The central point mass}

Add a point mass of fraction $f_\bullet$ and reduce the base
normalisation by the factor $(1-f_\bullet)$ so that the total
deflection at $\thetaE$ is still $\thetaE$.  The change in deflection
is
\begin{equation}
    \delta\alpha_\bullet(\theta)
    = f_\bullet\,\thetaE\left[\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}
      - \left(\frac{\theta}{\thetaE}\right)^{2-\gp}\right],
    \qquad
    \frac{\delta\alpha_\bullet}{\alpha_0}
    = f_\bullet\left[\left(\frac{\thetaE}{\theta}\right)^{3-\gp} - 1\right].
    \label{eq:mu_res_pm}
\end{equation}
It vanishes on the ring, is positive inside and negative outside, and
grows inward as $(\thetaE/\theta)^{3-\gp}$.  Near the ring, though, it
is linear in $\ln(\theta/\thetaE)$:
$\delta\alpha_\bullet/\alpha_0 \simeq -f_\bullet(3-\gp)\ln(\theta/\thetaE)$.
A change of the slope at fixed $\thetaE$ produces exactly this shape,
$\partial\alpha_0/\partial\gp = -\alpha_0\ln(\theta/\thetaE)$.  Any
realistic fit lets the slope float, so re-fit it with
$\delta\gp = -f_\bullet(3-\gp)$ (the base becomes slightly shallower to
compensate for the added central concentration).  What remains is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta\alpha_\bullet}{\alpha_0}\bigg|_{\rm ring+slope}
    = f_\bullet\big[u^{p} - 1 - p\ln u\big],
    \qquad u\equiv\frac{\thetaE}{\theta},\quad p\equiv 3-\gp,
    \label{eq:mu_res_refit}
\end{equation}
which is second order in $\ln(\theta/\thetaE)$ near the ring
(it starts as $\tfrac12 f_\bullet p^2\ln^2 u$) and grows as
$f_\bullet u^p$ inside.  The point-mass signature that survives a
reasonable fit is concentrated in the inner region.  A convenient
reference radius is the radial critical curve of the base,
\begin{equation}
    \theta_r = \thetaE\,(2-\gp)^{1/(\gp-1)}
    \qquad(1<\gp<2),
    \label{eq:mu_theta_r}
\end{equation}
where $1-\alpha_0'=0$ and near which radial and central images form;
$\theta_r = 0.25\,\thetaE$ for $\gp=1.5$.  A point mass can remove
this curve altogether: at $\gp=1.5$ any $f_\bullet > 27/4096 \approx
0.0066$ does so (Chapter~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass},
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pl_mcrit}), and the inner residual then falls on the
central and point-mass-induced images instead.  The radial ordering
below does not depend on which.

\subsection{The multipole}

In $\kappab$, the multipole cannot be absorbed by $\thetaE$, $\gp$ or
any other monopole parameter, because it averages to zero on every
circle (Theorem~\ref{thm:mu_zero_mass}).  A fit sees only the arcs, so
the absorption is limited to the partial-arc overlap of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_arc_overlap}, and it disappears once the multipole is
modelled.  Its fractional deflection is
radius-independent (Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_alpharatio}), so its absolute
deflection is $\propto\theta^{2-\gp}$ and is largest where the
tangential arcs are, near and outside $\thetaE$.  Its ratio between two
radii is $(\theta_{\rm in}/\theta_{\rm out})^{2-\gp}$.

\subsection{A worked comparison}

Take $\gp=1.5$, a central point mass with $f_\bullet=0.01$ (ring and
slope matched) and an $m=4$ multipole with $a_4=0.01$ (peak over
$\varphi$).  Evaluate $|\delta\alpha|/\thetaE$ at the base's radial
critical curve $\theta_r = 0.25\,\thetaE$ (this $f_\bullet$ exceeds
$27/4096$, so the composite lens itself has no radial critical curve)
and in the arc region at $1.2\,\thetaE$.  This is a linearised toy
(source fixed, first order in $f_\bullet$ and $a_m$); the numbers
illustrate the radial ordering, not any particular lens.

\begin{keybox}[The residual-map discriminator]
The two hypotheses misfit in opposite places and with opposite
angular structure:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item an omitted central mass leaves an inner, azimuthally uniform
          ($m=0$) residual, in the inner image region (at the radial
          critical curve if the lens still has one, otherwise at the
          central images);
    \item omitted azimuthal structure leaves an outer residual with a
          $\cos m\varphi$ pattern and zero azimuthal mean, in the
          tangential arcs.
\end{itemize}
A global $\chi^2$ cannot tell the two apart; the residual map, split
at a radius between $\theta_r$ and $\thetaE$ and examined for its
angular pattern, can.  A ``central mass'' whose inclusion mainly
improves the outer residual is not a central-mass signal.
\end{keybox}

\begin{annotation}[What this toy leaves out]
The comparison uses deflection perturbations at a few radii, holds the
source fixed and linearises in $f_\bullet$ and $a_m$.  In a real fit
the source position and structure also adjust, the residual is
measured in image-plane surface brightness (so it is weighted by the
local magnification and by the source's brightness gradients), and the
lens light, shear and ellipticity have their own freedom.  The
inner/outer split survives because it rests on two exact facts, the
multipole's zero azimuthal mean and the point mass's
$(\thetaE/\theta)^{3-\gp}$ growth, but the specific numbers in the
table are illustrations, not predictions for any particular lens.
\end{annotation}

\begin{bridge}[Forward to Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}]
The diagnostic of this section is the azimuthal counterpart of the
radial-profile question in Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}: there a
power law fitted without the central mass compensates by changing its
slope, and the compensation biases the inferred distance ratio; here
the question is whether omitted angular structure can masquerade as a
central mass.  In both cases a mis-specified model can be fooled, and
the residuals of a correctly specified one separate the two.
\end{bridge}

\section{The Dipole Resonance}
\label{sec:mu_dipole}

The denominator $m^2-(3-\gp)^2$ in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mu_psim}--\eqref{eq:mu_alphaphi} vanishes when
$m = 3-\gp$.  For integer $m$ and $1<\gp<3$ the only case is $m=1$,
$\gp=2$ (isothermal).  There the trial function
$\theta^{3-\gp}\cos\Delta\varphi = \theta\cos\Delta\varphi$ is a
linear function of position, hence harmonic ($\nabla^2=0$, zero
convergence), and its gradient is a uniform deflection.  A
uniform deflection translates the source plane and is
unobservable \citep[][\S4.1.3]{congdon_principles_2018}.  The divergent
amplitude is the usual signature of driving a homogeneous solution:
no finite potential of this power-law form produces an $m=1$
convergence at $\gp=2$.

\begin{remark}[The dipole is poorly conditioned]
For $\gp<2$ the $m=1$ mode is finite, but its potential has the
opposite sign to the $m\ge2$ modes and its response is large:
$m^2-(3-\gp)^2 = -1.25$ at $\gp=1.5$ and $-0.21$ at $\gp=1.9$.  Much
of that response is a nearly uniform deflection, i.e.\ nearly a source
shift.  Lopsidedness is therefore weakly constrained for
near-isothermal lenses, in line with the remark in
\S\ref{sec:which_multipoles} that the dipole is nearly degenerate with
the source position.  A fit that includes $m=1$ should expect strong
correlations with the source position.
\end{remark}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:mu_summary}

A single multipole on a power-law base,
$\kappab_m = \tfrac12 k_m(\thetaE/\theta)^{\gp-1}
\cos[m(\varphi-\varphi_m)]$, has the closed-form potential
$\psiL_m = -k_m\thetaE^{\gp-1}\theta^{3-\gp}\cos m\Delta\varphi/
[m^2-(3-\gp)^2]$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_psim}); its deflection is
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mu_alphar}--\eqref{eq:mu_alphaphi}, and its fractional
deflection is independent of radius.  The fractional density amplitude
is $a_m = k_m/(3-\gp)$ and the isophotal deviation is
$\varepsilon_m = a_m/(\gp-1)$; $a_m$ must always be derived from a
code's own amplitude definition.

A multipole with $m\ge1$ encloses zero mass at every radius, whereas a
central point mass is a pure monopole.  They are orthogonal in
$\kappab$ but not in a likelihood evaluated on partial arcs (overlap
$\sin m\Delta/(m\Delta)$), so an omitted multipole can masquerade as a
central mass, a specification error that disappears when the multipole
is modelled.  To first order the tangential critical curve moves by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_crit_shift}: $\delta\theta_c = a_m\thetaE\cos
m\Delta\varphi$ at $\gp=2$, amplified by $\sim1/(\gp-1)$ at shallow
slopes, with zero azimuthal mean.  The residual diagnostic is that an
omitted central mass misfits the inner region with an azimuthally
uniform residual, while an omitted multipole misfits the outer arcs
with a $\cos m\varphi$ residual.  The $m=1$, $\gp=2$ case is a
resonance with the source translation, and the dipole is poorly
constrained near isothermal.

\section{Problems}
\label{sec:mu_problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mu_potential}
\textbf{(Potential, deflection and response.)}
Verify that $\psiL_m$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_psim} satisfies
$\nabla^2\psiL_m = 2\kappab_m$ with $\kappab_m$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_kappam}, and that its gradient reproduces
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mu_alphar}--\eqref{eq:mu_alphaphi}.  Show that
$|\vecalpha_m|/\alpha_0$ is independent of $\theta$ and evaluate its
maximum, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_alpharatio}, for $m=3$ and $m=4$ at
$\gp=1.40$ and $1.50$ with $a_m=0.01$.  Which order perturbs the
deflection more at fixed $a_m$, and how does the response scale with
$m$ at large $m$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mu_isophote}
\textbf{(Isophotal translation.)}
Derive $a_m=(\gp-1)\varepsilon_m$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_am_eps}, for
contours $\theta_{\rm iso}=\theta_0[1+\varepsilon_m\cos m\Delta\varphi]$,
stating where you linearise and giving the first neglected term.
Evaluate $\varepsilon_m$ for $a_m\in\{0.01,0.03\}$ at
$\gp\in\{1.40,1.50,2.00\}$.  Which regime demands the larger
geometric distortion for the same lensing signal?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mu_zero_mass}
\textbf{(Zero enclosed mass and partial arcs.)}
(a) Prove Theorem~\ref{thm:mu_zero_mass} and the orthogonality
relation Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_orthogonality}.  (b) Show that the point-mass
deflection has no Fourier content at $m\ge1$.  (c) Derive
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_arc_overlap} and evaluate the absorbed fraction for an
$m=4$ residual over arcs of half-width $\Delta = \pi/8$, $\pi/6$,
$\pi/4$ and $\pi$.  Use the result to explain how a model without
angular freedom can ``detect'' a central mass that is not there.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mu_critical}
\textbf{(Critical-curve shift.)}
(a) Show that for the power-law base the eigenvalues at $\thetaE$ are
$\lambda_t=0$ and $\lambda_r=\gp-1$, and that
$\partial_\theta\lambda_t|_{\thetaE} = (\gp-1)/\thetaE$.
(b) Derive Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_crit_shift}.  (c) Evaluate its coefficient
for $m=3,4$ at $\gp=1.5$ and $2$.  (d) Show that the area enclosed by
the critical curve is unchanged at first order.  (e) Repeat for a
central point mass on a fixed base and show
$\delta\theta_c/\thetaE = f_\bullet/(\gp-1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mu_residual}
\textbf{(The inner/outer discriminator.)}
(a) Derive Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_res_pm}.  (b) Show that near the ring it
has the same shape as a slope change at fixed $\thetaE$ and that
$\delta\gp = -f_\bullet(3-\gp)$ cancels the linear term, leaving
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_res_refit}.  (c) Reproduce the numbers in the table
of \S\ref{sec:mu_residuals}.  (d) Describe the azimuthal structure of
each residual and say how you would use a residual map to tell the two
apart.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mu_convention}
\textbf{(Bookkeeping.)}
A code parameterises a multipole by the components of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_components}.  (a) A paper reports $a_4=0.01$ at
$\varphi_4=30^\circ$ for a $\gp=1.5$ lens.  What
$(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2)$ should you set?  Invert your answer to check
it.  (b) A colleague sets $|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}|=0.01$ believing that
to be ``a 1\% multipole''.  What $a_4$ did they inject at $\gp=1.5$ and
at $\gp=1.4$, and by what fraction is it low?  (c) Show that
$\varphi_m$ is defined only modulo $2\pi/m$ and that
$\varphi_m\to\varphi_m+\pi/m$ is equivalent to $k_m\to-k_m$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:mu_dipole}
\textbf{(The dipole resonance.)}
(a) Show that $m^2=(3-\gp)^2$ has the single solution $m=1$, $\gp=2$
for integer $m\ge1$ and $1<\gp<3$, and that $\theta\cos(\varphi-\varphi_1)$
is harmonic with a uniform gradient.  (b) Evaluate $m^2-(3-\gp)^2$ for
$m=1$ at $\gp=1.4$, $1.5$ and $1.9$.  (c) For $m<3-\gp$ the maximum of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu_alpharatio} is at $\cos m\Delta\varphi=\pm1$; evaluate
the peak $|\vecalpha_1|/\alpha_0$ for $a_1=0.01$ at $\gp=1.5$ and
$1.9$, and comment on what most of that deflection does to the images.
\end{exercise}
