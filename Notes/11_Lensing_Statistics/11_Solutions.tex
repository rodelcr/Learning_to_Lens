\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:point_cross}: Point-Mass Magnification Cross-Section}

(a) The total magnification of a point-mass lens is
$\mu_p(y) = (y^2+2)/\big(y\sqrt{y^2+4}\big)$, a monotonically decreasing
function of $y$.  Solving $\mu_p(y) = \mu$ for $y^2$: square both sides,
\[
    \mu^2 = \frac{(y^2+2)^2}{y^2(y^2+4)}
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \mu^2\,y^2(y^2+4) = (y^2+2)^2 .
\]
Writing $u = y^2$ and expanding gives
$\mu^2 u^2 + 4\mu^2 u = u^2 + 4u + 4$, i.e.
$(\mu^2-1)u^2 + 4(\mu^2-1)u - 4 = 0$.  The positive root is
\[
    u = y^2 = -2 + \frac{2\mu}{\sqrt{\mu^2-1}}
    = 2\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\mu^2-1}} - 1\right),
\]
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:point_ymu}.  Since $\mu_p$ decreases with $y$, the
condition $\mu_p > \mu$ is $y < y(\mu)$, so the cross-section is
$\sigma(\mu) = \pi\thetaE^2\,y^2(\mu)$. \checkmark

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:point_cross}(a), the quadratic formula step]
With $a = \mu^2-1$, $b = 4(\mu^2-1)$, $c = -4$, the discriminant is
\begin{equation*}
    b^2 - 4ac = 16(\mu^2-1)^2 + 16(\mu^2-1) = 16\,\mu^2(\mu^2-1)\,,
\end{equation*}
so $\sqrt{b^2-4ac} = 4\mu\sqrt{\mu^2-1}$ and
\begin{equation*}
    u = \frac{-4(\mu^2-1) \pm 4\mu\sqrt{\mu^2-1}}{2(\mu^2-1)}
    = -2 \pm \frac{2\mu}{\sqrt{\mu^2-1}}\,.
\end{equation*}
The lower sign gives $u < -2$, impossible for $u = y^2$, so only the upper
root survives.  Squaring both sides of $\mu_p(y) = \mu$ is safe because
both sides are positive.
\end{derivation}

(b) For $\mu \gg 1$, expand
$\mu/\sqrt{\mu^2-1} = (1-\mu^{-2})^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12\mu^{-2} + \cdots$,
so $y^2(\mu) = 2(\mu/\sqrt{\mu^2-1} - 1) \to \mu^{-2}$.  Equivalently
$\lim_{\mu\to\infty}\mu^2\,y^2(\mu) = 1$, hence $y^2 \sim 1/\mu^2$ and
$\sigma \propto \mu^{-2}$. \checkmark  Since the differential probability
is $p(\mu)\,d\mu \propto -\,(d\sigma/d\mu)\,d\mu$, this cross-section is
the integral form of the universal $p(\mu)\propto\mu^{-3}$ tail.

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:point_cross}(b), one more term and the differential law]
Binomial series: $(1-\mu^{-2})^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12\mu^{-2} +
\tfrac38\mu^{-4} + \dots$, so
$y^2(\mu) = \mu^{-2} + \tfrac34\mu^{-4} + \order{\mu^{-6}}$.  Differentiating
the leading term, $-d(y^2)/d\mu \to 2\mu^{-3}$, which is the $\mu^{-3}$ law.
At the other end, $y^2(\mu) = 1$ at $\mu = 3/\sqrt5 = 1.342$: a source on the
Einstein ring has $A = 1.34$, the threshold used throughout
Chapter~\ref{ch:microlensing}.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Cross-section is not optical depth]
$\sigma(\mu)$ is an \emph{area} (a solid angle in the source plane) belonging
to \emph{one} lens.  It is not a probability.  The probability that a random
source is magnified by more than $\mu$ is the optical depth, the sum of
these areas over all lenses along the line of sight per unit sky area
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:optical_depth}).  As long as the cross-sections do not
overlap, the shape of $\sigma(\mu)$ carries over to $\tau(>\mu)$; the
normalisation comes from the lens population and the cosmological volume.
Writing ``$P(>\mu) = y^2(\mu)$'' drops that factor.
\end{annotation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:sis_stats}: SIS Flux Ratio and Magnification}

For an SIS with $y = \beta/\thetaE < 1$ the two images have signed
magnifications $\mu_\pm = 1 \pm \thetaE/\beta = 1 \pm 1/y$.  The trailing
image ($\mu_-$) has $|\mu_-| = 1/y - 1$ (it is a saddle, negative parity).
The total magnification is
\[
    \mu = |\mu_+| + |\mu_-| = \left(1+\tfrac1y\right)
    + \left(\tfrac1y-1\right) = \frac{2}{y},
\]
and the flux ratio is
\[
    r = \frac{|\mu_+|}{|\mu_-|}
    = \frac{1+1/y}{1/y-1} = \frac{1+y}{1-y}. \quad\checkmark
\]
Since $r$ increases from $1$ (at $y=0$) to $\infty$ (at $y=1$), requiring a
flux ratio smaller than $r$ restricts the source to $y < (r-1)/(r+1) < 1$,
so the cross-section is bounded by the full multiple-imaging disk,
$\sigma \le \pi\thetaE^2$. \checkmark

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:sis_stats}, inverting the flux ratio]
From $(1+y)/(1-y) = r$: multiply out, $1 + y = r - ry$, so
$y(1+r) = r-1$ and $y = (r-1)/(r+1)$.  The cross-section in units of the
multiple-imaging disk is $\sigma/(\pi\thetaE^2) = [(r-1)/(r+1)]^2$; e.g.\
$r = 10$ gives $y < 9/11$ and $\sigma = 0.669\,\pi\thetaE^2$.  As
in Exercise~\ref{ex:magnification_high_z}, the absolute values matter: the signed magnifications sum
to $\mu_+ + \mu_- = 2$, not $2/y$.
\end{derivation}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:bias_null}: The $\beta=1$ Null of Magnification Bias}

With $N_0(>S) = A\,S^{-\beta}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mag_bias} gives
\[
    N(>S) = \frac{1}{\langle\mu\rangle}\int d\mu\; p(\mu)\,
    A\left(\frac{S}{\mu}\right)^{-\beta}
    = \frac{A\,S^{-\beta}}{\langle\mu\rangle}
      \int d\mu\; p(\mu)\,\mu^{\beta}
    = N_0(>S)\,\frac{\langle\mu^{\beta}\rangle}{\langle\mu\rangle}.
\]
At $\beta = 1$ the numerator is
$\int \mu\,p(\mu)\,d\mu = \langle\mu\rangle$ by the definition of the mean
magnification, so $N(>S) = N_0(>S)$ for \emph{any} $p(\mu)$. \checkmark

\begin{derivation}[Exercise~\ref{ex:bias_null}, the substitution]
The only step is pulling $S$ out of the power law:
$N_0(>S/\mu) = A\,(S/\mu)^{-\beta} = A\,S^{-\beta}\mu^{\beta}$, rule
$(S/\mu)^{-\beta} = S^{-\beta}\mu^{\beta}$.  For a constant $\mu$ (a single
lens, $p = \delta(\mu - \mu_0)$) the factor is $\mu_0^{\beta-1}$.  With the
high-magnification tail $p \propto \mu^{-3}$, the tail of the bias
integral is $\int^\infty \mu^{\beta-3}\,d\mu$, which equals $1/(2-\beta)$
from $\mu = 1$ and diverges as $\beta \to 2$.  This is why the main text says
the bias ``grows sharply as $\beta \to 2$''.
\end{derivation}

\begin{annotation}[Two meanings of $\beta$]
In this exercise $\beta$ is the \emph{slope of the source counts}.  It is not
the source position $\beta$ of the lens equation.  Chapter~\ref{ch:weak_lensing}
calls the same slope $\alpha$ (and there $\alpha$ is not a deflection).  The
null at slope 1 reappears there: the number-count method of breaking the
mass-sheet degeneracy has no signal for $\alpha = 1$ counts.
\end{annotation}

The physical reason is that magnification does two competing things.  It
brightens each source by $\mu$ (raising it above threshold), but it also
stretches the sky by $\mu$, diluting the surface density of sources by
$1/\mu$.  For counts with logarithmic slope $\beta = 1$ (equal flux per
logarithmic interval) these effects cancel exactly; steeper counts
($\beta>1$) are net-boosted, flatter counts ($\beta<1$) net-depleted.

\begin{bridge}[What a lens statistic constrains: Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_sensitivity}]
An optical depth such as $\tau \propto n_{\mathrm{com}}(\sigma_v/\speedoflight)^4
\times(\text{volume factor})$ mixes the cosmology with the lens population.
A change in the number density or the velocity-dispersion normalisation can
mimic a change in the volume factor, so counts constrain a degenerate
combination.  Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_sensitivity} looks at a statistic
of a different kind: the ratio of two Einstein radii in one double-source-plane
lens.  For an isothermal main lens $\sigma_v$ cancels in that ratio,
$n_{\mathrm{com}}$ never enters, and what remains is a ratio of
angular-diameter distances (corrected for the mass of the first source).  That chapter asks which direction in
cosmological-parameter space such a ratio actually pins down.
\end{bridge}
