\chapter{Cosmological Sensitivity of Compound-Lens Ratios}
\label{ch:cosmological_sensitivity}

\noindent
A double-source-plane lens (DSPL) turns the image geometry of a single
deflector into one number, the distance ratio $\Brat$ of
Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl}. That number depends on cosmology only
through angular-diameter distances, and it does not depend on the Hubble
constant at all \citep{collett_2012}. Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}
showed how a mass-model error, such as an unmodelled central mass, shifts
the inferred ratio by a fractional amount $\Delta\Brat/\Brat$. This
chapter takes the next step and asks what such a shift does to the
inferred cosmological parameters.

The obvious answer is to divide by a sensitivity,
$\Delta w=(\Delta\Brat/\Brat)/S_w$. That is a one-dimensional projection
of a multi-dimensional problem. A single DSPL constrains one combination
of $(\Omegam,w_0,w_a)$, so a bias is a \emph{direction} in parameter
space, and the number one quotes for $\Delta w$ depends on which other
parameters were held fixed. Compound lenses with more than two source
planes add a second subtlety: one inner-profile error enters every plane
at once, so the per-plane biases are \emph{coherent} and do not average
down like noise. Finally, a \emph{bias} has to be told apart from a
\emph{degeneracy}. Removing a datum from the fit does not shift the
answer; it gives up part of the constraint, and it must not be
propagated as if it were a small shift.

By the end of the chapter you should be able to
(a) compute $\Brat$ and its Jacobian for any flat dark-energy cosmology,
(b) explain why one DSPL constrains a direction in $(\Omegam,w_0,w_a)$ and
what that implies for quoted single-parameter biases,
(c) distinguish coherent from independent systematics across planes and
lenses, and
(d) tell a degeneracy from a bias.

\section{Why a Scalar Is Not Enough}
\label{sec:cs_why}

The inference chain of a galaxy-scale DSPL analysis
(Chapters~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl} and~\ref{ch:power_law_point_mass}) is
\begin{equation}
  \text{image positions}\;\longrightarrow\;
  \etamea=\frac{\thetaE^{(1)}}{\thetaE^{(2)}}
  \;\longrightarrow\;\Brat=\etamea^{\,\gp-1}
  \;\longrightarrow\;(\Omegam,w_0,w_a),
  \label{eq:cs_chain}
\end{equation}
where $\thetaE^{(i)}$ is the Einstein radius for source plane $i$ and
$\gp$ the three-dimensional density slope of a power-law deflector. The
last arrow is usually closed with a single number,
\begin{equation}
  S_w\equiv\pderiv{\ln\Brat}{w}\bigg|_{\Omegam}
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \Delta w=\frac{\Delta\Brat/\Brat}{S_w}.
  \label{eq:cs_scalar}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cs_scalar} is correct, but it answers a narrower
question than the one we want. It presumes
(i) a \emph{single} distance ratio,
(ii) all other cosmological parameters held \emph{fixed}, and
(iii) a bias small enough that a first-order expansion holds.
A real compound-lens cosmography can violate all three: it may use
several source planes, it marginalises over $\Omegam$ (and possibly over
a time-varying dark-energy equation of state), and some mass-model
failures produce excursions much larger than a linear expansion
tolerates.

What we need is the map from a \emph{vector} of per-plane ratio errors
to a \emph{displacement} in parameter space, together with the direction
along which that displacement is unconstrained.

\begin{keybox}[Notation for this chapter]
$\bm{p}=(p_1,p_2,p_3)=(\Omegam,w_0,w_a)$ is the cosmological parameter
vector; the universe is spatially flat. The deflector is at $z_d$ and the
sources at $z_1<z_2<\dots<z_N$. Index $i=2,\dots,N$ labels the ratio
$\Brat_i$ of plane~1 to plane~$i$. The quantity
$\delta_i\equiv\Delta\ln\Brat_i\simeq\Delta\Brat_i/\Brat_i$ is the
fractional error in $\Brat_i$ \emph{induced by the mass model}. It is a
systematic offset, not a measurement uncertainty.
\end{keybox}

\section{The Ratio $\Brat$ in a Dark-Energy Cosmology}
\label{sec:cs_cpl}

\subsection{The CPL equation of state}

Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances} treated dark energy with a
constant equation of state $w$, for which the fluid equation gives
$\rho\propto a^{-3(1+w)}$ (eq.~\ref{eq:rho_scaling}). To test whether
dark energy evolves, the standard two-parameter extension is the
Chevallier--Polarski--Linder (CPL) form
\citep{chevallier_polarski_2001,linder_2003}
\begin{equation}
  w(a)=w_0+w_a\,(1-a)
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  w(z)=w_0+w_a\,\frac{z}{1+z},
  \label{eq:cs_cpl_w}
\end{equation}
using $a=1/(1+z)$ (eq.~\ref{eq:redshift_scale_factor}). Here $w_0$ is the
present-day value and $w_a$ controls the evolution: $w\to w_0+w_a$ at
high redshift. The cosmological constant is $(w_0,w_a)=(-1,0)$.

With a time-dependent $w$ the fluid equation
$\dd\ln\rho/\dd\ln a=-3[1+w(a)]$ no longer integrates to a single power.
Writing $\rho_{\rm DE}(z)=\rho_{\rm DE,0}\,\zeta(z)$,
\begin{equation}
  \zeta(z)=\exp\!\left[3\int_0^z\frac{1+w(z')}{1+z'}\,\dd z'\right]
  =(1+z)^{3(1+w_0+w_a)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{3w_az}{1+z}\right).
  \label{eq:cs_zeta}
\end{equation}

\begin{derivation}[The CPL density factor]
Start from $\dd\ln\rho/\dd\ln a=-3(1+w)$ and change variable to $z$.
Since $\ln a=-\ln(1+z)$, we have $\dd\ln a=-\dd z/(1+z)$, so
$\dd\ln\rho/\dd z=3(1+w(z))/(1+z)$. Integrate from $0$ to $z$ with
$w(z')=w_0+w_a z'/(1+z')$:
\[
  \int_0^z\frac{1+w_0}{1+z'}\dd z'=(1+w_0)\ln(1+z),\qquad
  \int_0^z\frac{w_a z'}{(1+z')^2}\dd z'
  =w_a\Bigl[\ln(1+z)-\frac{z}{1+z}\Bigr],
\]
where the second integral uses $z'/(1+z')^2=1/(1+z')-1/(1+z')^2$.
Adding and multiplying by $3$ gives
$\ln\zeta=3(1+w_0+w_a)\ln(1+z)-3w_az/(1+z)$, which is
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_zeta}. Two limits are worth checking by eye:
$w_a=0$ gives $\zeta=(1+z)^{3(1+w_0)}$, the constant-$w$ result of
Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}, and $(w_0,w_a)=(-1,0)$ gives
$\zeta\equiv1$, a cosmological constant.
\end{derivation}

In a flat universe the expansion rate is then
\begin{equation}
  E^2(z)\equiv\frac{H^2(z)}{\Hubble^2}=\Omegam(1+z)^3+(1-\Omegam)\,\zeta(z),
  \label{eq:cs_E}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the concordance form, eq.~\eqref{eq:Hz_concordance}, for
$\Omegam=0.3$ and $\zeta\equiv1$, and satisfies $E(0)=1$ for every
$(\Omegam,w_0,w_a)$.

\subsection{$\Brat$ is a ratio of comoving distances}

Following Chapter~\ref{ch:multiplane_dspl},
\begin{equation}
  \Brat\equiv\frac{D_{\mathrm{d}s_1}\,D_{s_2}}{D_{s_1}\,D_{\mathrm{d}s_2}}
  =\frac{D_{\mathrm{d}s_1}/D_{s_1}}{D_{\mathrm{d}s_2}/D_{s_2}}\;<\;1 ,
  \label{eq:cs_B_def}
\end{equation}
the ratio of the lensing efficiencies of the nearer and farther source
planes. Every distance in eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_B_def} is a flat-universe
angular-diameter distance, eqs.~\eqref{eq:DA_flat} and~\eqref{eq:Dds_flat}:
$D_{s}=\chi_s/(1+z_s)$ and $D_{\mathrm{d}s}=(\chi_s-\chi_d)/(1+z_s)$, with
\begin{equation}
  \chi(z)=\frac{\speedoflight}{\Hubble}\int_0^z\frac{\dd z'}{E(z')} .
  \label{eq:cs_chi}
\end{equation}
Substituting, the $(1+z)$ factors cancel pairwise and
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Brat=\frac{1-\chi_d/\chi_1}{1-\chi_d/\chi_2}\;}
  \label{eq:cs_B_chi}
\end{equation}
Two properties follow at once. First, $\Brat$ involves only
\emph{ratios} of comoving distances, so the Hubble distance
$\speedoflight/\Hubble$ cancels: $\Brat$ is independent of $\Hubble$.
Second, $\Brat\to1$ as $z_2\to z_1$, so the cosmological signal vanishes
when the two source planes approach each other.

\begin{bridge}[Back to Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}]
Equation~\eqref{eq:cs_B_chi} rests on two facts from
Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmological_distances}: in a flat universe comoving
distances add, $\chi_{ds}=\chi_s-\chi_d$, and the angular-diameter
distance is $\chi/(1+z)$ (eqs.~\ref{eq:DA_flat} and~\ref{eq:Dds_flat}).
The remark there that $\Dds\neq\Ds-\Dd$ is exactly why
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_B_chi} has to be written in $\chi$, not in $D_A$. In a
curved universe eq.~\eqref{eq:Dds_general} replaces the simple
difference; the $(1+z)$ factors still cancel, but $\Brat$ then also
depends on $\Omega_k$.
\end{bridge}

\begin{annotation}[What $\Brat$ actually measures]
Because $\Hubble$ cancels, $\Brat$ is sensitive only to the
\emph{shape} of $\chi(z)$ between $z_d$ and $z_2$, that is, to how
$E(z)$ varies with redshift. This is why a DSPL probes $\Omegam$ and
dark energy but not the expansion rate today. It is also why the
sensitivities below are small: a modest change in $w_0$ alters the
shape of $\chi(z)$ only slightly, and the ratio of ratios in
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_B_chi} removes most of what is left.
\end{annotation}

\begin{aside}[Computing $\Brat$ in practice]
Never use a fitting formula for $\Brat$. Write the cosmology as a
callable function, integrate $1/E$ numerically, and form
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_B_chi}. The companion script does this in a few lines,
checks it against an independent $\Lambda$CDM implementation, and proves
symbolically that $\speedoflight/\Hubble$ drops out.
\end{aside}

\section{The Sensitivity Jacobian}
\label{sec:cs_jacobian}

\subsection{Definition}

For a fixed geometry $\{z_d;z_1,\dots,z_N\}$ define the
\textbf{sensitivity Jacobian}
\begin{equation}
  J_{ij}(\bm{p})\equiv\pderiv{\ln\Brat_i}{p_j}\bigg|_{\bm{p}},
  \qquad i=2,\dots,N,\quad j=1,2,3 .
  \label{eq:cs_jacobian}
\end{equation}
Each row is the response of one observable to cosmology; each column is
the imprint of one parameter across the planes. For a single DSPL, $J$ is
the row vector $(S_{\Omegam},S_{w_0},S_{w_a})$, and $S_w$ of
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_scalar} is its middle entry.

\subsection{Two ways to compute it}

\paragraph{Finite differences.} Evaluate $\ln\Brat$ at $p_j\pm h$ with
the other parameters fixed and take
$[\ln\Brat(p_j+h)-\ln\Brat(p_j-h)]/2h$. The truncation error is
$\mathcal{O}(h^2)$; combining steps $h$ and $h/2$ (Richardson
extrapolation) removes the leading term.

\paragraph{Differentiating under the integral.} From
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_chi},
\begin{equation}
  \pderiv{\chi(z)}{p_j}=-\frac{\speedoflight}{2\Hubble}\int_0^z
  \frac{1}{E^3(z')}\,\pderiv{E^2(z')}{p_j}\,\dd z' ,
  \label{eq:cs_dchi}
\end{equation}
and from eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_B_chi},
$\ln\Brat=\ln(\chi_1-\chi_d)-\ln\chi_1+\ln\chi_2-\ln(\chi_2-\chi_d)$, so
\begin{equation}
  \pderiv{\ln\Brat}{p_j}
  =\frac{\chi_1'-\chi_d'}{\chi_1-\chi_d}-\frac{\chi_1'}{\chi_1}
   +\frac{\chi_2'}{\chi_2}-\frac{\chi_2'-\chi_d'}{\chi_2-\chi_d},
  \qquad \chi'\equiv\pderiv{\chi}{p_j}.
  \label{eq:cs_dlnB}
\end{equation}
The derivatives of $E^2$ needed in eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_dchi} are elementary:
$\partial E^2/\partial\Omegam=(1+z)^3-\zeta$,
$\partial E^2/\partial w_0=3(1-\Omegam)\zeta\ln(1+z)$ and
$\partial E^2/\partial w_a=3(1-\Omegam)\zeta[\ln(1+z)-z/(1+z)]$.

The two routes share nothing but $E(z)$, so their agreement is a real
check. The companion script finds agreement to better than $10^{-10}$
after Richardson extrapolation.

\subsection{An illustrative geometry}

Throughout this chapter we use the illustrative configuration
\[
  z_d=0.5,\qquad z_1=1.0,\qquad z_2=2.0,\qquad
  \bm{p}_0=(\Omegam,w_0,w_a)=(0.3,-1,0),
\]
which is not meant to represent any particular observed system. There
$\Brat=0.674156$ and
\begin{equation}
  J=(S_{\Omegam},\,S_{w_0},\,S_{w_a})=(-0.03569,\;-0.03301,\;-0.007255),
  \qquad 1/S_{w_0}=-30.29 .
  \label{eq:cs_J_values}
\end{equation}
All three entries are negative: raising $\Omegam$, $w_0$ or $w_a$ lowers
$\Brat$. The scalar formula~\eqref{eq:cs_scalar} would therefore convert
a $1\%$ ratio error into $|\Delta w_0|=0.30$ at fixed $\Omegam$ and
$w_a$. The rest of the chapter is about what that number does and does
not mean.

\begin{annotation}[Why $S_{w_a}$ is small]
$w_a$ enters $w(z)$ multiplied by $z/(1+z)$, which is at most $1/2$
for $z\le1$ and at most $2/3$ for $z\le2$. Over the redshifts that
dominate $\chi_d$ and $\chi_1$, a unit change in $w_a$ therefore acts
like a much smaller change in $w_0$. At the illustrative geometry
$S_{w_a}/S_{w_0}=0.22$. The practical consequence is that a single DSPL
barely constrains $w_a$, and any statement about $w_a$ rests on the
other parameters being pinned.
\end{annotation}

\section{From a Model Error to a Parameter Displacement}
\label{sec:cs_biasvector}

Suppose the mass model biases the inferred log-ratios by $\delta_i$.
To first order the inferred parameters move by whatever $\Delta\bm{p}$
reproduces those offsets:
\begin{equation}
  J\,\Delta\bm{p}=\bm{\delta} .
  \label{eq:cs_linear}
\end{equation}
If the analysis carries a covariance $C$ on the $\ln\Brat_i$, the fit
minimises $\chi^2=(J\Delta\bm{p}-\bm{\delta})^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}
(J\Delta\bm{p}-\bm{\delta})$. Setting its gradient to zero gives the
generalised least-squares solution, and hence the induced bias
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Delta\bm{p}=\bigl(J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J\bigr)^{-1}
  J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\,\bm{\delta}\;}
  \label{eq:cs_biasvector}
\end{equation}
The matrix $F=J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J$ is the Fisher matrix of the
measurement, and $F^{-1}$ is the covariance of the \emph{statistical}
errors. Equation~\eqref{eq:cs_biasvector} says that the same $F^{-1}$
also converts a \emph{systematic} offset into a parameter shift. When
$J$ is square and invertible, eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_biasvector} reduces to
$\Delta\bm{p}=J^{-1}\bm{\delta}$ and $C$ drops out.

\begin{derivation}[The normal equations]
Write $\bm{r}=J\Delta\bm{p}-\bm{\delta}$. Then
$\chi^2=\bm{r}^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\bm{r}$ and
$\partial\chi^2/\partial\Delta\bm{p}=2J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\bm{r}$ (using
that $C^{-1}$ is symmetric). Setting this to zero gives
$J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J\,\Delta\bm{p}=J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\bm{\delta}$.
If $F=J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J$ is invertible, multiplying by $F^{-1}$ gives
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_biasvector}. If $J$ is square and invertible,
$F^{-1}=J^{-1}C\,(J^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$ and the $C$'s cancel, leaving
$J^{-1}\bm{\delta}$. Both statements are checked symbolically in the
companion script.
\end{derivation}

Two consequences deserve to be stated explicitly.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Direction matters as much as magnitude.} $\Delta\bm{p}$
  inherits the orientation of the poorly constrained eigendirections of
  $F$. A systematic that projects onto a well-constrained direction is
  comparatively harmless; the same magnitude along a degenerate direction
  is not.
  \item \textbf{Rank governs everything.} If $J$ has fewer rows than
  there are parameters (one DSPL, three parameters), $F$ is singular and
  eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_biasvector} has no unique solution. The bias is then a
  \emph{ray}, not a point.
\end{enumerate}

\section{One Source-Plane Pair: The Answer Is a Direction}
\label{sec:cs_direction}

\subsection{Rank and the blind direction}

With one DSPL, $J$ is a single row and $F=\sigma^{-2}J^{\mathsf T}J$ is
the outer product of one vector with itself. Its rank is $1$ whatever
$\sigma$ is. In the full $(\Omegam,w_0,w_a)$ space the null space is
two-dimensional, and in the $(\Omegam,w_0)$ plane it is the single
direction
\begin{equation}
  \bm{n}\propto(S_{w_0},\,-S_{\Omegam}),
  \qquad J\cdot\bm{n}=0 .
  \label{eq:cs_null}
\end{equation}
Moving the cosmology along $\bm{n}$ does not change $\Brat$ to first
order: $\bm{n}$ is the tangent to the contour $\Brat=\Brat_0$ in
Figure~\ref{fig:cs_B_contours}. Parametrising directions in the
$(\Omegam,w_0)$ plane as $\dd\Omegam=c\,\dd w_0$, the null direction
corresponds to
\begin{equation}
  c_\star=-\frac{S_{w_0}}{S_{\Omegam}} ,
  \label{eq:cs_cstar}
\end{equation}
which is $c_\star=-0.925$ at the illustrative geometry. We call
$(c_\star,1)$ the \textbf{blind direction} of the system. The companion
script confirms numerically that a step along it changes $\ln\Brat$ only
at second order: for a step of $0.01$ the response is about $4\%$ of the
response to the same step along $w_0$, and it halves when the step is
halved.

\subsection{The bias ray}

With one equation $J\cdot\Delta\bm{p}=\delta$ and two or three unknowns,
any direction $\hat{\bm{u}}$ with $J\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}\neq0$ provides a
solution:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\bm{p}=\frac{\delta}{J\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}}\,\hat{\bm{u}} .
  \label{eq:cs_ray}
\end{equation}
Choosing $\hat{\bm{u}}$ \emph{is} a modelling assumption. Taking
$\hat{\bm{u}}=\hat{\bm{e}}_{w_0}$, which amounts to declaring $\Omegam$
(and $w_a$) known, recovers eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_scalar} exactly:
$\Delta w_0=\delta/S_{w_0}$.

What decides $\hat{\bm{u}}$ in a real analysis? Usually another probe.
Suppose an external data set (supernovae, baryon acoustic oscillations,
the CMB) constrains the combination $\Omegam-c\,w_0$ and is itself blind
along $\dd\Omegam=c\,\dd w_0$. Minimising the joint $\chi^2$ of the DSPL
and the external probe, both centred on the truth, gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\Delta w_0=\frac{\delta}{S_{w_0}+c\,S_{\Omegam}},\qquad
  \Delta\Omegam=c\,\Delta w_0\;}
  \label{eq:cs_marginalised}
\end{equation}
\emph{independent of how precise the external probe is}. The case $c=0$,
an $\Omegam$ prior centred on the truth, gives back $\delta/S_{w_0}$
and $\Delta\Omegam=0$.

\begin{derivation}[Why the external precision drops out]
Let the DSPL contribute $(J\cdot\Delta\bm{p}-\delta)^2/\sigma_B^2$ and the
external probe $(\bm{e}\cdot\Delta\bm{p})^2/\sigma_X^2$ with
$\bm{e}=(1,-c)$, so that the probe is blind along $(c,1)$. There are two
parameters $(\Delta\Omegam,\Delta w_0)$ and two terms. The pair of
equations $J\cdot\Delta\bm{p}=\delta$, $\bm{e}\cdot\Delta\bm{p}=0$ has
the unique solution $\Delta\bm{p}\propto(c,1)$ with
$(cS_{\Omegam}+S_{w_0})\Delta w_0=\delta$. At that point both terms
vanish, so $\chi^2=0$, its smallest possible value, whatever $\sigma_B$
and $\sigma_X$ are. The companion script verifies the result by
inverting the $2\times2$ Fisher matrix symbolically.
\end{derivation}

\subsection{Is the fixed-$\Omegam$ number a bound?}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cs_marginalised} is a function of $c$ with a pole at
$c=c_\star$, where the external probe and the DSPL are blind along the
same line. Relative to the fixed-$\Omegam$ value it is larger by the
\textbf{amplification factor}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}(c)=\left|\frac{S_{w_0}}{S_{w_0}+c\,S_{\Omegam}}\right| .
  \label{eq:cs_amplification}
\end{equation}
At the illustrative geometry $\mathcal{A}(-\tfrac12)=2.18$ and
$\mathcal{A}(+\tfrac12)=0.649$. For a $1\%$ ratio error this means
$|\Delta w_0|=0.659$ at $c=-\tfrac12$, $0.303$ at $c=0$ and $0.197$ at
$c=+\tfrac12$. Since $\mathcal{A}\geq1$ exactly when
$|S_{w_0}+cS_{\Omegam}|\leq|S_{w_0}|$, the fixed-$\Omegam$ number is a
lower bound on the bias only for
\begin{equation}
  c\in[\,2c_\star,\;0\,]\qquad(\,=[-1.850,\,0]\text{ here}),
  \label{eq:cs_lb_interval}
\end{equation}
that is, only when the external degeneracy tilts toward the DSPL's own
blind direction. Outside that interval, marginalising \emph{reduces} the
bias. As $c\to c_\star$ the bias grows without limit: at
$c=0.99\,c_\star$ it is already more than fifty times the
fixed-$\Omegam$ value.

\begin{keybox}[What the fixed-$\Omegam$ number is, and is not]
$\Delta w_0=\delta/S_{w_0}$ is the bias projected onto $w_0$ under a
perfect $\Omegam$ prior. It is \textbf{not} a bound on the bias of a
marginalised analysis in either direction: it is the single point $c=0$
on a curve with a pole at the blind direction $c_\star$. It is a lower
bound only when the other probes' degeneracy lies in
$c\in[2c_\star,0]$. Report the bias \emph{vector}
(eq.~\ref{eq:cs_biasvector}) together with the blind direction, and
label any single-parameter number with the assumption that produced it.
\end{keybox}

There \emph{is} a genuine bound, but it concerns statistical errors, not
biases. For any Fisher matrix the variance of $w_0$ with $\Omegam$
fixed, $1/F_{w_0w_0}$, never exceeds the marginalised variance
$(F^{-1})_{w_0w_0}$. This is a property of every positive-definite
matrix, not a statement about estimators. (The Cram\'er--Rao bound is
the separate result that an unbiased estimator of $w_0$ has variance at
least $(F^{-1})_{w_0w_0}$.) With an $\Omegam$ prior of width $\sigma_X$
the difference of the two variances is exactly
$\sigma_X^2S_{\Omegam}^2/S_{w_0}^2\geq0$. It is a common slip to carry
the intuition that ``fixing parameters always underestimates'' from
errors over to biases, where it does not hold.

\begin{annotation}[Reading the formula against the literature]
Collett et al.\ \citep{collett_2012} report that the $\Omegam$--$w$
degeneracy of DSPLs is almost orthogonal to that of the CMB and baryon
acoustic oscillations. In the language of this section, combining with
those probes puts the external $c$ far from $c_\star$, which keeps
$\mathcal{A}(c)$ finite. Read literally, exact orthogonality of the two
blind directions, $(c_\star,1)\cdot(c,1)=0$, gives $c=-1/c_\star=+1.081$
and $\mathcal{A}=0.46$ at the illustrative geometry, a bias smaller than
the fixed-$\Omegam$ number. Two qualifications apply: ``orthogonal''
depends on how the $\Omegam$ and $w$ axes are scaled, and the statement
of Collett et al.\ refers to their geometries, not to this one. That is
the good news. The caution is that
$\mathcal{A}$ depends on $c$ as well as on the lens, so the
cosmological cost of a given $\delta$ cannot be quoted for the lens
alone. It belongs to the \emph{combination} of probes, and must be
recomputed for the combination actually used.
\end{annotation}

\subsection{Linear is only linear}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cs_marginalised} is first order. Near the blind
direction the first-order response $S_{w_0}+cS_{\Omegam}$ is small, so
the second-order terms that bend the contours of
Figure~\ref{fig:cs_B_contours} take over sooner.
Figure~\ref{fig:cs_degeneracy_bias} makes this concrete for
$\delta=+0.5\%$. Along $c=0$ and $c=+\tfrac12$ the exact solutions of
$\Brat(\bm{p}_0+t\,(c,1))=1.005\,\Brat_0$ are $\Delta w_0=-0.162$ and
$-0.091$, close to the linear values $-0.151$ and $-0.098$. Along
$c=-\tfrac12$ the linear formula predicts $\Delta w_0=-0.330$. In fact
$\Brat$ rises by at most $0.09\%$ along that ray before falling again,
so \emph{no} point on the ray absorbs a $0.5\%$ error. There the linear
formula returns a point that is not a solution of
$\Brat=1.005\,\Brat_0$.

\begin{bridge}[Back to the mass-sheet transformation]
The structure here (one observable, a flat direction, and an answer
fixed only once an external constraint chooses a point on it) is the same
as that of the mass-sheet degeneracy of Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing}
(\S\ref{sec:msd}). There, image positions fix the lens only up to the
family $\kappab_\lambda=\lambda\kappab+(1-\lambda)$, and $\Hubble$ is
pinned only by external information such as stellar kinematics. A
multi-plane version of that transformation exists, and Schneider
\citep{schneider_2014_multiplane} showed that for a two-source-plane
system in which the nearer source also acts as a deflector it can mimic a
change of cosmological parameters. (The model $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$
of this chapter neglects that deflection.) The flat
direction in cosmology studied here and the flat direction in the mass
model therefore have to be broken together.
\end{bridge}

\subsection{Raising the rank}

The rank can only be raised by an observable whose row of $J$ is
\emph{not} parallel to the DSPL row. There are three options:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \textbf{time delay} in the same system, which measures the
  time-delay distance $D_{\Delta t}=(1+z_d)\Dd\Ds/\Dds$
  (eq.~\ref{eq:time_delay_recap}). This is a different combination of the
  same distances, and it also depends on $\Hubble$;
  \item an \textbf{external probe} in the same likelihood, as above;
  \item a \textbf{second lens} at a different $z_d$, or a third source
  plane, each of which adds a row (but see \S\ref{sec:cs_coherence} for
  how much it helps).
\end{itemize}
More images of the \emph{same} source planes do not raise the rank: they
sharpen $\sigma$ but leave $J$ unchanged.

One further caution applies to every option. $\Brat$ is never observed
directly; it is inferred through a mass model, and a multi-plane
generalisation of the mass-sheet transformation can trade a change in
the mass model for a change in the inferred cosmology
\citep{schneider_2014_multiplane}. Raising the rank of $J$ does not by
itself break that second degeneracy.

\section{Many Planes: Coherence, Not Averaging}
\label{sec:cs_coherence}

A compound lens with $N>2$ source planes yields $N-1$ ratios. It is
tempting to treat them as $N-1$ independent measurements whose errors
average down. Two facts make that wrong, and both push the same way.

\subsection{The rows of $J$ are nearly parallel}

All $\Brat_i$ share the deflector redshift and the distance--redshift
relation; their responses to $\Omegam$ and $w_0$ differ only through the
lever arm in $z_i$. Add a third source at $z_3=3$ to the illustrative
system. The ratio of plane~1 to plane~3 has $\Brat_3=0.6095$ and row
$(-0.0600,\,-0.0456,\,-0.0114)$, with the same sign pattern as the first
row. In the $(\Omegam,w_0)$ plane the two rows are only $5.5^\circ$
apart, and the $2\times2$ Jacobian has condition number $22.7$. Nearly
parallel rows make $F$ ill-conditioned. The extra plane does raise the
rank, but it constrains the old blind direction only weakly, and a
systematic that points along that direction is amplified by the large
eigenvalue of $F^{-1}$.

\subsection{The model error is one error}

A single deflector with an incorrectly modelled interior produces one
physical error, one wrong inner profile, which propagates into
\emph{every} source plane. Take the case of
Chapter~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias}: a power-law deflector plus a central
point mass, characterised on plane $i$ by the central-mass fraction
$\mBH_i=\thetaBH^{(i)\,2}/\thetaE^{(i)\,2}$. The per-plane fractions
obey
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\mBH_i=\mBH_1\,\Brat_i^{\,(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}\;}
  \label{eq:cs_mscaling}
\end{equation}
for every $i$, where $\Brat_i$ is the ratio of plane~1 to plane~$i$.

\begin{derivation}[The $N$-plane coherence identity]
Let $\varepsilon_i\equiv D_{\mathrm{d}s_i}/D_{s_i}$ be the lensing
efficiency of plane $i$, so that $\Brat_i=\varepsilon_1/\varepsilon_i$
(eq.~\ref{eq:cs_B_def}). Both pieces of the lens scale with the inverse
critical density, which is proportional to $\varepsilon_i$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item point mass: $\thetaBH^{(i)\,2}=M\varepsilon_i$
  (cf.\ eq.~\ref{eq:einstein_radius_preview}, with
  $\Dds/(\Dd\Ds)=\varepsilon_i/\Dd$, and the constants absorbed in $M$);
  \item power law with convergence $\kappab\propto\theta^{1-\gp}$: the
  Einstein radius obeys $\thetaE^{(i)\,\gp-1}=A\varepsilon_i$, because
  $\kappab$ scales with $\varepsilon_i$ and the mean convergence inside
  $\thetaE$ must equal one.
\end{itemize}
Then $\mBH_i=M\varepsilon_i\,(A\varepsilon_i)^{-2/(\gp-1)}$ and
\[
  \frac{\mBH_i}{\mBH_1}
  =\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon_i}{\varepsilon_1}\Bigr)^{1-2/(\gp-1)}
  =\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon_1}{\varepsilon_i}\Bigr)^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}
  =\Brat_i^{\,(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)} .
\]
For the singular isothermal sphere, $\gp=2$, the exponent is $1$ and
$\mBH_i=\mBH_1\Brat_i$.
\end{derivation}

For $1<\gp<3$ the exponent is positive, and $0<\Brat_i<1$, so
$0<\mBH_i<\mBH_1$: farther planes feel a weaker but \emph{same-signed}
perturbation. The $\delta_i$ are therefore not independent draws; they
form a deterministic family driven by one amplitude. Write
$\delta_i=\mBH_1 r_i$ with response vector
$r_i\equiv\partial\ln\Brat_i/\partial\mBH_1$. The $\mBH_i$ share a
sign, but the $\delta_i$ share a sign only if the $r_i$ do.
Chapter~\ref{ch:perturbative_estimators} derives $r_i$ and shows that
the responses share a sign in the baseline where the images on every
plane respond equally strongly to the central mass; a plane whose image
lies close enough to the centre can reverse the sign of its $r_i$. Then
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_biasvector} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\bm{p}=\mBH_1\bigl(J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J\bigr)^{-1}
  J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\bm{r} .
  \label{eq:cs_coherentbias}
\end{equation}
Whether the planes reinforce or partially cancel is decided by how
$\bm{r}$ lines up with the poorly constrained eigendirections of $F$,
which can be computed from the geometry.

\begin{bridge}[Back to the power-law lens]
The scaling $\thetaE^{\gp-1}\propto\varepsilon$ used in the derivation is
the same one that gives $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$ in
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_chain}. It is the power-law profile of
Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxy_lensing} (\S\ref{sec:power_law_profile}) seen from
two source planes: the profile shape is fixed, and only its amplitude
scales with the lensing efficiency.
\end{bridge}

\subsection{Why coherent errors do not average as $1/\sqrt{n}$}

The cleanest way to see the difference is a one-parameter fit (say $w_0$
with everything else fixed) to $n$ ratios with equal variances. The
estimator is $\Delta w_0=\sum_iJ_i\delta_i/\sum_iJ_i^2$. Define the
\emph{per-plane bias} $b_i\equiv\delta_i/J_i$, the shift each ratio
would produce on its own. Then
\begin{equation}
  \Delta w_0=\sum_i\omega_i\,b_i,\qquad
  \omega_i=\frac{J_i^2}{\sum_kJ_k^2}>0,\qquad\sum_i\omega_i=1 .
  \label{eq:cs_convex}
\end{equation}
The combined bias is a weighted \emph{mean} of the per-plane biases, so
it lies between the smallest and the largest of them, for every $n$.
When the $b_i$ share a sign, as they do for a coherent error whenever
the $r_i$ and the $J_i$ share a sign, adding planes cannot push the bias
toward zero.

Compare independent errors of size $\delta$ on each ratio. The rms bias
is then $\delta/\lVert J\rVert$. With equal $J_i=J$ this is
$\delta/(J\sqrt n)$, while a coherent error $\delta_i=\delta$ gives
$\delta/J$ for every $n$. For unequal same-sign $J_i$ the ratio of
coherent to independent is $\sum_iJ_i/\lVert J\rVert$, which lies
between $1$ and $\sqrt n$. Coherent errors never benefit from the
$1/\sqrt n$.

The weighted-mean property belongs to the one-parameter fit. With
$\Omegam$ free as well, the planes at $z=2$ and $z=3$ of the illustrative
system give a square $2\times2$ Jacobian in $(\Omegam,w_0)$, and the bias
is $J^{-1}\bm{\delta}$ (the covariance drops out). For an illustrative
$\delta_i=1\%$ on both ratios this gives $\Delta w_0=-0.687$, more than
twice the per-plane fixed-$\Omegam$ biases $-0.303$ and $-0.219$. The
nearly parallel rows ($5.5^\circ$ apart) that make the system
ill-conditioned also amplify the coherent bias.

\begin{keybox}[Coherent systematics]
In an $N$-plane compound lens a single inner-profile error enters all
$N-1$ ratios through $\mBH_i=\mBH_1\Brat_i^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}$, with one
sign. In a one-parameter fit the induced bias is a weighted mean of the
per-plane biases (eq.~\ref{eq:cs_convex}), so it does not shrink as
$1/\sqrt{N}$. With several free parameters it can be larger than any
single-plane bias, because nearly parallel rows of $J$ amplify it. A
many-plane system is not automatically safer than a
two-plane one: it is more precise, and therefore \emph{more} exposed to a
systematic that does not average away.
\end{keybox}

\begin{annotation}[The same logic across lenses]
The argument does not need a single deflector. If several DSPLs are
analysed with the \emph{same} modelling convention (the same rigid
profile family, the same prior on $\gp$), a shortcoming of that
convention is a shared systematic. Stacking such lenses averages the
noise down as $1/\sqrt N$ but leaves the shared bias untouched, so the
stack yields an increasingly confident wrong answer. Whether the errors
are independent depends on how the lenses were modelled.
\end{annotation}

\section{Degeneracy Versus Bias}
\label{sec:cs_degeneracy}

\subsection{Three things an analyst can do with the radial image}

Chapters~\ref{ch:odd_images} and~\ref{ch:central_mass_bias} discuss how
a radial (central) image constrains the inner profile of the deflector.
For cosmography the useful classification is by what the analyst does
with that image, rather than by which mass profile is true:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Masked.} The radial image is removed from the data
  vector.
  \item \textbf{Included, rigid inner profile.} It is fitted with a
  single power law (no central mass, no break in slope).
  \item \textbf{Included, flexible inner profile.} It is fitted with a
  model that lets the inner slope differ from the outer one.
\end{enumerate}
Cases 2 and 3 produce \emph{biases}: a definite offset $\delta$ that the
linear machinery of
\S\S\ref{sec:cs_biasvector}--\ref{sec:cs_coherence} can propagate (with
the caveat of Figure~\ref{fig:cs_degeneracy_bias}). Case 3 is expected
to have the smaller bias, because the extra freedom can absorb the inner
structure that case 2 forces into the slope. Case 1 is different in
kind.

\subsection{Masking surrenders a constraint}

With the radial image removed, the data no longer determine the inner
slope, and $\Brat=\etamea^{\gp-1}$ inherits that indeterminacy: a range
of $(\gp,\Brat)$ pairs fits the remaining images equally well. Within
that range $\Brat$ is not shifted by the data; it is not determined by
them at all. The value an analyst reports is set by where their prior on
$\gp$ sits. Calling the width of that range a ``bias'' and pushing it
through eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_scalar} confuses two different objects.

That is the exact, noise-free limit. With noise, ellipticity and a
finite prior, the remaining images usually still exclude part of the
range, so the slope becomes prior-dominated rather than completely
undetermined. In a quad the surviving images can keep $\gp$ constrained
but biased, which turns the masked case back into a bias of the kind in
case~2.

\begin{keybox}[A degeneracy is not a bias]
A \textbf{bias} is a displacement: the data determine a value, and the
value is wrong by $\delta$. A \textbf{degeneracy} is a surrendered
constraint: the data determine a set, or nearly so, and the reported point within it is
chosen by a prior. Masking a feature that carried information creates a
degeneracy. Report it as the span of $\Brat$ allowed by the data, name
the prior that selects within it, and propagate it non-linearly as a
\emph{region} in parameter space. Never convert it into a linearised
$\Delta w$.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The model manifold is thin}

There is a second, blunter reason not to linearise large offsets: no
cosmology can absorb them. Figure~\ref{fig:cs_B_contours} shows how
little $\Brat$ moves. At the illustrative geometry, with $w_a=0$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item along $\Omegam=0.3$, $\Brat$ is \emph{not monotonic} in $w_0$.
  It peaks $1.81\%$ above $\Brat_0$ near $w_0=-2.2$ and falls on both
  sides;
  \item over the box $\Omegam\in[0.2,0.4]$, $w_0\in[-2.5,-0.5]$ the
  fractional change stays within $[-1.7\%,+3.3\%]$ (grid evaluation).
\end{itemize}
A fractional error of, say, $25\%$ is therefore not a ``large
$\Delta w$''. It lies far off the manifold of predictions, and no member
of the model family reproduces it. A naive linearisation would return
$|0.25/S_{w_0}|=7.6$, a number with no meaning: it extrapolates a local
derivative across an excursion over which $\Brat$ is not even monotonic.
The correct statement is that the inferred $\Brat$ is inconsistent with
the stated model family over the stated box. Freeing $w_a$ keeps flat CPL
far from $25\%$: over $\Omegam\in[0.20,0.42]$, $w_0\in[-8,-0.1]$,
$w_a\in[-3,3]$ the change stays within $[-3.4\%,+8.9\%]$ (grid
evaluation). Both qualifiers still matter, because a more flexible $E(z)$
can reach larger excursions. A flat model with $w=-1$ below $z=0.5$ and
$w=+4$ above it, physically contrived but admissible as an $E(z)$, moves
$\Brat$ by $+30.5\%$ at the illustrative geometry. ``No cosmology fits''
is a statement about a named family and a named box.

\begin{annotation}[Dynamic range depends on geometry]
The numbers above belong to the illustrative geometry. The dynamic range
of $\Brat$ shrinks as $z_2\to z_1$ (where $\Brat\to1$ for every
cosmology), so re-evaluate the excursion for each configuration. The
procedure is simple and worth automating: scan $\Brat$ over the
parameter box actually allowed, compare $|\delta|$ with that range, and
decide whether linear propagation is admissible before quoting any
$\Delta w$.
\end{annotation}

\section{A Practical Recipe}
\label{sec:cs_recipe}

For a given compound-lens system and a candidate mass-model systematic:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write the cosmology as a callable $E(z;\bm{p})$
  (eq.~\ref{eq:cs_E}) and compute every $\Brat_i$ from
  eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_B_chi}.
  \item Compute $J$ by finite differences and check it against
  eqs.~\eqref{eq:cs_dchi}--\eqref{eq:cs_dlnB}. $J$ belongs to the
  geometry; do not transport it to another system.
  \item Find the blind direction(s): the null space of $J$, or the
  smallest-eigenvalue directions of $F$ when $N>2$.
  \item Decide whether the systematic is a \emph{bias} (case 2 or 3) or a
  \emph{degeneracy} (case 1). Only biases go further.
  \item Check that $|\delta|$ is well inside the dynamic range of
  $\Brat$, and that the probe combination being used is not close to the
  blind direction. If either check fails, solve
  $\Brat(\bm{p})=\Brat_{\rm obs}$ non-linearly.
  \item For coherent multi-plane errors, build $\bm{\delta}$ from the
  single amplitude via eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_mscaling} and propagate with
  eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_coherentbias}. Do not divide by $\sqrt{N}$.
  \item Report $\Delta\bm{p}$ as a vector with the degeneracy direction
  shown, and give any single-parameter number with its projection
  labelled.
\end{enumerate}

DSPL cosmography has gone from a forecast \citep{collett_2012} to a first
measurement on an observed system \citep{collett_auger_2014}. Every step
from $\delta$ to $\Delta\bm{p}$ carries an assumption about directions in
parameter space, and those assumptions need to be stated alongside the
result.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:cs_summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item With CPL dark energy, $w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)$, the density factor is
  $\zeta(z)=(1+z)^{3(1+w_0+w_a)}e^{-3w_az/(1+z)}$ and
  $E^2=\Omegam(1+z)^3+(1-\Omegam)\zeta$.
  \item In a flat universe
  $\Brat=(1-\chi_d/\chi_1)/(1-\chi_d/\chi_2)$, a ratio of comoving
  distances, independent of $\Hubble$.
  \item The sensitivity Jacobian $J_{ij}=\partial\ln\Brat_i/\partial p_j$
  can be computed by finite differences or by differentiating under the
  integral. At the illustrative geometry all entries are negative and
  $S_{w_a}$ is several times smaller than $S_{w_0}$.
  \item A ratio error $\bm{\delta}$ maps to
  $\Delta\bm{p}=F^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\bm{\delta}$. With one DSPL $F$
  has rank $1$, so the bias is a ray, fixed only by a choice of
  direction.
  \item With an external probe blind along $(c,1)$,
  $\Delta w_0=\delta/(S_{w_0}+cS_{\Omegam})$, which diverges at the blind
  direction $c_\star=-S_{w_0}/S_{\Omegam}$. The fixed-$\Omegam$ value is a
  lower bound only for $c\in[2c_\star,0]$. For statistical errors, by
  contrast, fixing parameters always gives a lower bound.
  \item Near the blind direction the linear formula fails early, and can
  describe a solution that does not exist.
  \item In an $N$-plane lens one inner-profile error enters every ratio
  through $\mBH_i=\mBH_1\Brat_i^{(3-\gp)/(\gp-1)}$. In a one-parameter fit
  the resulting bias is a weighted mean of per-plane biases and does not
  average as $1/\sqrt{N}$; with several free parameters it can exceed
  every single-plane bias.
  \item Masking a constraining feature creates a degeneracy, not a bias:
  report a span and the prior, not a linearised $\Delta w$. Offsets larger
  than the dynamic range of $\Brat$ mean that no cosmology in the stated
  family and box fits.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problems}
\label{sec:cs_problems}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cs_cpl}
\textbf{(The CPL density.)}
(a) Starting from $\dd\ln\rho/\dd\ln a=-3[1+w(a)]$ with
$w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)$, derive eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_zeta}.
(b) Show that $\zeta(z)\to e^{-3w_a}(1+z)^{3(1+w_0+w_a)}$ as
$z\to\infty$, and interpret the exponent.
(c) Evaluate $\zeta(1)$ for $(w_0,w_a)=(-0.9,-0.3)$.
(d) Explain why $|S_{w_a}|$ is much smaller than $|S_{w_0}|$ for
galaxy-scale DSPLs.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cs_projection}
\textbf{(Projection.)}
Show that eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_ray} with $\hat{\bm{u}}=\hat{\bm{e}}_{w_0}$
reproduces $\Delta w_0=\delta/S_{w_0}$, and derive the expression when
$\Omegam$ moves along a fixed direction $\dd\Omegam=c\,\dd w_0$. At the
illustrative geometry, compare $|\Delta w_0|$ for $\delta=1\%$ at
$c=0,\pm\tfrac12$. For which $c$ is the fixed-$\Omegam$ value a lower
bound? Why is ``marginalising always makes it worse'' the wrong
intuition here?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cs_rank}
\textbf{(Rank.)}
For a single DSPL, show that $J^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}J$ has rank~$1$ and give
its null direction in the $(\Omegam,w_0)$ plane at the illustrative
geometry. Name an observable that raises the rank \emph{without} adding a
source plane, and check that its row is not parallel to the DSPL row.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cs_coherence}
\textbf{(Coherence.)}
Using eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_mscaling} with $\gp=1.6$ and $\Brat_2=0.7$,
compute $\mBH_2/\mBH_1$. If a third plane has $\Brat_3=0.5$ relative to
plane~1, do the three central-mass fractions have the same sign? Use
eq.~\eqref{eq:cs_convex} to argue whether the per-plane biases reinforce.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cs_nonlinear}
\textbf{(Non-linearity.)}
An analysis that masked the radial image reports that $\Brat$ could lie
anywhere in a $25\%$ span. Explain why quoting
$|\Delta w_0|=0.25/|S_{w_0}|$ is not meaningful, using both the
degeneracy argument and the dynamic range of $\Brat$, and state what
should be reported instead.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cs_design}
\textbf{(Design.)}
Would you rather have one DSPL with an exquisitely modelled radial
image, or three DSPLs whose radial images are all masked and which are
analysed with the same rigid profile and the same $\gp$ prior? Justify
your answer with the rank and coherence arguments of this chapter, not
with intuition about $\sqrt N$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:cs_newgeom}
\textbf{(A second geometry.)}
Compute $\Brat$, the Jacobian $(S_{\Omegam},S_{w_0},S_{w_a})$ and
$c_\star$ for $z_d=0.3$, $z_1=0.8$, $z_2=3.0$ at
$(\Omegam,w_0,w_a)=(0.3,-1,0)$, and compare with the illustrative
geometry. What does the comparison tell you about transporting $S_w$
from one system to another?
\end{exercise}
